# Source Ledger: Place

## Pauline Oliveros, Stuart Dempster, Panaiotis, and `Deep Listening`

- Claim: In 1988 the trio recorded in a large underground cistern at Fort
  Worden, Washington, roughly fourteen feet below ground, with a reverberation
  time of about forty-five seconds. The title `Deep Listening` began as a pun
  on physical depth and developed into Oliveros's broader practice.
- Sources: Pauline Oliveros, first-person “The Music of the Environmental
  Dialogue”; Rensselaer history of Deep Listening; IRCAM work chronology.
  https://www.econtact.ca/17_3/oliveros_music.html
  https://news.rpi.edu/luwakkey/3027
  https://ressources.ircam.fr/en/composer/pauline-oliveros/workcourse
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Caveat: Decay figures are approximate and position-dependent. Do not reduce
  Deep Listening as a practice to the recording session.

## John Luther Adams, `Inuksuit`

- Claim: Eighteen percussionists premiered the work at Banff on the summer
  solstice, June 21, 2009. The score allows nine to ninety-nine performers,
  dispersed through a site, and listeners may move. Adams calls it
  site-determined rather than site-specific.
- Sources: Adams and Steven Schick first-person premiere interview; later Adams
  interview on site-determined performance; Wise Music overview.
  https://www.johnlutheradams.net/interviews/7
  https://www.johnlutheradams.net/interviews/8
  https://www.wisemusicclassical.com/features/2020/06/john-luther-adams-music-without-walls/
- Confidence: HIGH.

- Claim: The title refers to Inuit-built stone landmarks; Adams associates the
  word with acting in the capacity of the human and with human vulnerability.
- Source: Adams's premiere interview.
- Confidence: HIGH for Adams's explanation of his title.
- Cultural caveat: Adams's interpretation does not define the full history,
  forms, functions, or cultural authority of inuksuit. Do not use them as
  generic Arctic decoration or imply that the work contains Inuit musical
  practice.

## Morton Feldman, `Rothko Chapel`

- Claim: John and Dominique de Menil commissioned the work after the chapel's
  1971 opening. Feldman wrote it for voices, viola, percussion, and celesta; it
  premiered in the chapel on April 9, 1972.
- Sources: IRCAM work record and reproduction of Feldman's programme note;
  Rothko Chapel event histories.
  https://ressources.ircam.fr/en/work/the-rothko-chapel
  https://rothkochapel.org/index.php/experience/events/event/morton-feldmans-rothko-chapel
- Confidence: HIGH.

- Claim: Feldman said the room and paintings substantially determined the
  forces, balance, and timbre. He wanted the music to fill the octagonal room
  at close range. The ending viola melody came from music written when he was
  fifteen; the soprano line was written on the day of Stravinsky's funeral;
  some intervals recalled synagogue sound to him.
- Source: Feldman's programme note reproduced by IRCAM.
  https://www.ircam.fr/media/uploads/creation/etincelle/etincelle_17_-_manifeste_2017.pdf
- Confidence: HIGH as the composer's retrospective account.

## Chapter-specific interpretations

- Place is a bundle of acoustic, social, historical, and environmental
  relations, not a backdrop.
- A site-determined score preserves a robust trajectory while allowing site and
  listener path to determine particulars.
- Surrounding sound becomes compositionally consequential only when the work
  establishes how it can interrupt, cue, mask, answer, or alter identity.
- Publicness changes musical responsibility because not every listener chose
  the event or can leave it equally.
