# Source Ledger: Limit

This ledger separates established events, participant recollections, musical
analysis, and the section's interpretations. It is a drafting aid, not the
book's final citation format.

## Olivier Messiaen, `Quartet for the End of Time`

### Composition, ensemble, and premiere

- Claim: Messiaen composed much of the quartet while a prisoner of war at
  Stalag VIII-A. The musicians available to him were clarinetist Henri Akoka,
  violinist Jean Le Boulaire, cellist Étienne Pasquier, and Messiaen at the
  piano. They premiered the work there on January 15, 1941.
- Sources: Olivier Messiaen official work list; Rebecca Rischin, `For the End
  of Time`; Chicago Symphony Orchestra history.
  https://www.oliviermessiaen.org/list-of-works
  https://books.google.com/books/about/For_the_End_of_Time.html?id=5zBeGrMJJf4C
  https://cso.org/experience/article/3594/pandemic-heightens-the-message-of-quartet-for
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Caveat: The instrumentation was unusual but not unprecedented. Say that the
  available musicians concretely shaped it, not that no one had used the
  combination before.

### Audience and damaged-instrument legends

- Claim: Archival and interview research does not support the frequently
  repeated audience of 5,000 or three-string cello. The audience consisted of
  several hundred prisoners and guards; the instruments were inferior but
  usable.
- Sources: Rischin, based on camp documents and interviews with performers and
  witnesses; Chicago Symphony Orchestra summary; University of Hong Kong event
  involving Rischin and explicitly raising the fictionalized-instrument story.
  https://books.google.com/books/about/For_the_End_of_Time.html?id=5zBeGrMJJf4C
  https://cso.org/experience/article/3594/pandemic-heightens-the-message-of-quartet-for
  https://www.hku.hk/press/news_detail_23229.html
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: Make the correction part of the argument against romanticizing
  deprivation. Do not imply that the actual camp conditions were mild.

### Section interpretation

- The prison camp and available musicians selected conditions; they did not
  explain the work's theological idea, rhythmic language, scale, or formal
  imagination.
- Treat this explicitly as the author's inference.

## Arvo Pärt, `Für Alina`

### Date, search, and first tintinnabuli work

- Claim: According to Nora Pärt, Pärt wrote the short piano work on February 7,
  1976 after roughly eight years of creative search. It is the first completed
  work in his tintinnabuli style and was named for Irena Veisaitė's daughter
  Alina, from whom she had been separated.
- Sources: Arvo Pärt Centre anniversary account and official work page.
  https://www.arvopart.ee/en/arvo-parts-tintinnabuli-style-at-50/
  https://www.arvopart.ee/en/arvo-part/work/477/
- Confidence: HIGH, with the exact day attributed to Nora Pärt.
- Caveat: Do not call the preceding years complete silence. The Centre
  describes a prolonged creative crisis and search.

### Rules and deliberate violations

- Claim: The melodic voice grows by one note per bar to eight notes and then
  contracts. A second voice follows notes of the nearest B-minor triad. Pärt
  breaks both systems: the triadic voice reaches C-sharp as the long pedal is
  interrupted, and the final melodic phrase continues beyond its expected
  single note.
- Source: Arvo Pärt Centre, official `Für Alina` analysis.
  https://www.arvopart.ee/en/arvo-part/work/477/
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: Describe the audible consequence of the exceptions without turning the
  section into a complete harmonic analysis of tintinnabuli.

### Early performance history

- Claim: The work was first heard as an unannounced encore by pianist Rein
  Rannap on March 23, 1976; later October performances were long misidentified
  as the premiere.
- Source: Arvo Pärt Centre, official work page and archival update.
  https://www.arvopart.ee/en/arvo-part/work/477/
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: Probably omit from prose unless the distinction serves the argument.

## Tom Johnson, `The Four Note Opera`

### Materials, form, and tone

- Claim: The 1972 chamber opera lasts about an hour, uses five singers and
  piano, and is built from four pitches: A, B, D, and E. It is a self-aware
  farce whose characters know they are in an opera; it ends as the singers
  become immobile and the music stops.
- Source: Wise Music Classical work page, including Johnson's programme note
  and the publisher's synopsis.
  https://www.wisemusicclassical.com/work/29299/The-Four-Note-Opera--Tom-Johnson/
- Corroboration: IRCAM work record.
  https://ressources.ircam.fr/work/l%27opera-de-quatre-notes
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: The ending is theatrically violent and comic; do not make a four-pitch
  opera sound like a serene austerity exercise.

### Premiere and performance flexibility

- Claim: Johnson says the opera premiered at the Cubiculo in New York in 1972,
  was soon taken up for television and European performance, and has supported
  widely differing casts and stagings.
- Source: Johnson's programme note on the Wise Music work page.
  https://www.wisemusicclassical.com/work/29299/The-Four-Note-Opera--Tom-Johnson/
- Confidence: HIGH as first-person retrospective account.
- Use: Support the claim that limiting pitch can enlarge the formal role of
  text, character, timing, staging, and performer personality.

## Section-specific interpretations

The following are the book's analytical proposals rather than claims made by
the cited composers:

- A productive limit changes the price of an event by making some remaining
  difference more consequential.
- Constraints may govern inventory, action, relation, duration, agency, or
  circumstance; these are listening distinctions, not an exhaustive taxonomy.
- A rule that cannot be heard or consequentially experienced may still aid the
  composer, but it does not carry the public form merely because it is elegant.
- Obedience is not compositional innocence. The composer remains responsible
  for the selected rule, its tolerances, and its results.
