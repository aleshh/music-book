## Erik Satie and `musique d'ameublement`

### The 1920 Galerie Barbazanges event

- Claim: During a March 1920 event at the Galerie Barbazanges in Paris,
  furniture music by Erik Satie and Darius Milhaud was played during the
  intermissions of Max Jacob's `Ruffian toujours, truand jamais`.
- Claim: Musicians were distributed around the room, and an announcement asked
  the audience to talk, move, and treat the music as though it did not exist.
  The audience instead became quiet and listened; Satie urged them to resume
  ordinary activity.
- Source: Erik Satie cultural site, “Musique d'ameublement,” lines 32–60.
  https://www.eriksatie.fr/musiques-d-ameublement
- Source: Sylwia Makomaska, “Erik Satie—the ‘progenitor’ of muzak or the
  precursor of ‘pipedown’ movement?”, `Interdisciplinary Studies in Musicology`
  19 (2019).
  https://doi.org/10.14746/ism.2019.19.9
- Confidence: HIGH for event, instructions, and audience response.
- Caution: Exact shouted phrases differ among retellings. Paraphrase Satie's
  intervention. The cultural-site account is useful but sometimes rhetorically
  broad; use the scholarly reconstruction for interpretation.

### The idea was small, specific, and ambiguous

- Claim: Satie's furniture music was conceived to accompany activity without
  dominating it. His surviving note to Jean Cocteau compares its function to
  light, heat, and comfort.
- Source: Makomaska, pp. 138–39, citing Satie's writings and Caroline Potter's
  archival biography.
  https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ism/article/download/21285/20548/44041
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: Paraphrase. Do not cast Satie as the sole inventor of ambient music or
  claim that all his repetitive, quiet, or popular pieces are furniture music.

## John Cage, `4'33"`

### Premiere and three-movement frame

- Claim: David Tudor premiered `4'33"` at Woodstock, New York, on August 29,
  1952. The work consisted of three movements; Tudor used the piano lid and a
  stopwatch to clarify their boundaries.
- Source: James Pritchett, “The Origin of `4'33"`,” lines 9–10.
  https://musicofjohncage.com/2018/09/03/the-origin-of-4-33/
- Source: Museum of Modern Art, “Composing Silence: John Cage and Black
  Mountain College.”
  https://www.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2014/01/03/composing-silence-john-cage-and-black-mountain-college-3/
- Confidence: HIGH.

### Weather and audience sound

- Claim: Accounts of the premiere remember wind, rain, and audience sound
  occupying the framed duration.
- Source: Research bank 01, anecdote 2, synthesizing MoMA, Getty Research
  Institute, and Pritchett.
- Confidence: HIGH for these as remembered features; exact ordering varies.
- Use: Say that accounts remember the weather rather than narrating a precise
  minute-by-minute sequence.

### Origin-story caution

- Claim: The Harvard anechoic-chamber visit and Rauschenberg's White Paintings
  mattered to Cage, but neither alone instantly caused `4'33"`; Cage had
  considered a silent piece years earlier, and Tudor's interest helped bring
  the work to completion.
- Source: Pritchett, lines 11–37.
  https://musicofjohncage.com/2018/09/03/the-origin-of-4-33/
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: The section does not need to retell the anechoic story.

## Brian Eno

### The harp-and-rain recollection

- Claim: While Eno was recovering after a January 1975 accident, Judy Nylon
  brought him a record of harp music. He later recalled that it played extremely
  quietly through one functioning channel while rain sounded outside; unable or
  unwilling to rise, he heard music and environment as one mixed field.
- Source: Ian MacDonald, “Before and After Science,” `New Musical Express`,
  November 26, 1977, interview lines 327–57.
  https://enoweb.co.uk/interviews/nme77a.html
- Corroboration and complication: Judy Nylon's later account says she
  deliberately balanced the harp against the rain.
  https://www.3ammagazine.com/litarchives/oct2001/interview_judy_nylon.html
- Confidence: HIGH for the convalescence, record, rain, and mixed field;
  DISPUTED concerning who set the level and whether the balance was accidental.
- Use: Name the disagreement briefly. Its existence strengthens the argument
  that the mixed relation, not a perfect accident myth, is what matters.

### Eno's attentional criterion

- Claim: In the September 1978 notes to `Ambient 1: Music for Airports`, Eno
  distinguished ambient music from derivative canned music and said it should
  accommodate several levels of listening attention without enforcing one.
- Source: Eno, original US liner notes, reproduced as a scan.
  https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5d4dcd89a70bc90001638861/t/5d9ca5a21e81bb3340e041d3/1570547107101/eno-ambient.pdf
- Bibliographic corroboration: National Library of New Zealand catalogue
  records composer program notes and the 1978 recording.
  https://natlib.govt.nz/records/33768075
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: The short phrase “as ignorable as it is interesting” may be quoted. Do
  not make it the book's universal definition of ambient music.

### Musical materials in `Music for Airports`

- Claim: The album contains four extended pieces using piano and wordless
  voices; its materials recur with soft boundaries and without a conventional
  dramatic succession.
- Source: National Library of New Zealand catalogue for forces and track
  lengths.
  https://natlib.govt.nz/records/33768075
- Source: published recording; analytical description must be checked by
  listening before final publication.
- Confidence: HIGH for forces and form at a general level; AUTHOR ANALYSIS for
  attentional consequences.
- Caution: Do not explain recording or looping technology. Describe only what
  the musical relations do.

## Pauline Oliveros

### Focal and field listening

- Claim: Oliveros's Deep Listening practice distinguishes involuntary hearing
  from conscious listening and asks practitioners both to open toward the whole
  field and to narrow attention toward matters of interest.
- Source: Center for Deep Listening at Rensselaer, “Deep Listening,” especially
  lines 32–49, including Oliveros's own 1999 statement.
  https://www.deeplistening.rpi.edu/deep-listening/
- Source: Oliveros, `Deep Listening: A Composer's Sound Practice` (2005),
  especially the discussion of focal and global attention.
  https://agosto-foundation.org/sites/default/files/upload/oliveros_pauline_deep_listening_a_composers_sound_practice_2005.pdf
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: Paraphrase rather than turning Oliveros's practice into a simplified
  cognitive diagram.

### `Sonic Meditations` and `Tuning Meditation`

- Claim: The first published `Sonic Meditations` (1971) emerged through months
  of work with a female-identified ensemble rather than through solitary theory.
- Source: Center for Deep Listening, lines 36–39.
  https://www.deeplistening.rpi.edu/deep-listening/
- Claim: `Tuning Meditation` alternates listening to the whole field, matching
  a distant voice, and contributing a tone no one else is singing.
- Source: Indexical, score description.
  https://www.indexical.org/pieces/tuning-meditation
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Caution: The section uses the score to show shifts of attentional width, not
  to give the full history of Deep Listening or ensemble agency.

## Author's theoretical synthesis

### Event, state, focal, peripheral, and distributed listening

- Status: AUTHOR'S FRAMEWORK, informed by Eno's levels of attention,
  Oliveros's focal/global practice, and the contrasting functions of Satie and
  Cage.
- Claim limit: These are compositional lenses, not exhaustive psychological
  categories and not claims that listeners behave identically.

### Relative salience

- Status: AUTHOR'S MUSICAL ANALYSIS.
- Claim: Onset, contrast, rarity, register, dynamics, rhythmic placement,
  harmonic implication, and density can attract attention, while continuity,
  blending, predictability, and redundancy can release it.
- Caution: Present these as variables a composer can test, not a universal
  ranking. Context and listener experience alter every result.
