# Source Ledger: Drift

## Steve Reich, `It's Gonna Rain`

- Claim: Reich recorded Brother Walter preaching in San Francisco's Union
  Square in 1964. While working with identical loops of “It's gonna rain” on
  two inexpensive recorders, small speed differences produced gradual phase
  separation. Reich used the discovery in the 1965 work.
- Sources: Reich's first-person Boosey & Hawkes video presented by Nonesuch;
  American Mavericks interview; `Writings on Music 1965–2000`.
  https://www.nonesuch.com/journal/watch-steve-reich-composing-its-gonna-rain-2023-08-17
  https://musicmavericks.publicradio.org/features/interview_reich.html
  https://academic.oup.com/book/27047
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Caveat: Keep 1964 recording and 1965 composition distinct. Brother Walter's
  identity and apocalyptic sermon remain part of the material; do not describe
  the voice as an abstract found rhythm.

## Steve Reich, `Piano Phase`

- Claim: Reich developed the work in 1966–67 after discovering that he could
  approximate live the phase process first heard with recorded loops. In the
  notated piece, two pianists begin together on the same pattern; one remains
  steady while the other accelerates gradually to the next alignment, and the
  procedure continues through the cycle.
- Source: Steve Reich official work page and composer note.
  https://stevereich.com/composition/piano-phase/
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: Reich explicitly distinguishes complete compositional determination
  from the performer's intense real-time listening. Do not call the piece
  improvised.

## Conlon Nancarrow, Study No. 21, `Canon X`

- Claim: In the two-part player-piano study, a very fast upper voice
  decelerates while a slow lower voice accelerates. Their speeds cross near the
  middle and continue past one another, producing the X of the title.
- Sources: IRCAM survey and work record; Music Theory Online study of
  Nancarrow's convergence points.
  https://resources.ircam.fr/en/composer/conlon-nancarrow/workcourse
  https://ressources.ircam.fr/en/work/study-no.-21
  https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.14.20.1/mto.14.20.1.nemire.pdf
- Confidence: HIGH for the opposing tempo curves and convergence. Avoid exact
  notes-per-second figures, which vary slightly among accounts and are not
  necessary for the argument.

## Steve Reich, `Music for 18 Musicians`

- Claim: The work combines a regular pulse in pianos and mallet instruments
  with voice and wind phrases measured by comfortable breath. Strings follow
  the bass clarinet's breathing contour. Its form is based on eleven chords,
  and performer cues govern sectional transitions.
- Source: Steve Reich official composer note; Nonesuch collected-works
  booklet.
  https://stevereich.com/composition/music-for-18-musicians/
  https://publicity.nonesuch.com/sites/g/files/g2000019346/files/2025-01/steve-reich-collected-works-booklet_1733880478967.pdf
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Caveat: “No conductor” does not mean leaderless. Different musicians carry
  cueing responsibility.

## Section-specific interpretations

- Phase is a particular kind of drift: periodic copies move through changing
  offsets and realignments.
- A resultant pattern is a listener's grouping of attacks distributed across
  parts, not a third literal performer.
- Drift requires an invariant or kinship against which changing temporal
  relation can be heard.
- Coincidences acquire form only when the piece establishes which kinds of
  meeting matter.
