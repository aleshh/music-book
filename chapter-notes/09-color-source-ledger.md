# Source Ledger: Color — Letting Sound Carry the Argument

This ledger separates established events, participant recollections, musical
analysis, and the section's interpretations. It is a drafting aid, not the
book's final citation format.

## Timbre, articulation, and blend

### Timbre is multidimensional and covaries with other parameters

- Claim: Instrumental timbre cannot be treated as independent of pitch,
  playing effort, dynamics, and articulation; changing these often changes the
  color as well.
- Source: Stephen McAdams, Meghan Goodchild, and Kit Soden, “A Taxonomy of
  Orchestral Grouping Effects,” `Music Theory Online` 28.3 (2022), especially
  section 5.1.
  https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.3/mto.22.28.3.mcadams.php
- Corroboration: Stephen McAdams, “Perspectives on the Contribution of Timbre
  to Musical Structure,” `Computer Music Journal` 23.3 (1999).
  https://sites.music.columbia.edu/cmc/courses/g6610/fall2011/week4/McAdams-timbre-structure.pdf
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: Keep acoustical vocabulary subordinate to audible action. The attack,
  continuation, and decay model is explanatory, not a claim that every sound
  has three neat stages.

### Blend is a continuum and can create an emergent color

- Claim: Timing, tuning, balance, common motion, spectral density, and attack
  affect blend. Strong fusion can create a perceived sonority in which the
  constituents are difficult to identify; partial fusion can preserve a
  resistant or heterogeneous voice.
- Source: McAdams, Goodchild, and Soden, sections 4.9–4.15.
  https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.3/mto.22.28.3.mcadams.php
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Caveat: Treat categories as listening tools, not objective verdicts detached
  from performance.

## DJ Shadow

### Album method and break chopping

- Claim: DJ Shadow set out to make `Endtroducing.....` entirely from samples.
  He says he generally divided breaks into about nine pieces, sometimes more,
  isolating successive bass-drum, snare, and hi-hat attacks so he could
  reconstruct or recombine the performed break.
- Source: Sam Cannon, “DJ Shadow,” `Keyboard`, October 1997, archived by
  Solesides, especially interview lines 75–93.
  https://www.solesides.com/dj-shadow-mdj-shadow-keyboard-october-1997.html
- Confidence: HIGH as a detailed contemporary first-person account.

### Decay, ambience, and “The Number Song”

- Claim: DJ Shadow avoided truncating a drum's decay or leaving the kick without
  ambience and sometimes inserted sampled “air” from the source record. For
  “The Number Song,” he sampled every usable passage of a vocal break in which
  the singer was not speaking, producing about sixteen fragments whose residual
  vocal reverb gives the beat weight and tone.
- Source: Cannon, `Keyboard`, lines 85–97.
  https://www.solesides.com/dj-shadow-mdj-shadow-keyboard-october-1997.html
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Caveat: The user's remembered “dozen nearly identical snares from a jazz
  recording” remains unverified. The fragments include multiple drum-kit
  elements and ambience, and the interview calls the source only a 45.

### Preserved drag

- Claim: DJ Shadow says he programmed the opening of “Napalm Brain” to preserve
  the slight drag with which a drummer settles into a groove, one of many small
  methods used to make sample-based music feel live.
- Source: Nathan Rabin, “DJ Shadow,” `The A.V. Club`, August 30, 2006.
  https://www.avclub.com/dj-shadow-1798209921
- Confidence: HIGH as first-person account.

## John Cage and `Bacchanale`

### The small-stage constraint

- Claim: Syvilla Fort asked Cage to compose for `Bacchanale`; the Cornish
  Theatre had neither wings nor pit and percussion would leave too little room
  for the dancer. A piano was available, so Cage altered its strings to create
  another field of sound.
- Source: John Cage, “How the Piano Came to Be Prepared,” first-person essay.
  https://data-johncage.org/preparedPiano/howThePiano.html
- Corroboration: New York Public Library, `Bacchanale` manuscript record.
  https://digitalcollections.nypl.org/items/b0fd4ab0-29a3-0133-92a7-60f81dd2b63c
- Confidence: HIGH.

### Cowell and the first preparations

- Claim: Cage had heard Henry Cowell pluck, mute, and rub piano strings. Cage
  first tried a pie plate, which bounced, then nails, which slipped, before
  finding that screws and bolts could remain between the strings; a preparation
  could yield both resonant and muted sounds depending on the pedal.
- Source: Cage, “How the Piano Came to Be Prepared.”
  https://data-johncage.org/preparedPiano/howThePiano.html
- Confidence: HIGH as Cage's retrospective first-person account.
- Caveat: Cage's inventories vary across retellings. Use the sequence as his
  account, not as proof of one definitive first configuration or an invention
  without precedent.

## Meredith Monk

### Voice as a nonverbal instrument

- Claim: Monk dates a decisive realization to around 1965: she could work with
  her voice as she had with movement and build a personal vocabulary of range,
  breath, resonance, gesture, timbre, texture, and nonverbal sound.
- Source: National Endowment for the Arts podcast interview, lines 128–43.
  https://www.arts.gov/stories/podcast/meredith-monk
- Source: Deborah Jowitt, “Getting Down to the Bones: Meredith Monk and Deborah
  Jowitt in Conversation,” Walker Art Center, lines 180–200.
  https://www.walkerart.org/reader/meredith-monk-deborah-jowitt-interview/
- Corroboration: Meredith Monk, official biography.
  https://www.meredithmonk.org/about/biography
- Confidence: HIGH as repeated first-person testimony and official chronology.
- Caveat: Avoid turning the date into an overprecise single-afternoon origin
  myth; the insight grew from prior vocal, movement, and performance work.

### Individuality within the vocal ensemble

- Claim: Monk says she does not seek imitators of her own voice; she values each
  ensemble member's unique timbre and quality and resists blending them into
  categories. She describes the vocal actions as bodily even when the singers
  remain seated in `Dolmen Music`.
- Source: “Interview with Meredith Monk,” Éditions Contrechamps.
  https://books.openedition.org/contrechamps/1683?lang=en
- Source: Jowitt interview, especially lines 193–202.
  https://www.walkerart.org/reader/meredith-monk-deborah-jowitt-interview/
- Confidence: HIGH as first-person account.

### `Dolmen Music`

- Claim: `Dolmen Music` was composed in 1979 for six voices, cello, and
  percussion. It began within the larger work `Recent Ruins`; the vocal
  ensemble's individual bodies and voices remained central to its development.
- Sources: IRCAM work record; Walker Art Center interview notes; IRCAM work
  overview.
  https://ressources.ircam.fr/fr/work/dolmen-music
  https://www.walkerart.org/reader/meredith-monk-deborah-jowitt-interview/
  https://medias.ircam.fr/en/composer/meredith-monk/workcourse
- Confidence: HIGH.

### Connection to DJ Shadow

- Claim: “Midnight in a Perfect World” incorporates sampled material from
  Monk's `Dolmen Music`.
- Source: Sam Inglis, “Classic Tracks: DJ Shadow ‘Midnight In A Perfect World,’”
  `Sound on Sound`.
  https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/classic-tracks-dj-shadow-midnight-perfect-world
- Corroboration: MusicBrainz release relationships; TIDAL interview/profile of
  Monk.
  https://musicbrainz.org/release/2ebb18c5-4bfe-4ca9-96a9-205c15e5134c
  https://tidal.com/magazine/article/meredith-monk-interview/1-88260
- Confidence: HIGH that Monk's recording is sampled.
- Caveat: Sources differ in their descriptions of which exact vocal or
  instrumental fragment is most audible. Do not specify more narrowly than
  “material from `Dolmen Music`” without inspecting source audio and permissions.

## Arnold Schoenberg, “Farben”

### Continuous timbral transformation

- Claim: In the third of `Five Pieces for Orchestra`, op. 16, instrumental
  groups alternate within a nearly stable five-voice chord. Schoenberg asks for
  entrances gentle enough that the changing composite color, rather than each
  handoff, becomes audible. Instrumental changes often occur faster than pitch
  changes.
- Source: McAdams, Goodchild, and Soden, sections 4.10–4.11, with score and
  audio examples and reference to Charles Burkhart's analysis.
  https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.3/mto.22.28.3.mcadams.php
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: A short supporting example, not a general history of
  `Klangfarbenmelodie`.

## Section-specific interpretations

The following are the book's analytical proposals rather than statements made
by the cited artists:

- Repeated rhythmic function can be stable while the acoustic identity of each
  event remains distinct.
- “Record air” carries room, decay, adjacent actions, and provenance rather
  than functioning as literal emptiness.
- The prepared piano makes selection of pitch inseparable from selection of
  material behavior.
- A timbral vocabulary becomes syntax when its identities can recur,
  transform, blend, resist, and cross thresholds.
