# Source Ledger: Discover the Form

## Gavin Bryars, `The Sinking of the Titanic`

- Claim: Bryars says the work began as a conceptual-art sketch for a 1969
  Portsmouth exhibition, became a performing version in 1972, and was recorded
  for the first release on Brian Eno's Obscure label in 1975. He identifies the
  work as `1969–`, reflecting its continuing versions.
- Source: Gavin Bryars, official work and recording note.
  https://gavinbryars.com/post/the-sinking-of-the-titanic-5
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Caveat: The page carries a 1975 release date while describing the work's
  earlier development. Preserve the distinction between composition history
  and release metadata.

- Claim: Bryars's notes cite junior wireless operator Harold Bride's April 19,
  1912 account that the band was still playing `Autumn` when he last saw it.
  Bryars turns this testimony into the poetic premise that the music might
  continue to reverberate underwater and descend with the ship.
- Sources: Bryars official note; Bryars's official Bandcamp edition reproduces
  more of the documentary discussion and conflicting testimony.
  https://gavinbryars.com/post/the-sinking-of-the-titanic-5
  https://gavinbryars.bandcamp.com/album/sinking-of-the-titanic
- Confidence: HIGH for what Bryars's work uses and proposes; MEDIUM for the
  historical identity of the final hymn.
- Caveat: Survivor accounts conflict, and hymn tunes called `Autumn` differ.
  State this uncertainty. The section is about Bryars's documented premise,
  not proof of what the band last played.

## Arvo Pärt, `Tabula rasa` and `Silentium`

- Claim: `Tabula rasa` was composed in 1977 for two violins, string orchestra,
  and prepared piano. `Silentium`, its slow second movement, uses a prolation
  canon whose voices move at different rhythmic speeds.
- Source: Arvo Pärt Centre official work record.
  https://www.arvopart.ee/en/arvo-part/work/537/
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Caveat: The section describes the thinning and ending as an audible
  interpretation; the source establishes the work structure and composer's
  context.

- Claim: Pärt wrote four unusually long measures of rest at the end, and
  accounts of the September 30, 1977 premiere recall that the audience delayed
  applause beyond them.
- Sources: Arvo Pärt Centre official work record and Centre article.
  https://www.arvopart.ee/en/arvo-part/work/537/
  https://www.arvopart.ee/en/arvo-part/article/the-sound-of-spirit/
- Confidence: HIGH for the written rests and attributed premiere memories.
- Caveat: Treat the remembered audience response as testimony, not a universal
  effect guaranteed by the score.

## Tōru Takemitsu, `Rain Tree`

- Claim: `Rain Tree` was composed in 1981 for three percussionists and premiered
  in Tokyo on May 31, 1981.
- Sources: Schott Music official work page; IRCAM work record.
  https://www.schott-music.com/en/rain-tree-noc91098.html
  https://brahms.ircam.fr/en/work/ame-no-ki-rain-tree
- Confidence: HIGH.

- Claim: The title alludes to Kenzaburō Ōe's image of a tree whose leaves
  release stored rain after the shower; the score uses two marimbas,
  vibraphone, and crotales, supporting an analysis of action, resonance, and
  aftersound.
- Sources: Hong Kong Philharmonic programme note; Schott official work page.
  https://www.hkphil.org/f/concert/23254/j10w40a-tea-nightingale-E-web.pdf
  https://www.schott-music.com/en/rain-tree-noc91098.html
- Confidence: HIGH for the literary source and instrumentation; INTERPRETIVE
  for the section's formal hearing.
- Caveat: Ōe's image appears in related writings with a publication history
  close to the work's date. Avoid claiming the music narrates a particular
  story or that every resonant attack depicts a drop.

## General source discipline

- Formal categories, note-level examples, and exercises are the author's
  theoretical synthesis and do not require historical attribution.
- Distinguish the last action, last audible resonance, written ending, and
  audience response rather than treating “silence” as one thing.
- Historical anecdotes establish problems; they do not prove that one formal
  method is universally effective.
