# Chapter Brief: Experience Time — Making Duration Perceptible

## Central question

How can a musical span feel necessary without being filled with incident?

## Chapter promise

The chapter will help readers compose the experience of duration rather than
merely select a number of minutes. It will distinguish length from slowness,
show how event rate, grouping, memory, continuity, contrast, and bodily effort
create different temporal experiences, and offer ways to test whether a piece
has discovered its scale or merely postponed its next decision.

## Central claim

Duration is not an empty container into which musical events are placed. Music
gives listeners clocks—pulse, breath, decay, phrase, recurrence, expectation,
and bodily effort—and can strengthen, multiply, or remove those clocks.

Sparse music does not simply make time pass slowly. A passage can feel slow in
the moment yet full when remembered; a dense passage can feel fast while it is
happening yet leave many landmarks behind. The composer's work is to shape the
relations among clock time, felt passage, and remembered span.

## Opening and return

Open with the four thick, oversized scores that arrived for the FLUX Quartet
before its landmark 1999 complete performance of Morton Feldman's
`String Quartet II`.
The physical object announces a problem before a note is played. In performance,
sections may last fifteen minutes and a recurrence may arrive an hour after its
earlier appearance. The quartet and listener cannot hold the work as they hold
a conventional movement.

Return at the end to the players several hours into the piece. The music's scale
is now carried not only by notes but by posture, memory, touch, fatigue, and the
fact that a familiar passage can return without restoring the person who first
heard it.

## Primary encounters

### Morton Feldman, `String Quartet II` and `Triadic Memories`

- Function: opening frame and fullest account of scale, memory, and bodily time.
- Makes audible: duration can exceed ordinary formal memory, turning imperfect
  recognition into a compositional condition.
- `String Quartet II`: repeated cells, long sections, recurrences after very
  long absences, quietness without uniformity, and continuous physical demand.
- `Triadic Memories`: repeated chords in unequal counts, reordered after the
  composer forgot their prior sequence, described by Feldman as formalizing a
  disorientation of memory.
- Caution: do not treat the music as an undifferentiated six-hour hush. Do not
  romanticize injury or confuse performer endurance with aesthetic merit.

### Éliane Radigue, `Trilogie de la Mort`

- Function: contrast Feldman's discrete gestures with nearly continuous change.
- Makes audible: a transformation may be coherent even when no listener can
  identify the instant at which it occurs.
- Musical focus: slow changes in the proportions within a sustained field;
  fundamentals yielding to harmonics, micro-beats, and pulsations; continuity
  that permits retrospective recognition of change.
- Caution: reserve the acoustical interior of drones for `Sustain`; here the
  subject is the timescale and perceptual threshold of transformation. Avoid
  technological explanation.

### Olivier Messiaen, `Abîme des oiseaux`

- Function: provide a shorter, dramatically contrasted model of experienced
  time.
- Makes audible: long, soft clarinet tones and broad silences create one kind
  of time; agile birdsong creates another inside the same solo movement.
- The slow and rapid materials are not merely different tempos. They assign
  different weight to breath, attack, continuity, and expectation.
- Caution: do not tell the prison-camp premiere story here; it remains available
  for `Limit`. State Messiaen's theological interpretation as his, not as a
  universal meaning of slow sound.

## Supporting theory

- Morton Feldman's distinction between form and scale after roughly ninety
  minutes
- Feldman's account of formal memory as something that can be deliberately
  disoriented
- The difference between felt speed while listening and remembered duration
  afterward
- Event density, grouping, attention, expectation, and bodily response as
  influences rather than deterministic formulas
- The distinction between music that represents suspended time and a listener's
  actual subjective loss of clock time

## Required theoretical ground

- Clock time and experienced time
- Length, tempo, event rate, and rate of change as separate variables
- Audible clocks: pulse, meter, breath, decay, phrase, harmonic rhythm, and
  recurrence
- Event density and event magnitude
- Segmentation: how boundaries make time countable and memorable
- Continuity: how elision and slow change weaken boundary perception
- Anticipation without promised climax
- Prospective listening and retrospective memory
- Multiple temporal scales inside one passage
- Acclimation, boredom, fatigue, and renewed attention
- Performer time: breath, muscle, posture, concentration, and recovery
- Permission for continuous focus, wandering, departure, or return

## Practical choices to map

1. Which clocks will the listener hear?
2. How frequently does something become an event rather than part of a state?
3. How large is each event relative to the surrounding activity?
4. Where are the boundaries, and must they be remembered?
5. Will change be detected as it happens or recognized afterward?
6. What does the performer have to sustain physically and mentally?
7. Does the work require uninterrupted attention, and has it earned that demand?
8. What test tells the composer that the duration is structural rather than
   merely desired?

## Boundaries

Reserve for later chapters:

- Full taxonomy and ethics of repetition: `Repeat`
- Beating, overtones, resonance, and drone construction: `Sustain`
- Phase, asynchronous ensemble clocks, and breath-coordinated layers: `Drift`
- The prison conditions and available-instrument story of Messiaen's quartet:
  `Limit`
- Complete theories of continuation and ending: `Discover the Form`

## Failure modes to address

- Equating slowness, length, quietness, and depth
- Event famine: too little happens for any temporal relation to become audible
- Uniform rarity: every isolated event is assigned the same weight
- Automatic inflation: stretching material without changing its scale
- Treating boredom as either proof of failure or proof of the listener's moral
  deficiency
- Mistaking a composer's desired duration for a musical necessity
- Requiring bodily endurance without composing rest, rotation, or consent
- Assuming listeners will experience time identically

## Exercises

### Notice: Passing and remembering

Hear two passages of equal clock length with different event densities. Record
how fast each felt while sounding, then what remains in memory. Do not turn the
result into a universal law.

### Isolate: Nine attacks

Write a four-minute study containing exactly nine attacks. Shape duration with
spacing, decay, register, and relation among the attacks. Make at least one long
wait feel structurally different from the others.

### Complicate: Change without a moment

Make a five-minute transformation between two clearly different states. The
change must be continuous enough that a listener can hear that it happened but
cannot confidently point to when. Then make a second version with one explicit
boundary and compare the experienced spans.

## Desired ending

Return to the FLUX players and describe scale as something borne rather than
merely measured. The next chapter, `Limit`, follows naturally: once duration
has shown how much a musical world can ask, the composer must decide which
materials and rules are sufficient to sustain it.
