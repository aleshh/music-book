# Section Brief: Repeat — Making Sameness Consequential

## Central question

How can repetition accumulate meaning instead of merely confirming what the
listener already knows?

## Section promise

By the end of the section, the reader should be able to hear and compose
several distinct kinds of repetition. The reader should understand that a
repeated unit can remain materially stable while its relationships, listener,
or context changes; know how the size and character of a repeating unit affect
form; and be able to design repetition that calms, opens, insists, grieves, or
creates forward pressure.

## Central claim

Repetition is not one technique and does not carry one affect. It is an
agreement about what will remain available while change moves elsewhere. The
composer's decisive question is therefore not simply “What repeats?” but
“Where will the change be allowed to occur?”

The section applies the book's four locations of change:

1. Material: the repeated thing is altered, accumulated, subtracted, or eroded.
2. Relationships: the thing remains while harmony, orchestration, density,
   register, or foreground changes around it.
3. Listener: memory, expectation, fatigue, and acclimation change its function.
4. Context: performers, audience behavior, social stakes, or place change what
   the repetition means.

## Opening and return

Open with Roscoe Mitchell performing `Nonaah` at Willisau in 1976. Some audience
members are hostile; Mitchell holds the opening phrase until the room's relation
to it changes. Return at the end to the fact that the phrase did not win by
remaining semantically identical. Its repetitions made a public boundary, and
the listeners became part of the form.

## Primary encounters

### Roscoe Mitchell, `Nonaah`

- Repetition as insistence, negotiation, and pressure.
- Small changes of accent, pacing, register, and extension matter because the
  identity remains unmistakable.
- The context and audience change; the phrase's social function changes.

### Terry Riley, `In C`

- Fifty-three modules repeated locally; performers decide when to advance.
- Steve Reich's high-C pulse creates a shared reference.
- Repetition opens collective freedom and produces an emergent vertical field.
- The identity of the piece resides in materials plus relations and rules, not
  in one fixed sounding result.

### Julius Eastman, `Gay Guerrilla`

- “Organic music”: new sections retain or gradually remove prior information.
- Accumulation is memory made structural.
- Repetition can carry identity, history, conflict, and political pressure.
- The hymn's emergence changes how the preceding process is heard.

## Supporting encounters

### Erik Satie, `Vexations`

- Extreme duration makes performers, audience movement, fatigue, touch, and
  renewed attention into form.
- Cage's 1963 realization is an interpretation of Satie's conditional note,
  not unquestionable proof of Satie's intended performance practice.

### Maurice Ravel, `Boléro`

- The melodic and rhythmic materials stay unusually fixed while orchestration,
  register, doubling, and dynamics carry the form.
- Tempo is structural because the long controlled increase depends on scale.

### Gavin Bryars, `Jesus' Blood Never Failed Me Yet`

- An irregular thirteen-bar vocal fragment remains sovereign while the frame
  around it gradually changes.
- Repetition can deepen attention to a vulnerable particular rather than erase
  identity.
- The story of the quiet, weeping art studio is identified as Bryars's memory.

### Johannes Brahms, Fourth Symphony finale

- Brief counterexample: a recurring ground can support intensely directed form.
- Reserve detailed developing variation and harmonic analysis for `Make a
  Harmonic World` or `Discover the Form`.

## Required theoretical ground

- Repetition as identity across time, not literal duplication of experience.
- The first, second, and later appearances of the same object have different
  formal functions.
- Unit size: attack, note, pulse, cell, phrase, harmonic cycle, or ground.
- Seam: audible loop boundary, elided boundary, overlapping or irregular return.
- Invariant and variable: what must remain for recognition; what may change.
- Rates of change and nested cycles.
- Accumulation, subtraction, replacement, orchestral reframing, and erosion.
- Repetition versus recurrence, variation, ostinato, and ground.
- Process as audible behavior without claiming that all process is neutral.
- Repetition's affect is made by tempo, articulation, register, dynamics,
  harmony, density, embodiment, and context—not by recurrence alone.
- Ending a repetition: fulfillment, exhaustion, interruption, mutation,
  dispersal, or a change of frame.

## Boundaries

- Phase, drift, asynchronous layers, and independent clocks receive only the
  explanation necessary for `In C`; they belong in a restored `Drift` section.
- Detailed theories of memory and long duration belong in `Experience Time`.
- Repetition as background, furniture, or environmental condition belongs in
  `Place` and `Attend`.
- Detailed passacaglia analysis belongs in a harmony or form section.
- Do not turn the section into a history of musical repetition or a survey of
  minimalism.

## Failure modes to address

- Repetition used because the composer does not know what should happen next.
- A process treated as an alibi for results no one has listened to critically.
- Continuous addition mistaken for inevitable growth.
- Fear of boredom leading to changes before an identity has become audible.
- Perfect regularity making every level of the form predictable.
- Assuming that repetitive music is necessarily calm, impersonal, or trance-like.
- Variation so constant that nothing remains available long enough to recur.

## Exercises

### Notice

Repeat one short phrase ten times without intentional variation. Afterward,
write down where it seemed to establish itself, become background, create an
expectation, or acquire a new accent through memory alone.

### Isolate

Use one cell to make two short studies. In one, repetition should reduce
pressure; in the other, it should increase pressure. Keep the pitches fixed and
change only timing, register, articulation, dynamics, and surrounding silence.

### Complicate

Give several performers the same small collection of modules and a shared
reference, but let each decide when to repeat or advance. Define one rule that
preserves identity and one rule that permits the group to surprise itself.

## Desired ending

Return to Mitchell. The repeated phrase became consequential because it was
held long enough for the room to change. Then point forward: repetition can
also change because two recurring parts gradually stop agreeing about where
“now” is. That is the question of drift, and it deserves a separate section.
