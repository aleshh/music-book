# Section Brief: Drift

## Central question

How can parts remain recognizably related while ceasing to share one grid?

## Section promise

By the end of the section, the reader should be able to distinguish phase,
drift, polyrhythm, pulse, meter, and general asynchrony; design independent
clocks whose changing relations remain audible; use breath and bodily cycles
without turning imprecision into a virtue; shape coincidences and near-meetings
as form; and give performers enough common information to separate without
losing the piece.

## Central claim

Drift is not freedom from time. It is a changing relationship between times.
For separation to matter, the parts must retain some audible kinship: common
material, pulse, cycle, field, contour, or formal boundary. The composer does
not need to specify every vertical event, but must decide what makes an
unplanned meeting consequential and what keeps independence from becoming
indifference.

## Opening and return

Open with Reich aligning two copies of Brother Walter's speech and hearing them
separate because two recorders do not run at exactly the same speed. Keep the
speaker and warning present while moving from the mechanism to the changing
rhythmic relation.

Return to the voice at the end. Drift has multiplied the phrase's internal
rhythms, but it has not made the source ownerless. Turn toward `Layer`: once
independent clocks produce changing vertical relations, which of those
relations can the listener actually perceive as strands, field, or mass?

## Primary encounters

### Steve Reich, `It's Gonna Rain` and `Piano Phase`

- The tape accident demonstrates material remaining fixed while relation
  changes.
- Define phase through gradual displacement and successive realignments.
- `Piano Phase` turns a machine-discovered process into an embodied listening
  practice: one performer holds, another accelerates slightly, and both hear
  emergent patterns.
- Everything is worked out, but exact moment-to-moment execution depends on
  performers' ears and coordination.

### Conlon Nancarrow, Study No. 21, `Canon X`

- One voice accelerates from slow to fast while another decelerates from fast
  to slow; their tempos cross near the middle.
- A player piano makes an otherwise unperformable temporal design exact.
- Contrast changing offset at near-equal speed with trajectories whose speeds
  themselves cross.
- The example shows that tempo relation can be the main formal material rather
  than an expressive coating applied to notes.

### Steve Reich, `Music for 18 Musicians`

- A continuous pulse in pianos and mallet instruments coexists with phrases
  measured by comfortable breaths in voices and winds.
- Strings follow the rise and fall of the bass clarinet's breath.
- Cueing responsibility passes among players; no conductor does not mean no
  leadership.
- This is not drift away from a common pulse but elasticity inside it, a useful
  counterexample to equating independent time with rhythmic blur.

## Required theoretical ground

- Pulse versus meter.
- Phase as changing offset between periodic copies.
- Drift as the broader family of changing temporal relations.
- Stable polyrhythm versus changing relation.
- Asynchrony with common material, boundaries, or duration.
- Resultant pattern: an emergent rhythm heard between parts rather than played
  by either alone.
- Tempo canon and convergence points.
- Breath, gait, bow, and bodily cycles as measures that remain variable.
- Coincidence, near-coincidence, and missed meeting as harmonic and formal
  events.
- The difference between independent timing and lack of ensemble attention.
- Notating tolerances, cues, convergence zones, and recovery procedures only
  as far as temporal design requires; defer general notation ethics.

## Boundaries

- `Experience Time` owns clock versus felt duration and memory.
- `Repeat` owns the general theory of recurrence and has already introduced
  Reich's process essay. Drift uses new works and asks only how copies and
  cycles change relative position.
- `Layer` owns masking, density, foreground, and strand perception. Drift may
  name emergent texture but not analyze it in full.
- `Release Control` owns performer agency, notation, improvisation, and chance.
  Drift discusses only the minimum shared information needed to coordinate
  independent clocks.
- `Sustain` owns breath as renewal inside a tone. Here breath sets phrase
  length against a stable pulse.

## Failure modes to address

- Starting several unrelated parts at once and calling the result drift.
- Making rates so similar that separation is imperceptible over the work's
  duration.
- Making them so different that no shared identity survives.
- Treating every accidental coincidence as meaningful.
- Asking performers to “play freely” while secretly expecting a polished blur.
- Calling bodily timing natural, organic, or morally superior to measured
  pulse.
- Giving no recovery path when performers lose a relation that the piece needs.
- Allowing the process to erase the identity and context of borrowed speech.

## Exercises

### Notice: Hear the Third Rhythm

Repeat one short pattern with another player. Displace one copy gradually and
write down the rhythms neither part plays but both produce.

### Isolate: Three Clocks, One Span

Give three parts related material and the same total duration but different
internal cycle lengths. Keep one audible invariant and let meetings occur
without correction.

### Complicate: Breath Against Pulse

Maintain a shared pulse while winds, voices, or bowed parts phrase by
comfortable breath or gesture length. Let performer cues govern sectional
change without making breath lengths uniform.

## Working length

Approximately 5,400–6,600 words.

## Desired ending

The two copies of Brother Walter's phrase remain related even at their most
dispersed. End with the question created by their counterpoint: when changing
relations become dense, how do we hear individual strands inside the layer?
