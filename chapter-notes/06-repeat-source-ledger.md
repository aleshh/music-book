# Source Ledger: Repeat — Making Sameness Consequential

This ledger separates established events, participant recollections, musical
analysis, and the section's interpretations. It is a drafting aid, not the
book's final citation format.

## Perception and terminology

### Acoustic, notated, and perceived repetition are distinct

- Claim: Repetition may be physically/acoustically exact, represented as a
  repeat in notation, or perceived as the return of an identity despite change.
- Source: Elizabeth Hellmuth Margulis, “Repetition,” Princeton research record.
  https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/repetition/
- Corroboration: Margulis, `On Repeat`, Oxford University Press.
  https://academic.oup.com/book/9924
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: Paraphrase. Avoid a detour into neuroscience. The section's four
  locations of change are its own analytical synthesis.

### Repetition redirects attention

- Claim: Repetition can establish a piece's basic parameters, redirect attention
  to less obvious levels of structure, and invite participation.
- Source: Margulis, “Repetition.”
  https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/repetition/
- Source: Margulis, “Attention, Temporality, and Music that Repeats Itself.”
  https://academic.oup.com/book/9924/chapter-abstract/157252036
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: Paraphrase cautiously as a theory of listening, not a universal sequence
  in which every listener responds identically.

## Roscoe Mitchell, `Nonaah`

### Willisau circumstances

- Claim: Anthony Braxton was delayed on August 28, 1976; festival organizer
  Niklaus Troxler asked Mitchell to substitute with about an hour to prepare.
- Source: Paul Steinbeck, “Talking Back: Performer-Audience Interaction in
  Roscoe Mitchell's ‘Nonaah,’” `Music Theory Online` 22.3 (2016), paragraphs
  1–4. The article draws on the recording, contemporary reports, Mitchell, and
  Troxler.
  https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.steinbeck.html
- Confidence: HIGH.

### Audience hostility

- Claim: When Braxton's cancellation was announced, a substantial portion of
  the approximately 1,000-person audience heckled Mitchell.
- Source: Steinbeck, paragraphs 5–6.
  https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.steinbeck.html
- Confidence: HIGH, but say “many” or “some,” not that the entire audience was
  hostile. The “half” estimate comes from later participant accounts.

### Repetition and variation strategy

- Claim: Mitchell repeatedly played an angular phrase spanning more than two
  octaves, first extending the final G-sharp, then varying tuning, timbre,
  dynamics, articulation, and eventually adding or subtracting notes.
- Source: Steinbeck, paragraphs 7–22, with score and audio examples.
  https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.steinbeck.html
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Analytical caution: Steinbeck uses written pitches for E-flat alto saxophone;
  the section need not give concert-pitch note names.

### Mitchell's recollection of the “battle”

- Claim: Mitchell later said he made audience noise part of the piece and that
  the music could not move until the hostile listeners respected him.
- Source: Mitchell quoted from Martin 1977 in Steinbeck, paragraphs 8–9.
  https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.steinbeck.html
- Confidence: HIGH as retrospective first-person testimony.
- Use: A very short quotation may be justified, but paraphrase surrounding
  material.

### Formal return and continuation

- Claim: In the sixty-second statement, about seven minutes in, Mitchell nearly
  restored the phrase's original profile, creating a possible point of arrival,
  but continued into a new cycle of variation. The complete performance lasts
  more than twenty minutes and ends to an extended ovation.
- Source: Steinbeck, paragraphs 17–27.
  https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.steinbeck.html
- Confidence: HIGH.

### Additional recording documentation

- Source: New World Records liner notes for Roscoe Mitchell recordings.
  https://nwr-site-liner-notes.s3.amazonaws.com/sa029.pdf
- Confidence: HIGH as institutional recording documentation.

## Terry Riley, `In C`

### Origin and year

- Claim: Riley wrote `In C` in 1964 and remembered its initial idea arriving
  while he rode a bus to a six-hour piano job in San Francisco.
- Source: Library of Congress interview with Terry Riley, April 3, 2022,
  transcript pp. 1–2.
  https://lcweb2.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/Interview_Terry-Reilly.pdf
- Confidence: HIGH as first-person recollection; the bus scene is not
  independently reconstructable and is not essential to the section.

### Fifty-three cells and performer decisions

- Claim: The one-page work has fifty-three ordered cells. Performers choose how
  long to repeat, how to enter and leave, and how to shape dynamics, while
  listening and remaining relatively near one another in the sequence.
- Source: Library of Congress interview, transcript pp. 2–3.
  https://lcweb2.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/Interview_Terry-Reilly.pdf
- Source: Robert Carl, `Terry Riley's In C`, Oxford University Press.
  https://academic.oup.com/book/1491
- Source: Riley's official score catalogue, which identifies the score and its
  performing directions.
  https://www.terryriley.org/sheetmusic.htm
- Confidence: HIGH.

### Reich's pulse suggestion

- Claim: During rehearsal, Steve Reich suggested the shared pulse because the
  musicians were not maintaining the same tempo; Riley says the work then began
  to take shape.
- Source: Library of Congress interview, transcript pp. 2–3.
  https://lcweb2.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/Interview_Terry-Reilly.pdf
- Confidence: HIGH as Riley's first-person account, corroborated by scholarly
  histories.

### Emergent community

- Claim: Riley emphasizes that no one can predict how the group's energy will
  coalesce and that the work depends on performers listening and relating.
- Source: Library of Congress interview, transcript p. 3.
  https://lcweb2.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/Interview_Terry-Reilly.pdf
- Confidence: HIGH as Riley's stated understanding of the work.

## Julius Eastman, `Gay Guerrilla`

### Work, premiere context, and title

- Claim: `Gay Guerrilla` dates from 1979 and was performed during Eastman's
  January 1980 residency at Northwestern University; his spoken introduction
  explains the title and his aspiration to be a guerrilla “if called upon.”
- Source: Wise Music Classical, `Gay Guerrilla` work page and composer note.
  https://www.wisemusicclassical.com/work/57877/
- Source: New World Records, `Julius Eastman: Unjust Malaise`, including the
  spoken Northwestern introduction.
  https://www.newworldrecords.org/products/julius-eastman-unjust-malaise
- Source: Andrew Hanson-Dvoracek, University of Iowa thesis record.
  https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/graduate/Julius-Eastmans-1980-residency-at-Northwestern/9983776932402771
- Confidence: HIGH.

### “Organic” form

- Claim: Eastman's organic form retains the information of preceding phrases
  or sections while adding new information and gradually removing old material.
- Source: DRAM/New World liner notes for `Unjust Malaise`.
  https://www.dramonline.org/albums/julius-eastman-unjust-malaise/notes
- Corroboration: Eastman's recorded Northwestern introduction and subsequent
  scholarship.
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: Paraphrase; do not imply that every passage follows the rule with
  mechanical consistency.

### Musical character and hymn

- Claim: The work builds vertically through accumulated pitches and competing
  rhythmic layers; Martin Luther's `A Mighty Fortress Is Our God` emerges as a
  climactic presence.
- Source: Alex Ross, “Julius Eastman's Guerrilla Minimalism,” `The New Yorker`.
  https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2017/01/23/julius-eastmans-guerrilla-minimalism
- Source: Luciano Chessa, “Gay Guerrilla: A Minimalist Choralphantasie,” in
  `Gay Guerrilla: Julius Eastman and His Music`.
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/gay-guerrilla/gay-guerrilla-a-minimalist-choralphantasie/6B74B5894D69326AF9EB6A41C8D354C2
- Source: University at Buffalo, `Performing the Music of Julius Eastman`.
  https://library.buffalo.edu/content/dam/library/pdf/libraries/exhibitions/Performing%20the%20Music%20of%20Julius%20Eastman.pdf
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Interpretive caution: The historical and political significance of the hymn
  should be argued as interpretation, not assigned to Eastman word for word.

## Erik Satie, `Vexations`

### Manuscript ambiguity

- Claim: Satie's undated manuscript conditionally suggests preparation if one
  intends to play the motif 840 times; it is uncertain whether “motif” means the
  entire notated cycle, and no evidence shows Satie performed or discussed a
  complete realization.
- Source: Robert Orledge, “Understanding Satie's `Vexations`,” especially pp.
  5–8.
  https://www.erik-satie.info/text-ORLEDGE%20-%20Understanding%20Saties%20Vexations.pdf
- Confidence: HIGH.

### 1963 realization

- Claim: Cage and Lewis Lloyd organized the first known complete public
  840-fold realization in New York on September 9–10, 1963; a relay of pianists
  played for eighteen hours and forty minutes.
- Source: `Time`, contemporary report, September 20, 1963.
  https://time.com/archive/6808157/recitals-shoot-the-piano-players/
- Source: Orledge, above.
- Confidence: HIGH for date, duration, and relay format. `Time` contains errors
  in its surrounding biographical chronology; do not borrow those details.

## Maurice Ravel, `Boléro`

### Limited experiment and compositional design

- Claim: Ravel described the work as an experiment in a very limited direction
  and as orchestral tissue without music; melody, rhythmic and tonal ostinati,
  and harmony remain unusually stable while timbre, register, density, and the
  crescendo carry the form toward a late rupture.
- Source: Gurminder K. Bhogal, “Orchestral Tissue, Subordinate Arabesques, and
  Turning Inward in Maurice Ravel's `Boléro`,” `Music Theory Online` 26.2
  (2020), drawing on Ravel interviews and autobiographical writings.
  https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.2/mto.20.26.2.bhogal.html
- Source: Patricia Shaw, “Ravel's `Boléro` Factory,” citing Ravel's 1928
  autobiographical sketch and 1931 interview.
  https://cpb-ap-se2.wpmucdn.com/blogs.unimelb.edu.au/dist/6/184/files/2016/09/33_Shaw-20vhmx8.pdf
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: Quote at most the short phrase “orchestral tissue without music.” Do not
  repeat the weakly sourced “mad woman” anecdote.

### Timbre as changing relation

- Claim: Each melodic return provides a new orchestral assignment or blend;
  timbral development and transformation create direction within repetition.
- Source: Bhogal, sections 1 and 4.
  https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.2/mto.20.26.2.bhogal.html
- Confidence: HIGH.

## Gavin Bryars, `Jesus' Blood Never Failed Me Yet`

### Source recording and composition

- Claim: In 1971 filmmaker Alan Power gave Bryars unused material from a film
  about people living rough in London. An unidentified man's approximately
  twenty-six-second song could form a thirteen-bar cycle; it was well pitched
  but rhythmically irregular. Bryars created a gradually expanding
  accompaniment that follows the voice.
- Source: Gavin Bryars interview, `Now Then`, May 13, 2020.
  https://nowthenmagazine.com/articles/gavin-bryars-never-failed-me-yet
- Source: `The Guardian`, June 20, 2018.
  https://www.theguardian.com/music/2018/jun/20/homeless-mystery-jesus-blood-never-failed-me-yet-gavin-bryars
- Confidence: HIGH. The precise thirteen-bar and twenty-six-second details are
  corroborated by the journalistic history and Bryars's liner-note tradition.

### Art-studio reaction

- Claim: Bryars remembers leaving the repeated voice sounding while he fetched
  coffee and returning to find the normally lively art studio subdued, with
  some people weeping.
- Source: Bryars interview, `Now Then`.
  https://nowthenmagazine.com/articles/gavin-bryars-never-failed-me-yet
- Confidence: HIGH as Bryars's retrospective memory; not independently
  corroborated. Attribute it to him in the prose.

### Bryars never met the singer

- Claim: Bryars worked on the film as mixer/editor and never met the singer;
  the singer and song remain unidentified.
- Source: `The Guardian`.
  https://www.theguardian.com/music/2018/jun/20/homeless-mystery-jesus-blood-never-failed-me-yet-gavin-bryars
- Confidence: HIGH.

## Johannes Brahms, Fourth Symphony finale

### Bach source and Spitta correspondence

- Claim: Philipp Spitta sent Brahms a copy of Bach's BWV 150 in February 1874
  and drew attention to the closing chaconne; Brahms adapted its ground for the
  finale of his Fourth Symphony in 1885.
- Source: Andreas Glöckner, `Bach-Jahrbuch`.
  https://bjb.publia.org/bjb/article/view/2016
- Source: Johannes Brahms Complete Edition, Kiel.
  https://www.brahmsausgabe.uni-kiel.de/erschienene-baende/symphonie-nr-4-e-moll-op-98/
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: Brief counterexample only. Do not use the less secure later anecdote in
  which Brahms supposedly played the bass for friends and proposed a symphonic
  movement.

## Steve Reich and audible process

### Perceptible process

- Claim: In his 1968 essay “Music as a Gradual Process,” Reich distinguishes
  music in which a listener can hear a process unfolding from concealed
  compositional procedures.
- Source: Steve Reich, `Writings on Music 1965–2000`, Oxford University Press.
  https://academic.oup.com/book/27047/chapter-abstract/196341890
- Official publication notice:
  https://stevereich.com/oxford-university-press-steve-reich-writings-on-music/
- Confidence: HIGH.
- Use: Brief theoretical contrast. Do not let the section drift into a history
  of phasing; reserve that for `Drift`.
