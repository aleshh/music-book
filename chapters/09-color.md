# Section 9: Color

::: {.section-summary}
A note name tells us remarkably little about the event that reaches the ear.
This Section treats timbre, articulation, resonance, blend, and resistance as
structures with syntax and memory, rather than decoration applied after the
pitches are chosen.
:::

## 109. Letting sound carry the argument

In 1997, DJ Shadow described how he built the drums on *Endtroducing.....*. He
did not usually take a performed break and repeat it as one intact loop. He
divided it into pieces.

A typical break might yield about nine: one bass-drum hit, the next bass-drum
hit, a snare, a hi-hat, another snare. Once separated, those events could be
placed back in their original order or made to enter new relations.[^1]

This procedure is sometimes summarized as though its goal were control: cut a
performance into units, then command each unit from a grid. The sound tells a
more interesting story. DJ Shadow was attentive to the parts of the break that
did not fit neatly inside the nominal drum hits.

A snare carried a decay. A kick carried the room around it. If a fragment ended
too sharply, he might add what he called the “air” of the source record so that
the reconstructed rhythm did not open onto unnatural blankness.[^2]

For “The Number Song,” the source break came from a 45 on which a vocalist
called over the drums. DJ Shadow sampled the portions where the singer was not
speaking, producing about sixteen usable fragments.

The words disappeared, but their reverberation did not. A trace of the voice
remained at the edges of the drums, giving the beat a weight and atmosphere
that no isolated drum had produced by itself.[^3]

This practice is easy to compress into a sharper anecdote: a dozen nearly
identical snares, taken from a jazz recording, are alternated to make a sampled
beat feel organic. I have not found a reliable source for those exact details.
The documented account is better.

It includes different members of the drum kit, fragments of ambience,
incomplete vocal residue, and performed timing. Its lesson is not that
variation can be sprinkled over a mechanical rhythm to simulate humanity. It is
that the supposedly interchangeable category “snare” never contained
interchangeable events in the first place.

One snare attack carries the drummer's force, the condition of the drumhead,
the room, the placement of the microphones, the vibration left by the previous
event, and the beginning of whatever happens next. Another snare from the same
performance has the same rhythmic and instrumental name but a different
history. When a composer chooses between them, the choice is not decorative.
It changes the event.

This is the compositional power of color. Timbre is often introduced as the
quality that lets us distinguish two instruments playing the same pitch at the
same loudness. That definition is useful in a classroom and nearly useless at
the moment of making music. Instruments rarely hold pitch, loudness,
articulation, effort, and acoustics constant so that color can report to the
laboratory alone.

Change how a note begins and its color changes. Play it in another register and
its color changes. Increase the force and the balance of its internal
components changes. Let it decay into a resonant room and the room continues
composing after the player has stopped.

Color is not what remains after the real notes have been written. It is how the
notes become real enough to have edges, weight, distance, and consequence.

Compose those dimensions directly, and a single note can arrive with the formal
force of a new harmony.

## 110. A note is not its name

Write a middle C on a page. The symbol tells us a great deal and almost nothing.

It identifies a pitch class and register. A stem and note value may indicate a
duration. A dynamic and articulation may narrow the action further. An
instrument name gives a family of likely sounds.

Yet no part of the notation contains the actual beginning of the note: the
moment a bow catches a string, air meets a reed, felt strikes wire, or a tongue
releases a syllable.

Those beginnings are not incidental gateways to a stable tone. They help us
identify what kind of event has occurred. Remove the first fraction of a piano
note and the remaining resonance becomes harder to recognize.

Give a flute tone a breath-heavy onset and its distance changes even if pitch
and measured loudness remain close. Let a singer approach the note through a
glottal break rather than a smooth connection and the interval acquires another
bodily meaning.

For practical purposes, we can listen to a sound as a changing profile.

The **attack** establishes the boundary. It may be sharp, delayed, scraped,
aspirated, struck twice before settling, or nearly absent.

The sound may then **grow or bloom**. A brass tone can gather brilliance after
its pitch is already clear. A bowed string can move from fragile contact toward
a centered vibration. A struck metal object may reveal lower components only
after the bright impact has passed.

During **continuation**, the sound can remain stable, fluctuate, roughen,
brighten, lose pitch, acquire vibrato, or shift its internal balance. A sustain
is not a frozen middle.

The **release** may be cut, breathed away, lifted, damped, or allowed to pass
into another articulation. Afterward, **decay and resonance** may remain in the
instrument or room.

Not every sound contains these stages distinctly. A click has almost no
sustained center. A slowly bowed cymbal may seem to begin by becoming rather
than attacking.

A voice can connect one vowel to another without a new onset. The profile is
not a template. It is a way to ask where the identity of this particular event
resides.

This question enlarges the familiar idea of an instrument. “Clarinet” does not
name one color. It names a territory shaped by register, reed, breath pressure,
embouchure, fingering, room, and player.

A low soft clarinet note and a high forceful one share a source without sharing
one timbral identity. The same is true of a voice, drum, piano, or recorded
fragment.

Treat instrument names as families, not paint labels. A family contains
resemblances strong enough to support recognition and differences strong
enough to carry form.

That range gives every familiar instrument more compositional futures than its
name suggests.

## 111. Color has grammar

Calling a sound warm, dark, thin, metallic, breathy, or rough can begin a
conversation. It cannot complete one. These words gather several perceptions
into an image, and different listeners do not always gather them in the same
way.

“Bright” may refer to strong upper components, a hard attack, high register,
loudness, or simply a learned association with a particular instrument.

A more useful description joins quality to behavior.

Instead of “a dark tone,” try: *the attack is soft, the pitch center appears
late, and little high-frequency edge remains after the bow settles.* Instead of
“a warm blend,” try: *the two instruments enter together, move in parallel, and
neither attack separates from the composite.*

Instead of “an aggressive sound,” try: *each event begins with a compressed
scrape, grows after the attack, and ends before its resonance can soften the
boundary.*

The purpose is not to ban metaphor. It is to make metaphor answer to an action.

Once sounds are described through relations, color can acquire grammar. A
grammar does not require a twelve-volume system. It requires identities and
consequences.

A sound can **recur**. The same dry attack returns often enough to become a
character rather than a surprise.

It can be **substituted**. Another instrument performs the same pitch and
rhythm, preserving function while changing weight, edge, and decay.

It can **transform**. Bow position moves gradually, a vowel narrows, breath
enters a tone, or resonance is progressively damped. The listener hears not a
series of unrelated effects but one identity traveling.

It can cross a **threshold**. A pitch becomes noise-like; a blend separates
into its constituents; a muted attack suddenly rings. The threshold may serve
as cadence, rupture, or beginning.

Two colors can **combine** and produce an emergent sound in which neither
source remains clearly identifiable.

Or they can remain **resistant**, sounding together without surrendering their
identities.

A color can leave **residue**. The played event stops, but its resonance, room,
or remembered edge changes what follows.

These verbs turn color from inventory into form. The question is no longer
*Which sound is interesting?*

It is *What can this sound become while remaining recognizably involved in the
same argument?*

Once color has verbs, it can carry continuity, contrast, and return with the
precision usually reserved for pitch.

## 112. A percussion ensemble inside one piano

In 1940, the dancer and choreographer Syvilla Fort asked John Cage to compose
music for her *Bacchanale* at Seattle's Cornish School. Cage had been writing
for percussion ensembles, but the theater had no pit or usable space in the
wings.

A collection of percussion instruments would leave too little room for Fort to
dance. There was, however, a grand piano at the side of the stage.[^4]

Cage decided that the piano, rather than the scale of his sonic idea, would
have to change.

He had heard Henry Cowell pluck, mute, rub, and sweep the strings inside a
piano. Cage first tried laying a pie plate on the strings; it bounced as they
vibrated. Nails placed between them slipped. Screws and bolts remained in
position. They altered the sounds of particular keys, sometimes producing a
resonant attack and, with a change of pedal, a quiet muted one.[^5]

The prepared piano is often described as a compact percussion orchestra. The
description is apt as long as we hear the orchestra rather than the novelty.
One key may retain a recognizable pitch with an altered edge.

Another may become a short wooden knock. Another produces a buzzing resonance
whose pitch is unstable or secondary. Adjacent keys no longer promise adjacent
versions of the same instrumental behavior.

On an ordinary piano, moving a figure up one step changes its pitches and
register while preserving a broadly familiar mechanism of attack. On a prepared
piano, the transposition may change the material category of every event.

A ringing tone becomes a thud; a muted click becomes a rattle. Harmony, rhythm,
and instrumentation can no longer be planned as separable stages.

This changes how a composer thinks at the keyboard. A four-note chord is not
only a vertical collection of pitches. It is a combination of substances and
decays.

Repeating a rhythm on another group of keys does not merely recolor the
pattern; it may turn a connected gesture into four unrelated attacks. The
keyboard remains spatially familiar while its consequences become local and
particular.

Cage's account is not a myth of invention from nothing. Fort's commission and
the physical needs of her dance created the constraint. Cowell's inside-piano
practice supplied a precedent.

The available instrument resisted Cage's first idea, and trial revealed
another. Color emerged from a relation among bodies, room, history, and
material.

The broader method does not require preparing a piano. Choose an instrument
you think you know and stop treating its normal tone as the instrument itself.
Ask what changes when the point of contact moves, when resonance is allowed or
prevented, when one register is approached with the physical action usually
reserved for another. The purpose is not to compile unusual noises. It is to
discover which differences can become stable enough to have syntax.

Novelty gives a sound one entrance. Form asks what happens after it arrives.

A discovered difference becomes useful when you can bring it back, alter its
role, and make its return matter.

## 113. Articulation gives time an edge

Two notes can have the same written duration and occupy time differently.

A hard attack declares a boundary. Even if the sound decays quickly, the onset
may remain vivid in memory. A soft attack can make the listener notice a tone
only after it is underway, weakening the distinction between silence and sound.

A connected articulation lets the end of one event become the beginning of
another. A damped release turns the end into an action; an undamped release
lets resonance obscure where playing stopped.

Articulation is therefore not a finish applied to rhythm. It decides what the
rhythm's boundaries are.

Play four evenly spaced notes with hard, dry attacks. We hear four points and
three intervals between them. Play the same pitches and timing with slow
beginnings and overlapping decays.

The measured onset times may remain, but the passage becomes a continuous body
with internal changes. Shorten the notes without hardening their attacks and
the gaps may feel porous. Lengthen them with sharp rearticulation and
continuity may still fracture.

Dynamics are similarly entangled with color. A louder note is not usually the
same tone magnified. Increased breath or bow pressure changes which components
of the sound become prominent. A percussion instrument struck more forcefully
may produce a brighter and shorter initial edge.

A quiet high wind tone may become less stable as the player approaches the
limits of breath support. A crescendo can therefore transform material even
when pitch and orchestration remain fixed.[^6]

This is one reason dynamic curves can carry form without functioning only as
climax. A sound may grow louder while becoming more focused, or louder while
breaking apart. It may become quieter and more resistant as noise enters the
tone. A decrescendo can reveal resonance, reduce brightness, expose breath, or
increase tension if the player is asked to maintain a difficult sound near its
threshold.

DJ Shadow's treatment of performed time offers a related example. He later
explained that the first bars of “Napalm Brain” preserve the slight drag with
which the source drummer settles into the groove.[^7]

The choice does not add random timing to make the rhythm seem less exact. It
preserves a trajectory: the groove is not fully located at the first attack but
becomes itself through the player's arrival.

A composer working with color should listen for these trajectories. Does the
sound disclose its identity at once, or become recognizable?

Does its effort increase or release? Does the resonance finish the phrase after
the attack has ended? The answers belong to rhythm and form as surely as the
note values do.

Shape that trajectory, and the sound itself can supply the phrase the notation
only begins.

## 114. Blend, resistance, and the sound between

When two instruments play together, “blend” is often treated as a compliment. A
well-blended section is disciplined; a player who stands out has failed to
join.

This makes sense in music that needs several sources to produce one unified
body. It is not a universal good.

There are at least three useful relations.

In **blend**, sources remain partly identifiable but cohere strongly. Their
attacks, tuning, balance, motion, and tone support one composite event.

In **emergence**, the combination produces a color that seems to belong to no
single source.

Identification of the constituents becomes difficult or irrelevant. The
ensemble behaves like a temporary instrument.

In **resistance**, sources sound together but retain their edges. One attack
protrudes from a sustained body. A bright, inharmonic component refuses to melt
into a smooth chord. Two voices match pitch while their vowels keep them
separate.

These conditions form a continuum, and performance can move a passage among
them. Slightly asynchronous attacks make constituents visible. Precise common
motion encourages grouping.

Balance determines whether one source dominates. Tuning can strengthen fusion
or produce beating that makes the relation itself the foreground.[^8]

Arnold Schoenberg's “Farben,” the third of his *Five Pieces for Orchestra*,
offers a compact example of color becoming motion. A five-voice chord changes
slowly, while different instrumental groups gently take over its tones.

The score asks that entrances avoid emphasis so that the handoffs do not become
a sequence of little announcements. What moves forward is the composite color.
Instrumental change can occur faster than harmonic change, reversing the
priority common in music where orchestration follows a progression already
complete at the piano.[^9]

The passage is not merely one chord painted several ways. As instruments enter
and leave, some components become more audible, the attack profile changes,
and the apparent source of the sonority shifts. The chord's identity is partly
its capacity to survive those transformations.

To compose blend, decide what should remain recognizable. If one instrument is
the core, others may augment its edge, resonance, or continuation. If an
emergent color is the goal, align the features that encourage fusion and avoid
letting one familiar attack repeatedly claim ownership.

If resistance matters, do not simply make one source louder. Give it a
different temporal profile or material behavior so that the disagreement has
character.

Blend without difference can become anonymous. Resistance without relation can
become a collage of sounds declining to meet. The musical question is what the
combination permits that its members could not do alone.

Find that permission, and the combined sound becomes a third participant rather
than a compromise.

## 115. The voice finds more voices

Around 1965, Meredith Monk was sitting at a piano and vocalizing in the
classical manner she had learned. She had already trained in music and dance,
made interdisciplinary performances, and developed movement from the particular
capacities of her body.

She later described a realization: she could work with her voice in the same
way. It need not begin as an interpreter of a text. It could be a nonverbal
instrument with its own vocabulary.[^10]

Monk widened her range. She explored breath, diaphragm, sighing, whispering,
laughing, glissando, the break between registers, and resonances felt in
different parts of the head and body. These were not ornaments placed on a
neutral sung line.

A glottal break could become the hinge of a gesture. A vowel could change the
apparent register and identity of a sustained pitch. A laugh could operate as
rhythm without becoming comic; a breath could be material rather than
preparation for material.[^11]

The phrase “the voice as an instrument” can mislead if it means removing the
person so the voice can function like an abstract sound source. Monk's practice
does the opposite.

The instrument is a particular body. Its range, training, effort, age, history,
and habits are not errors around an ideal voice. They are the conditions from
which a vocabulary can be made.

This became especially consequential in ensemble work. Monk has said she does
not want singers to become copies of her. She values the unique timbre and
quality of each voice. Even inside a tightly coordinated ensemble, one person
should not be mistaken for another.[^12]

That principle complicates the classical ideal of choral blend. Voices can form
a coherent field without surrendering identity. In *Dolmen Music*, composed in
1979 for six voices, cello, and percussion, the ensemble moves among sustained
sonorities, pulsed syllables, calls, rougher attacks, and changing degrees of
individual prominence.

At times the voices establish a collective body; at others, a particular grain
or gesture refuses absorption. The work does not treat one standardized vocal
tone as the medium from which expressive deviations depart. Difference is the
medium.[^13]

There is an unexpected return here. DJ Shadow later incorporated material from
*Dolmen Music* into “Midnight in a Perfect World.”[^14]

The connection should not be reduced to a pleasing bit of trivia: experimental
vocalist secretly meets hip-hop producer. It demonstrates what happens when
color travels.

Inside Monk's work, a vocal event belongs to a body, ensemble, dramatic world,
and history of disciplined exploration. Inside DJ Shadow's piece, a captured
fragment enters another meter, harmony, atmosphere, and network of recorded
sources.

Its color helps construct a new identity, but it does not arrive without a
past. A sample is both present material and evidence of another performance.

The fact deserves compositional attention. Sounds do not become anonymous
because they are removed from their original sequence.

An instrument can carry cultural association; a vocal technique can resemble
practices with histories the composer has not studied; a recording can preserve
a person whose name is not in the immediate frame. Color is powerful partly
because recognition and memory adhere to it.

This does not prohibit borrowing, transformation, or cross-contextual work. It
requires accuracy about what has been carried and judgment about what the new
form asks it to do. “Interesting texture” is not a solvent for history.

Working accurately with what sound carries can deepen transformation rather
than restrict it.

## 116. Designing a color form

Color becomes formal when the listener can hear identity, relation, and
consequence across time. Several questions help make that possible.

### 1. What is the family?

Choose the range of sounds the piece will treat as related. The family may be
one instrument across several actions, several instruments sharing an attack,
a set of recorded events from one room, or voices joined by a vowel or manner
of breath.

Make the resemblance audible before testing its limits. If each sound appears
only once, the listener may hear a catalogue. Recurrence establishes the
family; difference gives it internal life.

### 2. Where does identity reside?

Is the sound recognized by its attack, center, decay, register, resonance, or
physical action? Change another feature and test whether the identity survives.

A dry wooden attack may remain itself across several pitches. A breath-heavy
tone may lose identity when played loudly enough for a focused pitch to take
over. A blended chord may survive substitutions of inner instruments but break
apart when one onset becomes audible.

The answer determines what can transform without becoming a new object.

### 3. Is the change categorical or continuous?

A flute replaced by a drum creates a clear substitution. A bow moving toward
the bridge can create a trajectory with no single moment of arrival.

Both are useful. The first articulates sections; the second makes becoming
itself audible.

Do not assume continuous change is subtler or more sophisticated. A gradual
process can become inert if its endpoints have no consequence. A sudden
contrast can reveal a relation that minutes of transition would blur.

### 4. What combines, and what resists?

Decide whether simultaneous colors should reinforce one identity, create an
emergent one, or remain distinct.

Then compose the attack, tuning, balance, register, and motion that make the
relation plausible.

Let the relation change. A fused sonority may reveal one constituent as its
attack hardens. Two resistant colors may gradually learn a common articulation.

The formal event is not only which colors sound but what kind of togetherness
they can sustain.

### 5. What does dynamic change do to the sound?

Do not write a crescendo as though it were a transparent enlargement. Listen
for the point at which a tone brightens, roughens, focuses, or destabilizes.
The dynamic may be important chiefly because it crosses that timbral threshold.

Likewise, a decrescendo may expose breath, resonance, mechanical noise, or the
effort of maintaining pitch. Decide whether those are residues, destinations,
or failures of the intended sound.

### 6. What remains after the action?

A struck object resonates. A room returns an altered version of an attack. A
sample includes the edge of a voice.

A performer releases tension. The event's aftermath can connect to the next
sound, obscure it, or become a section of the form in its own right.

Composers often plan entrances more carefully than decays. Color asks for equal
attention to what the sound leaves behind.

Compose the remainder, and one release can become the threshold of the next
event.

## 117. When novelty goes slack

The first danger is **the adjective instead of the sound**. A sketch asks for
something warm, luminous, icy, earthy, or haunting, but no action has been
identified.

Such words can help performers when a shared practice gives them meaning. They
cannot replace decisions about attack, pressure, register, balance, and decay.

The second is **the catalogue**. Each new technique appears once, displays its
difference, and makes room for the next.

The sounds may be individually compelling, but no identity persists long enough
to develop. Restrict the family. Let one color recur until a small change
matters.

The third is **novelty as alibi**. An unusual sound can attract attention
before it has acquired any formal job.

Ask the same questions you would ask of an ordinary triad or rhythm: why now,
in relation to what, and what becomes possible afterward? A rare sound is not
exempt from syntax.

The fourth is **false independence**. Pitch, dynamics, articulation, and color
are planned as separate columns, though the instrument will not produce them
independently. A requested soft high note may acquire breath; a forceful low
bowed note may resist the desired smoothness. Compose with those couplings
rather than blaming the performer for physics.

The fifth is **anonymous material**. A recording, voice, or culturally marked
instrument is treated as color detached from a person or practice.

Musical transformation can change context without erasing provenance. Find out
whose sound is being asked to carry the atmosphere.

The sixth is **virtuosity without care**. Extended action can injure a voice,
embouchure, hand, or hearing even when the score contains few notes.

Consult performers. Rehearse the actual duration. A sound is not
compositionally advanced because someone had to survive it.

Finally, there is **color without friction**. Every sonority is blended,
balanced, and gently transformed. The result may be refined but unable to make
a claim.

Abrasion, refusal, and exposed mechanism are also colors. A form needs not only
attractive materials but differences with something at stake.

Restore a real difference, and color regains the power to alter what can happen
next.

## 118. Three experiments

### 1. One note, several bodies

Choose one comfortable sustained pitch. Ask a performer to produce it in four
ways, altering one physical action at a time: point of contact, breath content,
vowel, bow pressure, damping, or another safe variable appropriate to the
instrument.

Keep the versions close in measured duration and general dynamic. Describe
what each sound does without beginning from evaluative adjectives:

- When does the pitch become clear?
- Which part of the sound attracts attention: attack, continuation, or decay?
- Does the sound focus, spread, roughen, or lose stability?
- How does the release affect the next silence?

Arrange the four versions into a phrase. Then reverse them. If the order makes
no difference, the colors may be distinct but not yet syntactic. Find a
transition or contrast that creates expectation.

### 2. A family of attacks

Make two versions of one short rhythm.

In the first, use one attack every time a particular rhythmic role recurs. In
the second, use six to twelve closely related but nonidentical attacks: several
strikes on one drum, repeated plucks on one string, consonants spoken by one
voice, or fragments drawn from one performed passage. Keep the timing and broad
dynamic profile as stable as possible.

Compare the versions. Does the family create a larger phrase through changes
of decay or pressure? Does it merely make the rhythm less consistent? Does one
event become a foreground because its edge is too different? Reorder the
attacks until their differences do formal work.

The aim is not to make a sequence “more human.” It is to hear how much
information lives inside events notation might call the same.

### 3. A three-part color form

Choose a narrow pitch collection and one rhythmic identity. Keep both
recognizable throughout three short sections.

In the first section, create an emergent blend. Align attacks, motion, and
balance so that the sources form one composite body.

In the second, gradually separate the constituents without changing the pitch
collection. Use articulation, register, dynamics, or timing to let distinct
identities resist one another.

In the third, return to the first combination, but preserve one feature learned
from the separation.

The blend may now retain a visible edge, an asynchronous attack, a rough
component, or a different decay.

Do not announce the sections with rests unless you need them. Let changes in
the kind of togetherness articulate the form.

Afterward, make a version in which dynamics remain fixed. If the structure
collapses, decide whether dynamic change was the true argument or merely the
most convenient signal.

Once the convenient signal is removed, the surviving structure reveals what the
colors themselves can do.

## 119. What the air carries

Return to DJ Shadow listening between the drums. What he called air was not
silence. It contained the source record's surface, the room around the kit, the
tail of a snare, and the reverberation of a voice whose words had been cut
away.

Inserted into the reconstructed beat, that apparent background joined the
attacks into a place.

The detail matters beyond sampling. Every musical sound arrives with an edge
that notation may leave open. A bassoon note includes breath and key action.

A prepared piano key includes metal, felt, string, wood, and the uneven life of
a particular instrument. A voice includes the body that found it. An ensemble
color includes the performers' success or refusal at becoming one.

To compose color is not to choose a pleasing surface for a finished structure.
It is to decide which of these particulars will establish identity, which will
change, which will combine, and which residue the music will allow to remain.

Pitch and rhythm do not become less important. They become inseparable from the
way a sound enters the world.

There is also responsibility in this hearing. A color is made by an action, and
an action is made by someone or preserved from someone. The performer who
roughens a tone, the drummer whose slight drag survives inside another beat,
the singer whose recorded vowel enters a new piece: none is merely the last
step of an abstract plan.

Once sound carries bodies and decisions so clearly, composition must ask a
further question.

Which choices belong to the composer, which to the performer, and which can
only be made by listening together?

When those choices become audible, releasing control can enlarge the work
without making responsibility disappear.
