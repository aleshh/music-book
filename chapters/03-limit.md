# Section 3: Limit

## Chapter 29: What a Boundary Makes Audible

In the winter of 1940–41, four professional musicians were prisoners at Stalag
VIII-A near Görlitz. Henri Akoka played clarinet. Jean Le Boulaire played
violin. Étienne Pasquier played cello.

Olivier Messiaen played piano and was composing much of the work that would
become *Quartet for the End of Time*. Their presence gave the piece its
peculiar ensemble.[^1]

The story is often improved in the telling. The cello, we are told, had only
three strings. Five thousand prisoners listened in freezing rain while guards
stood nearby. The actual conditions of a prisoner-of-war camp were apparently
not severe enough; history had to provide theatrical properties.

Rebecca Rischin's research among camp documents, witnesses, performers, and
their families does not support those details. The instruments were poor but
usable.

The audience consisted of several hundred prisoners and guards, not five
thousand. The concert occurred under brutal circumstances without needing the
assistance of a broken cello or an audience the barracks could not have
held.[^2]

Removing the legend changes the lesson. In its embellished form, deprivation
becomes a silent collaborator. The damaged instruments seem to squeeze a
masterpiece out of adversity.

In the less picturesque history, four particular musicians and their available
instruments establish a concrete condition. Messiaen still has to imagine the
music.

The distinction matters whenever composers praise limitation. It is easy to say
that restrictions make us creative. Sometimes they do. A narrow pitch
collection can force rhythm, register, or color to assume greater weight.

A fixed rule can obstruct a familiar solution long enough for another one to
appear. An awkward ensemble can suggest relations that a standard ensemble
would conceal.

But restriction has no virtue in itself. A prison is not an artist's retreat. A
performer's injury is not a useful challenge placed in our path.

Fewer choices can produce clarity, or merely fewer choices. The limit does not
write the piece.

This section asks what the limit actually does. What does it make more audible?
What habit does it prevent? What relation does it intensify? Who chose it, and
who must live inside it? Most importantly, what decisions remain after the rule
has been stated?

## Chapter 30: A Limit Is Not a Lack

Imagine a table with three objects on it: a cup, a key, and a folded piece of
paper. If these are the only objects available, we can describe the table as
sparse. That tells us almost nothing about what may happen. The objects might
remain untouched.

They might be arranged in every possible order. Each might be assigned to one
performer. The key might always move when the cup is lifted. The paper might
contain an instruction that alters the meaning of the other two.

Inventory is not behavior.

Music makes this distinction easy to miss because a small collection is visible
on the page. We can count three pitches, one rhythm, or two gestures and
believe we have identified the method.

Yet a piece using two pitches in any register, at any duration, in any color,
and in any order may permit more surface possibilities than a chromatic piece
governed by one strict pattern of voice-leading. The number of materials and
the degree of constraint are related only after we know what the materials are
allowed to do.

A lack is an absence viewed from outside. There is no bass clarinet, no fourth
player, no time for another rehearsal. A limit is a boundary that has entered
the musical thinking.

The missing bass clarinet may simply be missed, or the ensemble may
redistribute its low register so thoroughly that the absence becomes part of
its identity. The short rehearsal may yield a compromised performance, or it
may lead the composer to devise relations that can be understood, remembered,
and revised by ear.

This does not mean every hardship can be redeemed by a clever score. It means
that conditions become compositional only through decisions. Some are chosen.
Some are inherited. Some should be resisted rather than celebrated. The useful
question is not whether the boundary was voluntary. It is whether the music
has developed a consequential way of meeting it.

## Chapter 31: What, Exactly, Is Limited?

The word *constraint* often suggests a prohibition: do not use more than five
pitches; do not repeat a duration; do not play loudly.

Prohibitions are only one kind of boundary. It helps to hear several different
kinds because each redirects attention toward a different musical problem.

An **inventory limit** governs what may appear. One chord, one interval, four
instruments, six attacks, a single register, or a collection of found sounds
defines the available material.

Inventory limits make the identity and weight of each item easier to hear. They
do not say how the items relate.

An **operational limit** governs what may be done. A line may transpose but not
invert. A sound may lengthen but never grow louder.

A chord may change its spacing while retaining every pitch. The materials can
be numerous while the allowed transformations remain few.

A **relational limit** takes the form of a condition: whenever the voice rises,
the percussion falls silent; a player may enter only on another player's
release; adjacent phrases must share one duration but no pitch. Here the rule
does not merely reduce material. It creates behavior among parts.

A **limit of scale** establishes a count, span, or proportion. A gesture may
occur once, a section may last until one player's breath ends, or the whole
piece may fit on a page. Scale can expose proportion and consequence, although
duration in itself belongs to the larger problem of musical time we have
already considered.

A **limit of agency** governs who may decide. The composer fixes the pitches
but not their order. One performer chooses when the group moves. No one may
begin twice in succession.

Such a boundary is also a social arrangement. We will examine its
responsibilities fully when we reach open form, improvisation, and shared
control.

Finally, a **circumstantial limit** arrives from the world: these are the
players, this is the room, the instrument decays this quickly, the audience is
also eating dinner, the violinist can sustain this pressure but not that one.

Circumstance does not wait to be invited into the method. A composer can ignore
it, accommodate it, expose it, or build with it.

These categories overlap. A four-player ensemble is an inventory of bodies, a
distribution of agency, a collection of ranges and timbres, and a practical
condition of rehearsal. Their value is not taxonomic.

They prevent a common mistake: choosing a small list of things when the music
really needs a rule of relation, or writing an intricate system when a material
boundary would make the desired difference immediate.

Suppose a passage feels diffuse because every player changes independently.
Reducing the pitch collection may make it more consonant without making its
relations clearer.

A better limit might require that every new action continue one feature of the
action before it: its register, duration, contour, or mode of attack.
Conversely, if the relations are clear but the piece lacks a recognizable
world, one sharply chosen inventory may do more than another conditional rule.

Before accepting a constraint, finish the sentence: *This limit allows us to
hear…* If the sentence cannot be finished, the limit may be a private aid, a
habit, or a decoration in the programme note. Any of those may be harmless.
None has yet earned the right to govern the piece.

## Chapter 32: The Price of an Event

A limit changes what an event costs.

If a melody moves freely among twelve pitches, one chromatic note may pass with
little notice. If the music has inhabited one interval for several minutes, a
third pitch can reorganize the field.

If every attack is soft, a hard edge may feel enormous at modest volume. If
three players always enter together, one solitary entrance can sound less like
a textural variation than a change in the group's social order.

The event has not acquired an intrinsic meaning. Its price has risen because
the surrounding system made it scarce.

This is one reason limited music can make tiny decisions feel large. It is also
a danger. Composers sometimes introduce one forbidden event and expect the
violation to supply a climax.

The new pitch arrives, the loud chord sounds, the pulse breaks—and nothing
important happens. Scarcity has made the event noticeable, but notice is not
consequence.

For an exception to matter, it must change a relation. A new pitch might reveal
that the old collection was functioning as a ground. A louder sound might force
a previously fused ensemble to separate.

A broken pulse might expose a line that had depended on it. The listener should
not merely be able to point to the exception. Something afterward should be
newly possible, newly impossible, or newly understood.

Limits therefore work in two directions. They concentrate the present by
reducing alternatives, and they create a shadow inventory of everything being
withheld. We hear what happens against what could have happened but did not.
The narrower the rule, the clearer that absent field may become.

This is compositional leverage, not free expression. A small event can bear
great weight only if the rest of the piece has prepared the scale on which that
weight can be felt.

## Chapter 33: Two Voices, One Language

On February 7, 1976, according to his wife, Nora, Arvo Pärt wrote a short piano
piece after roughly eight years of creative searching.

It would be titled *Für Alina*. The piece lasts about two minutes and became
the first completed work in the method Pärt called tintinnabuli.[^3]

Accounts of Pärt's life sometimes turn the preceding years into absolute
silence followed by revelation. The more accurate story is slower.

He had reached a creative crisis, withdrawn earlier works, studied plainsong
and early polyphony, and continued searching. The two-page piece was not an
impulsive decision to use fewer notes. It was the point at which a restricted
relation became a language.

Two voices proceed together. One is a stepwise melodic line. The other chooses
notes from a B-minor triad in relation to that melody. The melodic phrases grow
by one note at a time until they reach eight, then contract.

Low resonance and the spaces between phrases enlarge the consequences of each
small movement. The rule does not produce two independent strands. It binds
them into one compound action: motion beside a triadic reference.[^4]

We could describe the system and still miss the piece. The right hand's wide
spacing makes some paired notes feel intimate and others exposed.

The unhurried, unequal phrases allow each sonority to decay before the next
relation arrives. Register makes the rule audible as distance. Silence makes it
audible as memory.

Most important for our purposes, Pärt breaks his rules.

At one point the voice assigned to the B-minor triad reaches C-sharp instead.
The long pedal field is interrupted at the same moment. Near the end, when the
contracting melodic design should have returned to a single note, the line
continues for three more. The system has not failed. Its clarity has made these
departures possible as musical thoughts rather than bookkeeping errors.[^5]

A rule can create freedom by giving freedom an edge.

This does not mean every system should contain a picturesque exception. Pärt's
departures matter because the piece has made the relation among melody, triad,
pedal, register, and phrase length perceptible.

The first violation briefly unfastens that relation. The last suggests that the
process of contraction does not own the final word.

The title adds another kind of specificity. Pärt named the work for Alina, the
daughter of a family friend, Irena Veisaitė. Mother and daughter had been
separated.

The dedication does not decode each note into a biographical symbol, but it
prevents us from treating the score's spareness as an abstract display of
purity.[^6] A small relation can hold distance, consolation, and the
possibility of continuation without illustrating any of them.

The lesson is not to imitate tintinnabuli. It is to notice how many decisions
remain after a lucid rule is found: range, spacing, pacing, resonance, phrase
shape, exception, title, and the human situation in which the method will be
heard. Reduction has not removed choice. It has made choice easier to locate.

## Chapter 34: A Rule Is Not an Alibi

Once a process has been designed, it can become tempting to obey it with a kind
of relief. The rule will choose the next pitch. The count will determine the
entrance.

The system will end when it exhausts its combinations. The composer can step
aside and report that the piece has done what it had to do.

But a rule cannot take responsibility for its effects.

The composer selected its domain, starting condition, order, speed, tolerance,
instrumentation, and stopping point. Even a rule borrowed from elsewhere was
borrowed rather than another rule. If the system generates six dull minutes,
its consistency does not make the result innocent.

This is not an argument for concealing process or revising every rough edge
into polished expression. A piece may need indifference, awkwardness, excess,
or a stretch in which the procedure becomes more apparent than the composer's
taste.

The question is whether those consequences belong to the work's pressure or
merely document that the rule continued running.

It helps to subject a proposed constraint to four tests.

First, **does it redistribute attention?** If the available pitches shrink,
perhaps register, duration, or tuning becomes newly vivid.

If nothing else assumes consequence, the piece may simply have less
information.

Second, **does it resist a habit?** A useful limit often blocks the composer's
most automatic solution.

The point is not difficulty for its own sake. It is to prevent one familiar
answer from arriving before the question has been heard.

Third, **does it establish identity?** After several events, can a listener or
performer sense the kind of world this boundary produces, even without reciting
the rule?

Identity may be felt as expectation, pressure, humor, weight, or a
characteristic way of turning.

Fourth, **does it generate decisions?** The best constraints often remove a
large category of trivial choices while exposing smaller, more consequential
ones. A poor constraint either settles everything or produces endless clerical
labor. In one case the composer has nothing left to hear; in the other, the
hearing is buried beneath administration.

A rule can fail these public tests and remain useful as private scaffolding.
Perhaps a hidden numerical plan helps the composer finish a passage whose
audible form depends on something else.

There is no obligation for every method to be decoded. The mistake is to assume
that an elegant backstage system automatically produces an elegant experience
in the hall.

The ear is not disrespecting the system when it hears another piece. It is
reporting the result.

## Chapter 35: Rules That Behave

Some limits work as fences. They keep particular materials outside. Others
work as engines: each event alters what can happen next.

Suppose five gestures are available. A fence says that no sixth gesture may
appear. A generative rule says that a gesture cannot return until another has
changed its duration, or that the end of one gesture determines which player
may choose the next.

The inventory remains small, but the state of the piece keeps changing. History
enters the rule.

This is the useful musical sense of a game. A game need not contain a winner,
competition, or theatrical playfulness. It needs a set of possible actions,
conditions that give those actions different consequences, and participants
capable of recognizing the current state.

A rule that tells everyone exactly what to do may be a procedure. A rule that
requires players to notice what has happened before choosing among meaningful
alternatives begins to create musical behavior.

The distinction is important because composers often confuse freedom with a
large inventory. A player offered forty unrelated gestures and no reason to
prefer one may have many options but little agency. Another offered three
actions whose meanings change with the ensemble may have a narrow vocabulary
and considerable responsibility. We will return to the ethics and notation of
that responsibility later. For now, notice that limitation can concentrate
choice rather than remove it.

A self-imposed impossibility can also be useful when it contains a productive
contradiction. Make a passage continually intensify without becoming louder.
Make every new phrase sound like a continuation while prohibiting literal
repetition.

Let the ensemble preserve a recognizable identity while replacing every member
of its material inventory. None of these instructions has one correct solution.
Each blocks the parameter most likely to supply an automatic answer and asks
the remaining parameters to define what *intensify*, *continue*, or *identity*
actually means.

The impossibility should remain musical. “Use no pitches” may lead to a useful
piece, but only after *pitch* and *use* acquire concrete meanings.

“Write what cannot be played” may reveal the border of an instrument, or may
merely ask a performer to repair an unfinished thought. A contradiction earns
its place by producing perception and decision, not by looking radical in one
sentence.

Limits themselves can move. A pitch inventory can widen while permissible
rhythms narrow. Authority can pass from one player to another. A strict order
can gradually become a field of options.

Such changes allow the system to carry form without relying on one dramatic
violation. We hear not only events inside the boundary but the boundary
changing its pressure.

If several rules operate at once, make their hierarchy clear. Which one defines
identity? Which one produces local variation? Which may break when they
conflict?

A piece with seven equally sacred constraints is likely to become a small
bureaucracy. Music can survive administration, but it should not be made to
fill out forms unless the forms are the point.

## Chapter 36: Four Notes, Five Egos

Tom Johnson's *The Four Note Opera* uses A, B, D, and E. Five singers—a
soprano, contralto, tenor, baritone, and bass—play characters named for their
voice types. A piano accompanies them. The work lasts about an hour.[^7]

This sounds like the premise for an austere study in pitch economy. It is a
farce.

The characters know they are in an opera. They discuss their arias, complain
about the distribution of attention, anticipate the chorus, and behave as if
the composer has trapped them inside both a theatrical convention and a
four-note fence. The restricted pitch inventory becomes part of the joke
because no one onstage is permitted to forget it. Near the end, the cast
becomes immobile and the music slows to a stop in a mass theatrical death.
Four pitches, it turns out, are sufficient for vanity, resentment, structural
analysis, and annihilation.

Johnson's limit does not reduce the opera to four interchangeable events. It
increases the importance of voice type, register, rhythm, text, pacing, and
character.

A pitch that belongs to the soprano's self-description does another job in the
bass's complaint. The inventory remains constant while theatrical relations
keep changing.

The composer traced the work's premise to Luigi Pirandello's *Six Characters in
Search of an Author*, whose figures know they inhabit a theatrical world.

Johnson remembered the 1972 premiere in a small New York theater and the many
productions that followed. Different casts and designs changed the work's
character so substantially that its strictest invariant helped support unusual
flexibility elsewhere.[^8]

This is a useful counterweight to the idea that limited materials naturally
produce quietness, slowness, spirituality, or refined restraint. A method has
no fixed emotional wardrobe.

Four pitches can sustain prayer, slapstick, argument, mechanism, tenderness, or
noise. The remaining parameters and the work's social situation decide.

The distinction also clarifies what we mean by *constraint as identity*.
Identity is not the fact that the work uses four notes. It is the recurring way
those notes encounter operatic expectation: characters sing about the form
that confines them, and each performance gives new bodies to the confinement.
The rule has become dramatic circumstance.

A constraint becomes personal not when no one else has used it, but when the
composer discovers a characteristic set of consequences inside it.

## Chapter 37: Tasteful Thinness

Reduction has acquired a flattering cultural vocabulary. A sparse object may be
called disciplined, essential, contemplative, pure, or honest before anyone has
asked what it does.

A crowded object is made to defend its appetite; an empty one is often credited
with restraint.

Composers should be suspicious of compliments that arrive before the sound.

Tasteful thinness occurs when a piece adopts the signs of reduction without
creating pressure inside them. Events are widely spaced but no interval of
waiting changes their weight.

The pitch collection is small but its members remain functionally
interchangeable. Dynamics are soft but never alter distance or effort. Every
sonority is agreeable, and none risks becoming necessary.

The problem is not beauty. Beauty can focus attention, intensify loss, or make
an unstable relation bearable long enough to hear. The problem is beauty used
as insulation.

A pleasant surface can prevent the method from encountering abrasion,
embarrassment, boredom, conflict, or surprise—the very evidence that would tell
us what the limit can and cannot hold.

Severity offers no automatic escape. A hard, loud, or physically demanding
restriction can be just as decorative as a gentle one. Endurance may become a
badge displayed by composer and performer while the listener hears only
unvaried insistence. An impossible instruction may look uncompromising on the
page while transferring all artistic and bodily cost to someone else.

This is where the difference between chosen and imposed limits returns with
ethical force. A composer may choose to work with one pitch. A prisoner did not
choose the camp.

A performer may consent to explore breathlessness, fatigue, or a precarious
technique; the notation does not make that consent permanent. To describe
adversity as creatively useful can quietly assign aesthetic value to another
person's deprivation.

Ask of any severe boundary: who receives the clarity, and who performs the
cost?

The answer does not require all music to become comfortable. It requires the
composer to distinguish risk from negligence and limitation from romance.

A constraint can be abrasive, comic, ugly, tender, or physically intense. It
should not be exempt from judgment because it is simple enough to summarize.

## Chapter 38: When the Rule Should Break

Some constraints are productive precisely because they never break. Their
continuity creates the condition in which every other relation becomes legible.

Others contain an exception, erosion, or expiration point. The composer must
decide which kind of promise has been made.

A break can **reveal the boundary**. We may not have consciously identified a
rule until its absence makes the earlier behavior clear.

It can **change the scale**. A new event may show that what seemed like a whole
world was one section inside a larger one.

It can **expose exhaustion**. The system has not reached a climax; it has
ceased to generate consequential differences, and continuing it would merely
document obedience.

It can **reveal the real rule**. A piece that appears to prohibit a pitch may
turn out to care more deeply about preserving a contour, a registral vacancy,
or a relation among players. Breaking the visible limit shows what was
actually invariant.

Pärt's exceptions in *Für Alina* perform more than one of these jobs. Because
the triadic relation and phrase process are clear, the departures briefly show
the system from outside.

Yet the piece does not turn its violations into explosions. One can leave a
rule quietly.

Not every deviation should be promoted to formal office. Performers vary,
rooms answer unevenly, counts slip, and sounds contain more information than a
rule can govern. Decide which deviations are errors, which are tolerated
variation, and which can reinterpret the system. If the distinction matters,
make it available to the people who must perform it.

There is also dignity in keeping a rule to the end. Breaking a constraint
merely because the piece needs “something to happen” often means the original
limit never generated enough life.

Before adding the forbidden event, ask whether the neglected motion is already
present in spacing, register, effort, resonance, ensemble relation, or memory.

An exception should not rescue a rule from the consequences of being poorly
chosen.

## Chapter 39: Designing a Useful Limit

Begin with a musical problem, not an admiration for small numbers.

Perhaps your melodies keep developing before their first interval acquires an
identity. Limit contour, not pitch. Perhaps every player fills a silence as
soon as it appears. Limit the right to begin.

Perhaps harmonic changes are doing work that could be carried by register or
voicing. Hold the collection still long enough to find out. Perhaps the piece
is polished but anonymous. Choose a rule that risks an awkward result your
taste would normally edit away.

Then identify the parameter that should inherit consequence. If pitch is
limited, what becomes active: rhythm, tuning, register, articulation, density,
memory?

If entrances are limited, what becomes audible about waiting and response? If
one player controls change, what do the others hear and decide?

Set the scale of the rule. A productive boundary at the level of one gesture
may become inert across a whole work. Another needs a long span before its
pressure appears. Decide whether the constraint governs every event, a family
of events, one section, or the relation among sections.

Specify tolerances. “Use one note” might permit octave displacement, microtonal
inflection, noise, resonance, and sympathetic response—or none of them.

These are not loopholes to close automatically. They define what *one* means in
this piece.

Finally, give the rule an expiration test. It may end after a count, after a
perceptual threshold, after a performer can no longer distinguish new
relations, or not at all.

The test need not be announced to the audience, but the composer should know
whether continuation is still discovering the limit or merely maintaining it.

The aim is not to find the smallest possible method. It is to find a boundary
strong enough to make decisions audible and permeable enough to let the music
answer back.

## Chapter 40: Three Experiments

### 1. The Cost of One Event

Take a short passage you have already written. Do not simplify everything.
Remove one category of possibility: upward motion, simultaneous attacks, one
register, a type of chord, continuous sound, or the right of one player to
begin.

Play or imagine the revision several times. Identify the event that has gained
the most weight because of the removal.

It may be a remaining downward leap, a solitary attack, a crossing into the
vacated register, or a player's first entrance after waiting.

Now decide whether that weight improves the piece. If it does, shape the
surrounding relations so the event changes what follows.

If it does not, restore the removed possibility. The exercise is not a contest
to see how much can be subtracted. It asks whether absence has altered
consequence.

### 2. One Inventory, Three Laws

Choose a small inventory of five sound-actions. They might be pitches, short
figures, instrumental actions, spoken fragments, or mixed events. Keep this
inventory unchanged through three brief studies.

In the first, fix the order but leave duration and spacing free.

In the second, abandon the order. Require each action to inherit one audible
feature from the action before it.

In the third, let a performer choose freely from the inventory, but prohibit
the same adjacency from occurring twice.

Do not make the studies contrasting merely by assigning fast, slow, loud, and
soft. Listen for the behavior each law creates. Which one produces memory?
Which produces response? Which makes the five actions sound like a collection,
and which makes them sound like a grammar?

The inventory is not the composition. The relations are.

### 3. The Constraint Audit

Write or improvise an ensemble passage with three or four explicit
restrictions.

Include more than one kind: an inventory boundary, a relational rule, a limit
of agency, and perhaps a circumstantial condition supplied by the actual
instruments or room.

Perform the passage. Then remove one rule at a time while leaving the others in
place. After each version, ask two separate questions.

What changed for a listener?

What changed for a performer?

A rule may be nearly inaudible yet transform how closely the players listen.
Another may produce an obvious surface pattern while requiring no meaningful
decision from anyone.

Neither result is automatically better. Decide which kind of consequence the
piece needs.

Keep only the constraints whose removal changes something worth preserving.
If the musicians must follow a difficult rule that produces no experiential
consequence, either redesign it or admit that the difficulty is the subject.
Do not hide labor behind elegance.

## Chapter 41: The Vessel and What It Holds

Return to Stalag VIII-A. There are four musicians: clarinet, violin, cello, and
piano. Those facts matter. They shape register, balance, attack, sustain,
rehearsal, and the combinations Messiaen can place before an audience.

They do not account for birdsong, suspended rhythm, immense slow phrases, or
the theological idea of time ending.

The ensemble is a vessel, but a vessel is not empty and it is not an author.
Its weight, size, flaws, and history alter what can be carried. The composer's
work is to hear those conditions without asking them to take responsibility
for the music.

Once a useful boundary has been chosen, another temptation appears. We add a
new event before the existing one has disclosed its interior.

Suppose, for a moment, that we do not. Suppose one sound remains. What has to
be composed inside it so that remaining becomes an action?
