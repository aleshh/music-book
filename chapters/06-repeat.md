# Section 6: Repeat

## Chapter 69: Making Sameness Consequential

Roscoe Mitchell had one hour.

He had not expected to give a solo concert that afternoon. He and the other
members of the Art Ensemble of Chicago were in Willisau, Switzerland, during a
rare break in their 1976 European tour.

Anthony Braxton was scheduled to play at the town's jazz festival on August 28,
but his arrival had been delayed. By late afternoon it was clear that he would
not make the concert. The festival's organizer asked Mitchell to take his
place.

Mitchell ran to his hotel for his alto saxophone. Ordinarily he preferred
several hours to prepare for a solo performance. This time he had about sixty
minutes. On the surviving recording, he can be heard backstage playing long
tones while the master of ceremonies introduces him.

Then the audience learns that Braxton will not appear. Many of the listeners
are furious. Some have traveled a long way specifically to hear him. Catcalls
greet Mitchell as he walks onstage.[^1]

He begins *Nonaah*.

The opening phrase is quick and angular, vaulting across more than two octaves.
It does not soothe the room. At the end, Mitchell sustains the final note for a
little more than a second. A listener shouts. Mitchell plays the phrase again
and holds its last note nearly twice as long, occupying the space in which
another shout might land. Then he repeats the phrase.

And repeats it.

This is not a retreat into a loop. Mitchell adjusts the phrase as though
testing the resistance of an unfamiliar surface. He lengthens the final note
until one breath is nearly consumed by it. He changes the tuning of the high
note that announces each return.

He alters dynamics, roughens the tone, bends a pitch, allows a note to split
into several attacks. Because the identity of the phrase remains clear, every
small departure from it is exposed. The crowd can hear him listening to their
response, but he will conduct the conversation in music and on his terms.

Mitchell later described the situation as a battle. The music, he said,
“couldn't move [until] they respected me.”[^2] He did not mean that nothing in
the phrase could change. Much was changing: breath, timbre, tuning, pacing, the
crowd's noise, the physical effort needed to sustain the final note.

What could not yet change was the subject. Before the piece could proceed, the
room had to hear that subject as something other than an inadequate replacement
for the concert it had expected.

Within two minutes, applause begins to mix with the heckling. Soon the applause
spills into the silences where the shouts had been. Mitchell continues. Around
the sixty-second statement, some seven minutes into the performance, he nearly
restores the phrase as it first sounded: clean in tone, compact in duration,
the final note once again brief.

It could serve as an arrival, a rounded return, an exit from the first section.
Instead he goes on, now adding and subtracting notes, changing the speed of the
cycle, and carrying the phrase into another kind of variation. By the end of
the performance, more than twenty minutes later, the room gives him an extended
ovation.[^3]

We could describe this as a story of persistence, and it is. But persistence
alone is not enough to explain the form. Mitchell did not defeat hostility by
playing an indifferent object over and over. He made repetition into a field of
unusually exact consequences.

The final note became a measure of breath, a claim on public space, a defense
against interruption, and eventually an event the audience anticipated. A rough
tone that might have passed as incidental on its first appearance became, when
repeated, a proposal. When the listeners applauded it, its next return became
an acknowledgment of their response.

The phrase kept coming back. Its meaning did not.

That is the central fact of repetition: the material may return, but the event
cannot. Time has passed. Another sound may now be present. The listener knows
something that was not known before. A hand is more tired; a bow has moved
closer to the bridge; a crowd has changed its mind. Even an acoustically exact
repetition arrives at a new position in our experience.

For a composer, this is not a philosophical consolation for having written the
same thing twice. It is a practical resource.

Repetition is an agreement about what will remain available while change moves
somewhere else. The real question is not merely *What repeats?* It is *Where
will the change occur?*

## Chapter 70: Same Sound, Different Event

Imagine a pianist playing a low D. After a pause, she plays the same D again,
at the same dynamic, with the same touch.

On the first attack, we hear a sound. On the second, we also hear a relation.
The second D tells us that the first was not isolated after all.

If a third D arrives after the same pause, the silence between the notes begins
to sound measured. The next attack may be anticipated.

If it does not come, the empty place where we expected it is no longer an
ordinary silence. Repetition has composed an event by withholding it.

This familiar progression—from occurrence, to recognition, to expectation—is
useful, but it is not a law of three hearings. A listener may fail to recognize
the second sound, or may recognize a pattern after one partial statement. A
complex phrase may require many returns before its boundary becomes clear. A
single event can evoke a familiar type without being repeated at all. The
important point is more modest: a repetition does not simply add another copy
of an object. It changes the network of expectations in which that object is
heard.

Music theorist Elizabeth Hellmuth Margulis distinguishes acoustic repetition,
notated repetition, and perceived repetition.[^4] The distinctions matter. Two
events can be notated with the same symbol and sound noticeably different
because different performers play them. A passage can return in another
register or key and still be recognized as the same idea.

A written repeat sign may send us back to identical measures, but the second
traversal is not formally identical to the first. We know where the cadence
leads. We remember a detail that seemed unimportant before. We may listen less
to the melody and more to an inner line.

Repetition choreographs attention. The first hearing often helps us locate the
object; subsequent hearings allow us to enter it. This is one reason a short
pattern can become more spacious as it repeats. We stop spending all our effort
asking what it is and begin noticing how it is made.

An offbeat accent comes forward. A note we heard as decoration becomes the
hinge of the phrase. The lower voice reveals that it has been tracing a
separate contour. Nothing needs to be added for the music to acquire another
layer.

But the reverse can happen. A word repeated often enough may lose its ordinary
meaning and become a collection of vowels, consonants, and rhythms. A harmonic
figure can cease to feel like a progression and become a color or a place.

A sharp dissonance can become less exceptional, while a tiny change within it
becomes enormous. Repetition can disclose structure, but it can also dissolve
the level of structure we first heard.

This is why “exact repetition” is an incomplete compositional description. It
tells us what the material does. It does not tell us what repetition does to
the material.

## Chapter 71: Where Change Can Go

When a repeating passage feels inert, composers often assume that they need to
alter the passage. Sometimes they do. But material is only one of four places
where change can occur.

The first is **the material itself**. A pitch is added to the cell. One attack
is removed. The rhythm stretches, contracts, or acquires an ornament.

This is the most obvious location because it appears directly in the notes.
Accumulation, subtraction, substitution, erosion, and conventional variation
all live here.

The second is **the relationship among materials**. A two-note figure remains
unchanged while the bass beneath it moves. Another instrument doubles it at an
unexpected interval. A once prominent pattern becomes partly masked by a
denser layer. The object stays in place while its illumination changes. Ravel's
*Boléro* will give us an unusually disciplined example of this kind.

The third is **the listener**. Familiarity redirects attention. Memory makes a
return legible or distorts it. Anticipation gives an attack weight before it
sounds. Fatigue enlarges one detail and erases another.

In a long repetition, the composer does not control these changes in the way
one controls a chord, but can create conditions in which they become
structurally important.

The fourth is **the context**. Different players repeat the same figure. A room
fills or empties. An audience begins to move. A phrase quoted later in a piece
acquires a historical association.

At Willisau, the context included an angry crowd, Mitchell's breath, and the
absent musician listeners had expected to hear. The same notated phrase would
not have done the same work in an empty rehearsal room.

These locations are not sealed chambers. A change in orchestration is both a
change of relationship and, at the level of sounding material, a change of
timbre. A tired player may alter a rhythm, carrying bodily context into the
notes.

The categories are valuable because they correct a common reflex: if the music
repeats, change must be inserted into the repeating unit. Often the more
consequential move is to keep the unit clear and change what it can mean.

Try a simple example. Repeat the pitches C, E, and D at an even pace. Keep their
register and rhythm fixed. On each cycle, change only the bass:

- Place C below the figure. The E brightens it; the D may sound like a
  neighboring tone that wants to settle.
- Place A below it. The same three notes now suggest a different center and a
  more suspended relation.
- Remove the bass. What had sounded like an upper line may become complete in
  itself, or suddenly exposed.

Nothing in C–E–D has changed, yet its harmonic action has. Now restore the C in
the bass and move the figure two octaves higher. The harmony is nominally the
same, but the distance between the layers has made a space. Put a soft tremolo
between them and the space becomes inhabited. Repeat the figure more slowly
and the D has time to become a sonority rather than a passing event.

This is composition by relocation of change. It is especially useful in music
whose surface must remain recognizable.

The repeating figure provides a fixed reference; changes around it become
measurable because they have somewhere to register.

## Chapter 72: How Large Is “Again”?

Before deciding how repetition will change, decide what counts as one time.

A drummer may repeat an attack. A bass may repeat a two-note cell. A melody may
repeat every seven beats while the harmony repeats every four measures.

An entire section may return after twenty minutes. Each is repetition, but each
creates a different scale of memory and expectation.

The repeated unit is sometimes called a pattern, cycle, loop, or ostinato.
These words overlap, but they direct attention differently. An **ostinato** is
a persistent musical figure, often one layer among others. A **ground** is a
recurring bass or harmonic scheme that supports variation above it.

A **cycle** emphasizes the time required to return. A **loop** makes us aware
of a seam: the place where an ending joins a beginning. **Recurrence** is
broader. Something comes back after an absence, recognizably itself but perhaps
altered by what happened in between.

No word decides the music for us. What matters is the unit's length, internal
character, and boundary.

A one-note pulse can be learned almost immediately. Because little effort is
needed to recognize it, attention becomes available for deviations in spacing,
accent, and color. A five-note cell may have a contour strong enough to remain
identifiable under transposition or revoicing.

A thirty-second phrase brings more internal hierarchy: a beginning, a high
point, an implication of closure. When that phrase returns, we do not merely
recognize its notes; we remember how it traveled.

Longer is not necessarily richer. A unit crowded with incident can monopolize
attention and make each repetition feel like a complete rerun. A very short
unit can support an astonishingly complex form if it is distinct enough to
remain audible while other cycles cross it. Conversely, a pattern that is too
neutral may repeat without providing a reference. There is no shame in a plain
figure, but plainness must still be shaped.

The seam also has a musical character. A rest at the end of a phrase makes the
return explicit. Elide the last note into the first and the cycle may seem
continuous.

Let one player restart before another has finished and the seam becomes a
contrapuntal region rather than a line. Use a unit of uneven length against a
regular pulse and its beginning will occupy different metrical positions. The
notes are unchanged, but their accents migrate.

Composers sometimes conceal the seam because they fear that a clear loop will
sound crude. That fear gives away a useful resource.

An audible return can be a source of pleasure, pressure, humor, ritual, or
dread. The seam is where the piece admits what it is doing. Conceal it when
continuity matters; expose it when recognition matters.

There is another decision: how many levels of repetition will coexist. Suppose
a three-beat figure sounds over a four-beat bass cycle and both sit inside a
sixteen-measure harmonic span. The listener can enter the piece at several
scales. The three-beat figure produces frequent local returns.

Its alignment with the bass takes longer to recur. The harmonic span supplies a
still larger breath. If every level begins and ends together, the form may feel
strongly boxed. If the levels cross, local repetition can create large-scale
change without any layer abandoning its identity.

This crossing of clocks leads toward phase and drift, a subject we will reserve
for its own section. For now, the important point is that the size of the
repeated unit determines the size of the difference we can hear.

## Chapter 73: Eight Hundred and Forty

Erik Satie left *Vexations* as a page of slow, chromatic keyboard music and a
peculiar note. The wording does not straightforwardly command every pianist to
play the complete page 840 times. It suggests that if one intends to repeat the
motif 840 times, it would be wise to prepare through profound silence and
serious stillness.

Scholars continue to debate what Satie meant by “motif,” whether the number was
a practical proposal, and whether he expected the work to be performed at all.
No evidence shows that he heard a complete realization.[^5]

John Cage and Lewis Lloyd took the possibility seriously. On September 9 and
10, 1963, they organized the first known complete public realization at the
Pocket Theatre in New York. A relay of pianists played for eighteen hours and
forty minutes.

Each performer entered a musical situation already underway, continued it for a
shift, and handed it to the next. Listeners were permitted to leave and return.
One could hear a few repetitions, sleep, eat, walk through the city, and come
back to find the same page still sounding.[^6]

“The same page” is doing a great deal of work in that sentence.

At the scale of one minute, *Vexations* is an object: a bass theme and two
strange harmonizations, deliberate and unsettled. At the scale of several
hours, it becomes an environment for differences that the manuscript does not
specify. Pianists have unlike hands and temperaments.

Fatigue changes weight and concentration. The room moves through evening,
night, and morning. An audience dwindles and renews itself. A player who has
listened for an hour before sitting down does not enter the same piece as the
first pianist.

The repetition transfers part of the form from the page to bodies and social
time. This does not mean the notated material ceases to matter. Its particular
slowness and harmonic irresolution make the duration unlike eighteen hours of a
cheerful eight-bar tune.

But the page alone cannot account for the event. A complete realization of
*Vexations* is made partly from shifts, handoffs, departures, returns,
stiffness, missed counts, and the fact that breakfast eventually becomes
relevant to music history.

Extreme repetition exposes a principle present in shorter pieces: performers
are not transparent carriers of recurrence. They are among the things that
repetition changes.

## Chapter 74: Freedom Inside the Cycle

In 1964, Terry Riley wrote fifty-three short musical cells on a single page.
Some are no more than a repeated pitch or a small rhythmic turn; others have a
longer melodic contour. The performers of *In C* move through these cells in
order, but each performer decides how many times to repeat the current cell
before going on. One musician may remain with a figure while another advances.
A third may rest and re-enter. The players stay relatively near one another in
the sequence, close enough that neighboring cells can combine, answer, and
temporarily lock together.[^7]

The work did not arrive with all of those relations solved. Riley remembered
that its initial line came to him on a bus to a piano job in San Francisco. The
page of cells followed, but the earliest rehearsal copies had no full set of
instructions. The musicians had to discover how the idea could become an
ensemble.

At first they did not always share a tempo. Steve Reich, one of the performers,
proposed the repeated high C that became the work's familiar pulse. Riley later
said that once the players had this common beat and learned to remain near one
another, the piece began to take shape.[^8]

The pulse is not a conductor. It does not tell anyone which cell to play or
when to proceed. It is closer to a public clock in a town where everyone is
free to decide when to leave home. Without it, the players' freedoms may not be
perceptibly related.

With it, independence becomes measurable. A performer can enter halfway through
another cell, repeat across its boundary, or discover a momentary unison. The
common reference makes difference audible.

This is an important use of repetition: not as an order to remain, but as time
granted for choice. A player repeats a cell long enough to hear what the other
cells are doing.

She may reinforce a composite rhythm, support a fragile harmony, or decline an
emerging consensus. Her next step is not scheduled, but neither is it
arbitrary. The repetition lets her listen before acting.

The vertical music of *In C*—the actual chords, doublings, and rhythmic
interferences that fill the room—is never completely specified. It emerges
from many local decisions. Yet the piece has a powerful identity. The
fifty-three cells, their order, the pulse, the proximity among performers, and
the requirement of attentive coordination create a resilient world. Freedom
does not weaken that identity because the identity was never located in one
final arrangement of notes.

This suggests a distinction between **repeating a result** and **repeating a
condition**. A fixed passage returns as a result: these notes, in this order,
with these relations.

A cell in *In C* also repeats as material, but the more consequential
recurrence is the condition shared by the ensemble: listen, remain within this
neighborhood, decide whether to stay or advance. Repeating that condition
produces different results without producing a different work.

Composers sometimes reach for open form by removing instructions. *In C* is
open because Riley supplied the right instructions. Its freedoms have edges.
The modules must proceed in sequence.

The players must remain attentive to their relative positions. A common pulse
holds the field. These restrictions do not apologize for the freedom; they make
it useful.

There is also a social consequence. In a conventional repeated passage, the
composer may determine how many times everyone plays and when the group moves
on. In *In C*, each performer temporarily controls the duration of one strand
while remaining responsible to the others.

Staying on a cell can stabilize the ensemble or obstruct it. Advancing can lead
or abandon. The same act has a different meaning according to what the group
needs.

Repetition, in this setting, is a way of sharing time without requiring a
single will.

## Chapter 75: Accumulation Remembers

Fifteen years after *In C*, Julius Eastman made a different kind of collective
repetition audible in *Gay Guerrilla*. At a Northwestern University residency
in January 1980, Eastman introduced several of his works by explaining what he
called organic music. In an organic form, each new section carries the
information of the previous sections forward, or removes it at a gradual and
logical rate. A piece grows by remembering what it has been.[^9]

The word *organic* can be dangerously flattering. It may suggest that the form
develops naturally, as if the composer merely waters a process and watches it
become inevitable.

Eastman's music is more willful than that. Layers are added, thickened,
interrupted, displaced, and allowed to collide. Retaining prior information
does not guarantee smooth continuity. Memory can produce crowding.

*Gay Guerrilla*, written for multiple instruments of a similar kind and most
famously preserved in a performance with pianos, begins by establishing
reiterated figures and expands through vertical accumulation. Pitches pile onto
pitches. Rhythmic identities remain active while other patterns arrive beside
them.

Consonance does not simply yield to dissonance once; areas of clarity and
congestion keep renegotiating the field. The music can feel less as though it
is traveling down a road than as though a gathering is becoming too large for
the room.

Then a historical object becomes audible inside the accumulation: the Lutheran
hymn *A Mighty Fortress Is Our God*. It does not enter as a neatly framed
quotation announced after the process has stopped.

It emerges through the texture, as though the process has been carrying the
possibility of the hymn before we could name it.[^10]

That recognition bends time in both directions. The hymn affects what follows,
but it also changes our memory of what preceded it.

Repeated fragments that seemed abstract acquire a retrospective gravity. What
sounded like an accumulation of intervals begins to sound like the pressure of
something trying to become speakable.

Eastman explained the title *Gay Guerrilla* in the same Northwestern remarks.
For him, a guerrilla was someone prepared to sacrifice for a cause, and he
hoped he might be such a person if called upon.[^11] This does not turn every
musical detail into a biographical code. It does prevent us from describing the
piece's repetition as neutral. A Black gay composer brings a battle hymn of the
European Protestant tradition into a work named for queer struggle. The hymn
can carry faith, fortification, institution, militancy, and a claim upon an
inherited tradition at once. Its repetitions do not settle those associations.
They intensify their coexistence.

Compare this with the smooth language often used around repetitive music: flow,
meditation, release, suspension. Those experiences are real, but they belong to
particular musical conditions, not to repetition itself.

Eastman's accumulation creates pressure because several parameters reinforce
one another: density grows; registers fill; attacks compete; harmonic friction
increases; the performers' labor becomes audible; the quoted material brings
history into the room. Use the same additive rule with soft attacks, widely
spaced registers, consonant intervals, and frequent subtraction, and the result
might feel generous or weightless.

The process is not the affect. The process organizes the conditions from which
an affect may arise.

Eastman's organic principle also improves on a simplistic picture of addition.
If a new element enters every eight measures, a piece may become thicker, but
thickness is not growth. Growth requires consequences among the parts. Does the
new layer mask an old one?

Does it force a register change? Does a consonance become a beating cluster?
Does the pulse that once united the ensemble now seem to hold incompatible
activities together? Addition becomes form when it changes the meaning of what
has been retained.

Subtraction deserves equal care. Removing a layer may clear space, but it can
also expose a wound. A bass that seemed merely supportive may be revealed as
the source of harmonic gravity.

A pulse removed after a long span may continue inside the listener, making the
unsynchronized music that follows feel haunted by a coordination no longer
present. Organic form is not a pile. It is a changing account of what the piece
can still remember.

## Chapter 76: The Melody Stays; the Light Changes

Maurice Ravel's *Boléro* offers a severe experiment in moving change away from
melody and harmony. Its snare-drum rhythm persists. Two long melodies alternate
again and again. The tonal center remains fixed for almost the entire piece.
Instead of thematic development in the usual sense, Ravel uses orchestration,
register, instrumental combination, density, and one immense crescendo to
carry the form.

Ravel called the piece an experiment in a very limited direction and, with
characteristic provocation, “orchestral tissue without music.”[^12] The phrase
is entertaining, but it should not persuade us that composition has been
removed.

Once melody, rhythm, and harmony are constrained, the assignment of a melody to
a particular instrument is no longer secondary decoration. It is the event.

At first, a single instrumental color presents the melody against a sparse
field. On later repetitions the line changes body. It may become more nasal,
more fragile, heavier, brighter, or less easily separable from its
surroundings. Doublings create colors that no one instrument owns.

Register and density alter the apparent scale of the tune. Meanwhile the
crescendo changes the physical pressure of every return. The melodic outline
remains familiar enough to serve as a measuring device for the orchestra's
transformation.[^13]

Imagine repainting the walls of a room while leaving every piece of furniture
in place. At first the comparison is helpful: the object remains, the setting
changes.

But *Boléro* eventually exceeds the metaphor because an orchestral color is not
a wall behind a melody. Color changes the melody's edge, weight, and internal
balance. We do not hear tune plus instrumentation. We hear a different
incarnation of the tune.

The piece's late harmonic break has force because so much has been withheld. A
modulation that would be an ordinary event in another work becomes a crack in
the structure.

Repetition establishes not only familiarity but law; the first violation of
that law inherits the weight of every prior obedience.

This is another reason to resist casual variation. If Ravel had changed a few
notes of the melody on each pass to keep it interesting, the orchestral
transformations would be harder to measure and the harmonic rupture would lose
scale. By refusing one kind of development, he enlarges another.

## Chapter 77: Repetition and Care

In 1971, filmmaker Alan Power gave Gavin Bryars unused recordings from a film
about people living rough in London. On one short fragment, an unidentified man
sang a religious line: *Jesus' blood never failed me yet*. Bryars did not meet
him; he had worked on the film as an editor and mixer after the material was
recorded. No image of the singer survives, and attempts to identify either him
or the source of his song have failed.[^14]

The voice is remarkably steady in pitch, but its tempo remains flexible. The
fragment forms a cycle of roughly thirteen bars, about twenty-six seconds long.

Bryars allowed the singer's timing to determine the return and wrote an
accompaniment that gradually grows around it. The uneven phrase is not
straightened to meet a grid. The surrounding music learns how to meet the
phrase.

Bryars has often recalled an incident from the work's beginning. While the
voice was repeating in an art studio at Leicester, he left to get coffee. When
he returned, the normally active room had become subdued. People were moving
slowly; some were weeping.

We have Bryars's memory of the scene, not independent testimony from the
students. It is worth telling as his memory because it clarified an ethical and
compositional fact for him: the fragment already had an emotional integrity. An
accompaniment should attend to it, not rescue it.[^15]

The work's repetition creates intimacy through renewed exposure. We hear the
catch of breath before a word, the way one pitch leans toward another, the
slight mismatch between one cycle and our expectation of it.

These details do not become interesting because the singer is nameless. They
were already the particulars of a person. Repetition gives us time to stop
treating the voice as documentary evidence and begin hearing how he sings.

At the same time, the accompaniment changes the frame. New harmonies can make a
note sound hopeful, wounded, stable, or unresolved. Added instruments enlarge
the public scale of a private fragment. The composer cannot disappear behind
the repetition and claim that the material speaks for itself. Choosing to
repeat is already an act of emphasis; harmonizing is an act of interpretation;
presenting the voice to audiences is an act with consequences.

Repetition can preserve a fragile object, but it can also consume one. The
difference lies partly in whether the surrounding music keeps listening.

In *Jesus' Blood Never Failed Me Yet*, the irregular phrase remains sovereign.
The accompaniment changes the light without trying to straighten the line.

## Chapter 78: A Ground Is Not a Loop

The finale of Brahms's Fourth Symphony is built over an eight-measure pattern
adapted from the closing chaconne of Bach's cantata BWV 150.

Bach scholar Philipp Spitta sent Brahms a copy of the cantata in 1874 and
specifically drew his attention to that movement. Eleven years later, Brahms
used the ground as the basis for thirty variations and a coda.[^16]

The movement belongs in this section as a warning against confusing recurrence
with circularity. The ground keeps returning, but harmony, phrase, orchestral
weight, contrapuntal activity, and dramatic function drive toward irreversible
consequences.

Sometimes the pattern is obvious; sometimes it is absorbed into the surface.
The recurrence is a source of coherence, not a command to remain in place.

A detailed account belongs in a later section on harmonic worlds and large
form.

For now, Brahms gives us one useful question: is the repeating structure the
thing the listener follows, or the thing that allows everything else to travel?

## Chapter 79: Designing the Return

We can now turn these encounters into a set of compositional decisions. They
are not a taxonomy of repetitive music. They are questions that prevent
repetition from becoming an unexamined default.

### 1. What must remain recognizable?

Identify the invariant. It may be a precise sequence of pitches, a contour, a
rhythm, an interval, a registral shape, a harmonic rhythm, a timbre, or a rule
of interaction. If everything is invariant, the listener may have little room
to hear changing relations. If nothing is invariant, variation loses its
reference.

Try stating the identity of the material without mentioning every note: “a
falling minor third followed by two short attacks”; “a widely spaced chord with
one unstable inner voice”; “a breath-length tone answered by a dry click.”

This description reveals which alterations the object can survive. Transpose
the falling third and it remains itself. Reverse its contour and perhaps it
does not. Change the chord's pitches but preserve its spacing, and you may
discover that register was its true identity all along.

### 2. What is the unit, and where is its seam?

Decide whether the listener should recognize a pulse, cell, phrase, cycle, or
larger return.

A unit must last long enough to contain the identity you need and short enough
for that identity to remain available in memory.

Then shape the return. A breath, rest, cadence, accent, or change of register
can expose the seam. Overlap, elision, resonance, and staggered entrances can
soften it.

Do not hide the seam automatically. A hard restart can create the pleasure of
recognition or the unease of entrapment.

### 3. Where will change occur?

Choose among material, relationship, listener, and context. More than one can
change, but give the passage a center of gravity.

If the material changes, decide which parameter carries the process. If the
relationship changes, keep the repeated object clear enough to measure its new
surroundings. If the listener is the main location, resist intervening before
attention has time to reorganize itself. If context matters, compose the
conditions: who plays, who may move, what the room contributes, and how the
work responds to unplanned sound.

### 4. What is the profile of change?

Gradual change is only one option. A process may accumulate steadily, alternate
between two states, remain flat and then rupture, erode irregularly, or circle
through several conditions without establishing a climax.

Draw the profile before writing notes. A straight rising line will reveal the
assumption behind a continuous crescendo. A staircase suggests discrete
additions. A narrowing band might represent a reduction of register.

A line that repeatedly approaches a threshold and falls away may create
anticipation without arrival. The drawing is not the form; it exposes the verb
you have silently assigned to the repetition.

### 5. Who decides when to continue?

The composer can fix the count, as Ravel largely does. Performers can decide
locally, as in *In C*.

A duration can be discovered through breath, audience response, environmental
change, or a condition such as “continue until all players have arrived at the
same figure.”

Every allocation has musical consequences. A fixed count can produce exact
proportion. Performer choice can make listening part of the form.

An environmental condition can admit surprise but may also surrender the
piece's ending to a trivial accident. Giving away a decision does not remove
responsibility for the kind of result that decision can produce.

### 6. Why does the repetition stop?

A repeated passage may fulfill a count, exhaust its material, dissolve, be
interrupted, mutate beyond recognition, or simply be reframed by what comes
next. Each ending interprets the repetition.

If the passage stops at the peak of accumulation, we may hear the process as a
climb. If layers depart one by one, the same process becomes depletion. If the
music cuts off while the pattern remains vigorous, the audible boundary may
feel external, leaving the cycle active in memory. If a repeating figure
gradually becomes another figure, the question is not where it stops but when
we cease to call it the same.

Do not wait until the end to ask this. The possibility of stopping shapes every
return.

## Chapter 80: When the Method Goes Slack

Repetition often fails not because there is too much of it, but because the
composer has not decided what it is for.

The first danger is **repetition as postponement**. A pattern continues because
no next event has been imagined. The remedy is not necessarily to add an event.
Ask what the continued pattern is changing.

If you cannot hear a consequential answer—not in its relationships, the
listener's orientation, or the context— then either give the repetition a job
or let it end.

The second is **process as an alibi**. Steve Reich wrote of wanting a musical
process to be perceptible as it unfolded.[^17] That clarity can be bracing, but
no process exempts a composer from listening to its results.

A rule may be elegant and produce clumsy harmony. An additive scheme may spend
ten minutes approaching a destination that does not reward the attention it
requests. “The system made it” is a description of causation, not a defense of
the music.

The third is **automatic inflation**. Continuous accumulation is easily
mistaken for drama because it gets louder and thicker.

But if each added layer does the same kind of work, the process may become more
crowded without becoming more consequential. Change the relation, not merely
the quantity.

The fourth is **premature rescue**. A composer worries that the listener will
be bored and alters the figure just before it becomes familiar enough for a
deeper hearing.

Repetition asks for nerve. If the material deserves sustained attention, allow
it to establish the scale on which small changes matter.

The fifth is **single-scale regularity**. If the attack, phrase, harmony,
density, and section all renew on the same boundary, the piece may reveal its
boxes too completely. Let one level cross another. A regular pulse can support
an irregular phrase; a fixed cell can inhabit a harmonic cycle of another
length; a long dynamic arc can pass through many local returns.

The last is **preassigned feeling**. Repetition is not inherently peaceful,
hypnotic, ritualistic, impersonal, ecstatic, or oppressive. Those words name
responses to tempo, touch, harmony, loudness, register, duration, embodiment,
and circumstance.

Mitchell and Riley both organize repeated cells, but the Willisau *Nonaah* and
a convivial performance of *In C* do not make the same social or emotional
proposition. A method acquires character through the conditions we give it.

## Chapter 81: Try It

### Notice: Ten Returns

Choose a phrase that lasts between three and seven seconds. It should be clear
enough that you can repeat it without consulting notation.

Play it ten times with no intentional variation. Leave the same amount of
silence between each statement.

Do not correct small performance differences. Listen instead for changes of
function:

- When does the phrase become recognizable as the unit?
- When do you begin anticipating its first attack?
- Does attention move from contour to rhythm, harmony, touch, or resonance?
- Does the silence after the phrase change character?
- Does one accidental difference seem to become structural because you are
  waiting for it to recur?

After the tenth statement, omit the eleventh but preserve the silence in which
it would have begun. The exercise is complete when you can hear an absent
repetition.

Now repeat the study on another day. Do not expect the same thresholds. This is
not an experiment designed to prove a universal count. It is practice in
hearing the listener as one location of change.

### Isolate: Calm and Pressure

Use one cell to make two short studies. Keep its pitches and order fixed.

In the first study, use repetition to reduce pressure. You may alter spacing,
register, articulation, dynamics, resonance, and surrounding silence.

Avoid relying on a continuous fade. Ask instead what allows the figure to
require less vigilance while remaining alive.

In the second, make the same cell increase pressure. Again, do not change its
pitches. You might shorten the silence before each return, compress its
register, harden its attacks, add competing accents, place dissonant harmony
beneath it, or make the performer sustain one note near the edge of a breath.

Compare the results. Which parameters carried the affect? If the calm version
depended only on softness and the pressured version only on loudness, exchange
their dynamics and solve the problem again.

### Complicate: A Repeating Condition

Write five to nine short modules for three or more performers. Give everyone
the same ordered collection. Each player may repeat a module or move to the
next, but define:

1. one shared reference, such as a pulse, drone, breath cue, or recurring
   signal;
2. one proximity rule that prevents the ensemble from becoming unrelated;
3. one behavior that performers must listen for before advancing; and
4. one clear condition for ending.

Rehearse the piece once. Then ask each player which decision felt genuinely
musical and which felt merely permitted.

Revise the rules, not by adding instructions wherever something went wrong, but
by clarifying what the group's freedom is meant to reveal.

The purpose is not to write another *In C*. It is to discover how repetition
can give performers time to hear and responsibility for what happens next.

## Chapter 82: The Phrase Returns

At Willisau, Roscoe Mitchell did not repeat the opening of *Nonaah* because he
had nowhere else to go.

He repeated it because the piece could not honestly go elsewhere yet. The room
had become part of the form, and the relation between the phrase and that room
was unfinished.

By holding the phrase in place, he allowed several kinds of change to gather
around it. The material flexed without losing identity. Breath turned a final
note into visible effort. The audience's interruptions became spaces for
response.

Hostility became attention; attention became participation. When the phrase
returned near its original shape after sixty-two statements, it was not a
reset. It carried seven minutes of consequence.

Repetition is sometimes described as the refusal of time. Mitchell's
performance suggests the opposite.

Repetition can make time answerable. It lets us hear what each return has
inherited and what, despite returning, can no longer be the same.

There is another possibility. Two musicians may begin by repeating together
and, through the smallest difference of pace, gradually cease to agree about
where the pattern begins. Neither abandons the material. The relation itself
starts to move.

That is no longer only the problem of repetition. It is the beginning of drift.
