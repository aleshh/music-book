# Chapter 1: Attend

## Composing the Listener's Notice

In March 1920, an audience entered the Galerie Barbazanges in Paris for a play
by Max Jacob. Between the acts, a small group of musicians stationed around the
room began playing music by Erik Satie and Darius Milhaud. The audience had
received unusually clear instructions: keep talking, keep moving, and pay no
attention to the music. It was meant to mix with the room's other activity, as
light, heat, and furniture did.

The audience stopped talking and listened.

Satie reportedly moved among the guests urging them to resume their
conversations. His assurances only made the situation stranger. A composer was
asking listeners not to listen, and the listeners—trained by a lifetime of
concert behavior—were refusing to comply.[^1]

The story is funny because Satie's failure was so complete. It is useful because
the notes were not necessarily what failed. A century of performances, lectures,
and recordings has made it tempting to treat *musique d'ameublement*, or
“furniture music,” as a prophetic genre: an early announcement of ambient music
before ambient music had a name. That turns a live problem into a tidy origin
story. Satie and Milhaud made only a small group of such pieces, in circumstances
that are not always clear, and those experiments do not supply a complete theory
of musical background.[^2] What the gallery episode gives us is better than a
theory. It gives us evidence that attention is not contained in the music.

The guests heard musicians begin. They recognized the social frame of a
performance and supplied the behavior that frame usually required. The title,
the placement of the players, and Satie's instructions proposed another kind of
relationship, but the room overruled him. The audience did not merely listen to
the piece. It helped decide what kind of piece could occur.

Composers often speak as if attention were something a work possesses. A piece
is “attention-grabbing,” “immersive,” or “ignorable.” These descriptions can be
convenient, but they hide the relation that produces them. The same sustained
chord can be an object of close analysis in a quiet hall, an atmosphere at
dinner, an obstruction in a hospital corridor, or a barely registered color
behind an absorbing conversation. The sound matters. So do the listener's
activity, the room, the volume, the duration, the expectations attached to the
occasion, and whether anyone can leave.

Attention is not a substance poured into music. It is a changing relation among
sound, listener, activity, and setting. That relation can be composed, but it
cannot be commanded.

This is where an account of ambient and minimalist practice should begin. Not
with slowness, repetition, or a particular collection of instruments, but with
a question: *How does this music ask to be noticed?*

## Attention Is Wider Than a Spotlight

Imagine a room with an open window. Someone nearby speaks your name. A bus
brakes in the street. A refrigerator hums. Farther away, a child strikes the
same piano key several times and then stops.

If we describe attention as a spotlight, only one of these sounds can stand at
the center. The image is useful when we need to discuss focus: your name may
pull the beam away from the bus. But listening is often wider and less orderly
than that. You can follow the meaning of a sentence while registering that the
refrigerator has stopped. You can notice the room become quieter without
knowing immediately which sound has vanished. You can move deliberately from
one layer to another, or hold several in a broad field without making any one of
them the permanent subject.

For compositional purposes, it helps to distinguish three widths of attention.

**Focal listening** follows an event or relation closely. We track a melody,
wait for a cadence, compare two successive chords, or listen to the grain of a
single tone. Focus does not require loudness. In a sparse field, the quietest
sound may receive the closest attention.

**Peripheral listening** registers a continuing condition without monitoring
each event. We know that a pulse continues while we follow a voice above it. We
remain aware of a low harmony without counting its attacks. The layer has not
disappeared; it has become the field against which something else can be
measured.

**Distributed listening** moves among several layers, or between music and its
surroundings, without assigning one permanent foreground. This is not vague
attention. It may be extremely exact. An improviser can hear her own line, the
ensemble's total density, a distant percussionist, and the audience's restlessness
as different parts of one situation. She does not hear all of them with equal
detail at every instant. She changes scale.

These are not psychological boxes into which listeners can be sorted. They are
ways of asking what a passage permits. A piece may invite close focus for ten
seconds, release it for several minutes, and then make a previously peripheral
tone suddenly consequential. Another may encourage continuous distributed
listening. A third may make the strain of maintaining focus its subject.

We also need a second distinction. **Event listening** asks, *What happened?*
A note entered; the chord changed; a rhythm was interrupted; an expected return
failed to occur. **State listening** asks, *What continues, and what is it like
to be inside it?* A harmony persists; several tones beat against one another; a
texture maintains its density; a register remains open.

Event and state are not opposites. A state begins as an event. Remain inside it
long enough and minute events appear: a breath, an overtone, a small imbalance
between players. Conversely, a rapid series of events may fuse into a state, as
separate attacks become a tremor or band of sound. The composer's decision is
not simply whether to write events or states. It is which scale of listening
makes each one audible.

This is why apparently uneventful music can require active attention, and busy
music can become environmental. A dense stream that never changes its manner
may recede. A single quiet note after a long absence may reorganize a room.

## What Calls the Ear

Place a soft, evenly articulated figure in the middle register: A, E, A, E,
with no accent and no large gap between repetitions. After it has continued for
a while, add a high B once.

The B need not be louder than the figure. Its register, rarity, and novelty may
be enough to bring it forward. If it returns regularly every four beats, it
will soon become part of the pattern. If it returns at irregular but not wholly
unpredictable intervals, the listener may begin waiting for it. If it sounds
only once, the ear may keep the space of its possible return open long after
the pitch has gone.

This is **relative salience**: the power of a sound to attract notice because of
its relation to a field. No musical feature guarantees salience by itself.
Loudness can attract attention, but a loud continuous layer may recede behind a
rare whisper. A high note often separates from a middle-register texture, but
not if the music has taught us that high notes are ordinary and a low one is
the exception. Dissonance can press forward in a stable consonant field; in a
rough field, a plain unison may do the same.

Several variables commonly attract the ear:

- **Onset.** A beginning cuts into continuity. The sharper the attack, the more
  clearly it marks a before and after.
- **Contrast.** A change of register, articulation, dynamic, harmony, rhythm, or
  color distinguishes an event from its surroundings.
- **Rarity.** An event that occurs once or infrequently can acquire more weight
  than a busier layer.
- **Displacement.** A sound arriving earlier, later, or between established
  pulses can disturb the current temporal picture.
- **Implication.** A note that seems to require continuation—a leading tone, an
  unfinished contour, a suspended dissonance—can hold attention through what it
  makes us await.
- **Change of density.** One voice entering a bare field or one voice remaining
  after a crowded texture clears can become prominent without changing its own
  volume.

This is not a recipe for grabbing attention. It is a list of relations to test.
A familiar listener may hear harmonic implication where another hears color.
A performer may make a normally inconspicuous note vivid through touch. The
same offbeat entrance that wakes one passage may be routine in another.

Composers also shape the release of attention. Continuity allows the ear to
stop marking every onset. Predictable recurrence lowers the cost of missing an
instance. Similar registers and blended articulations make it harder to isolate
one strand. Stable dynamics, elided phrase boundaries, and gestures that feel
locally complete can let a listener look away and later return without needing
to reconstruct a broken argument.

Release does not mean removing musical life. It means declining to make every
instant a checkpoint.

Suppose the A–E figure continues while the high B returns several times. If you
want the B to move from foreground toward background, you could make it more
regular. You could let another high tone join it, reducing its singularity. You
could narrow the registral distance between it and the figure. Or you could
leave the B unchanged and introduce one low, dry attack beneath everything.
The foreground can move because something else has become rarer.

This suggests a more useful compositional question than *How do I make this
sound prominent?* Ask instead: *Prominent relative to what, for how long, and
what will release it?*

## A Frame in Three Movements

On August 29, 1952, at the Maverick Concert Hall near Woodstock, New York,
pianist David Tudor sat at a piano to perform a new piece by John Cage. Tudor
closed the keyboard lid, used a stopwatch, and marked the ends of three
movements by opening and closing the lid. He played no intentional piano sound.
The work was *4'33"*.[^3]

Cage later remembered wind outside during the first movement, rain on the roof
during the second, and the audience's own sounds in the third.[^4] The details
come to us through recollection, and the work had several intellectual and
musical sources; it should not be reduced to a single revelation in an anechoic
chamber.[^5] What matters here is the function of the frame.

The sounds at Maverick were not remarkable because no one had previously heard
weather. They became remarkable because Tudor's actions established a bounded
occasion for hearing them. The lid closed: a movement had begun. The lid
opened: something had ended. The frame did not cause wind or rain, and it did
not erase their ordinary meanings. It changed their relations. A cough could
be heard not merely as a cough but as an interruption, contribution, symptom
of impatience, or formal event. Rain acquired an entrance because the movement
was already under way when it began.

Silence, in this sense, is not an empty material that the composer places in a
score. It is a request to hear what intentional sound normally covers or
excludes. That request can be shaped with great precision. Give the silent span
three movements and we compare them. Put it in a concert hall and the social
discipline of the hall becomes audible. Ask a performer to mark time and
inaction becomes an action we watch.

But the frame does not make every sound permanently or equally music. Outside
the performance, the same rain may again become weather, annoyance, or nothing
we consciously notice. Inside it, one listener may attend to the roof while
another watches Tudor's hands. The work offers a relation; listeners complete
it differently.

This makes *4'33"* nearly the inverse of Satie's gallery experiment. Satie and
Milhaud supplied intentional notes and asked that they recede into social
activity. Cage withheld intentional performance sound and used the concert
frame to bring incidental sound forward. Satie tried to weaken the behavioral
force of the event. Cage made that force the instrument.

The contrast matters because it shows that foreground and background cannot be
identified by musical substance alone. Quietness did not make Satie's music
background, and the absence of played notes did not make Cage's work empty.
Each depended on an agreement about where attention belonged. One agreement
failed. The other made the difficulty of the agreement audible.

For a composer, framing can be as consequential as orchestration. A title can
direct the listener toward a process. A visible entrance can make the faintest
sound feel deliberate. A pause after applause can preserve a performance frame
even when no one plays. Conversely, music that should remain available to
conversation may be defeated by a ceremonial beginning, fixed seating, and the
social authority of a stage.

Instructions help, but they do not float above custom. Satie discovered that
asking people not to listen may only focus them more intensely. Every whispered
“ignore this” contains a small trumpet fanfare.

## The Harp and the Rain

In 1975, while recovering from an accident, Brian Eno was visited by his friend
Judy Nylon, who brought a recording of eighteenth-century harp music. After she
left, the music played so quietly that it mingled with rain outside. Eno later
remembered being unable or unwilling to rise and adjust it, and hearing the
harp less as a sequence to follow than as one element in a larger field.[^6]

Nylon later recalled the situation differently: in her account, she
deliberately balanced the harp against the rain.[^7] The disagreement is worth
preserving. The prettiest version is an origin myth in which chance teaches an
artist a new genre while he lies helpless in bed. Nylon's version restores a
second listener and a purposeful act. In either case, the musical discovery is
the same relation: composed sound did not have to defeat the environment to
remain present within it.

Three years later, in the notes to *Ambient 1: Music for Airports*, Eno proposed
that ambient music should support several degrees of listening attention. His
compact phrase—“as ignorable as it is interesting”—has become famous enough to
sound like a slogan.[^8] It is more useful as a compositional test.

To be ignorable in this sense is not to be negligible. Negligible music offers
little when attention arrives. Permissive music offers detail without making
continuous surveillance the price of entry. It can be heard closely, heard at
the edge of another activity, left, and encountered again.

Listen to the recurrent piano and wordless vocal sonorities of *Music for
Airports*.[^9] Individual sounds have soft boundaries. They do not usually
announce themselves as indispensable steps in a tightly chained argument. A
listener who misses one entrance has not missed the clue required to understand
the next. The gestures are locally sufficient: a sonority can be received as a
complete occurrence even when its larger pattern is not being followed.

This does not mean nothing matters. Register, spacing, recurrence, and the
length of the gaps determine the field. A low tone can change the weight of a
high chord. The return of a voice can make the intervening piano sound newly
solitary. Long intervals between related sonorities permit the environment to
enter, but they also create memory: when something returns, we may recognize it
without having waited for it.

Music that tolerates incomplete attention often has several related qualities.
Its continuity is recoverable. Its boundaries do not all demand to be counted.
Important relations recur or persist long enough to be found from more than one
entrance point. Local gestures reward notice without holding later events
hostage. None of these qualities is mandatory, and each can support many
characters: tender, severe, bright, mournful, comic, uneasy. Permission is not
a mood.

Nor is low information automatically permission. A single high tone repeated
at irregular intervals may monopolize a listener more fiercely than a generous
polyphony. Extreme quiet can compel attention if listeners must strain to hear
whether anything is happening. A long unresolved dissonance can occupy the
mind precisely because so little changes. Sparse music may be restful, but it
may also behave like someone who pauses in the middle of an alarming sentence.

The essential question is not how much material the passage contains. It is
what missing part of it costs.

## Writing for Departure and Return

Imagine a listener leaving the music for thirty seconds—not necessarily by
walking out, but by answering a question, reading a departure board, watching a
dancer, or following a thought. When attention returns, what is available?

There are at least three useful answers.

The listener may find **continuity**. The same condition remains: a harmony, a
pulse, a registral field, a family of gestures. The missed interval does not
need to be reconstructed. This is the clearest form of permission.

The listener may find **evidence of change**. The texture is recognizably the
same, but one layer has accumulated, the register has opened, or a once rare
sound has become common. The absent listener has missed events but can infer a
process. Returning becomes a way of measuring change.

The listener may find **a new state**. The music has crossed a threshold. The
return does not erase the loss; it makes that loss part of the experience. A
new chord has arrived, a pulse has disappeared, or an ensemble has dispersed.
This can be entirely appropriate. Music that permits departure need not promise
that nothing important will happen while we are gone.

These answers describe an **attentional profile** across a form. A composer can
summon, hold, release, redirect, and welcome attention back. The profile need
not stay level. In fact, a work that is equally reticent at every moment may
flatten its own distinctions. A rare event acquires force partly because the
music has not insisted on every preceding instant.

To summon attention, introduce a relation that asks to be resolved or compared.
To hold it, let that relation continue changing at a scale the listener can
track. To release it, complete the local question, establish continuity, or
make recurrence trustworthy. To redirect it, alter the relationship between
layers rather than simply adding a louder object. To welcome it back, provide a
recognizable condition with evidence of where the music has gone.

This vocabulary should not become stage directions for the listener's mind.
No composer knows precisely when another person will wander or return. The
profile is an account of invitations and consequences, not inner control.

The distinction becomes especially important in ensemble music. A player may
need to maintain focal attention to cues while a listener is free to drift. Or
the players may distribute attention across the whole group so that no one line
acts as permanent leader. What sounds effortless to an audience can require
alert, difficult listening onstage. Background is a role, not an absence of
labor.

## The Whole Field and One Distant Voice

Pauline Oliveros made this movement of attention into an explicit practice. In
her writing on Deep Listening, she distinguished involuntary hearing from
conscious listening and asked practitioners to move between focal and global
attention: narrowing toward one sound, then opening toward the whole field.[^10]
The aim was not to remain indefinitely diffuse. Breadth and focus could correct
and strengthen one another.

Her *Tuning Meditation* makes that alternation social. Singers listen to the
entire field. A participant may choose a distant voice and match its pitch, or
may contribute a tone not currently present. Each action requires a different
width of notice. To find an unoccupied pitch, one must hear the group broadly.
To match a distant voice, one must select and follow. Upon singing, the listener
also changes the field everyone else is hearing.[^11]

This is distributed attention with consequences. The whole is not an anonymous
wash. It is made by distinct choices, each of which depends on listening beyond
oneself. A singer who contributes a new pitch increases the available harmonic
field. Another who matches it changes its social and acoustical weight. The
same tone can move from solitary event to shared state without becoming louder.

Oliveros's example also corrects a common assumption about attentive music: that
the highest form of listening is fixed concentration on one object. Sometimes
close focus is necessary. Sometimes it narrows the musical world until only the
thing already selected can be heard. An improvising group can fail because each
player listens too little, but it can also fail because everyone attends so
intently to one prominent voice that the quieter relations disappear.

For a composer, distributed attention can be supported by giving layers
different kinds of legibility. One may have a clear contour, another a stable
register, another an identifiable articulation. If all layers compete through
the same means, the listener is forced to rank them. If each establishes a
different identity, attention can move among them without losing the whole.

The issue is not maximum separation. Strongly blended layers can invite
distributed listening too, especially when their small internal differences
keep changing the total color. The fuller theory of density and masking belongs
to a later chapter. Here the point is simpler: attention can widen because the
music offers more than one meaningful scale.

## Foreground Is a Job

Foreground and background are often treated as places. The melody sits in
front; the accompaniment sits behind. But their musical roles can change while
their notes and volume remain nearly fixed.

Suppose a viola sustains G and D while a flute moves above it. At first the
viola establishes a harmonic field and the flute provides events. Then the
flute settles on a repeated pitch. The viola continues exactly as before, but a
small wavering between its two notes begins to matter. What was background has
become the source of change. The flute remains audible, yet it now provides a
reference against which the viola is heard.

Foreground is therefore a job performed within a relation. It may carry
direction, provide the rare event, disturb a state, or supply the detail by
which change is measured. Background may sustain continuity, but it may also
contain latent events waiting for attention to reach them.

This is good news for composers who worry that a quiet passage needs a new
melody every time attention begins to loosen. Often the passage needs a change
of role, not more material. Remove one layer and the remaining one becomes
exposed. Hold a formerly active line and another layer's rhythm comes forward.
Change the register of an accompaniment and the same melody acquires distance.
The attention moves because the relations move.

It is also possible to compose a foreground without force. Rather than making
one line louder, make it rarer. Rather than sharpening every attack, allow a
single articulation to differ. Rather than crowding a cadence with harmonic
signals, leave one pitch unresolved after the rest of the field settles.
Listeners are sensitive to exceptions. The less often a passage points, the
more consequential one pointing gesture can become.

The reverse is equally valuable: a foreground can relinquish its job. A melody
may gradually shorten until it becomes one color among others. A distinctive
attack can recur until its novelty is spent. A solo voice can settle on a tone
the ensemble already holds. Attention is released not because the material
vanishes, but because its claim changes.

## The Listener Brings a Past

Relative salience does not begin from zero. Listeners arrive with learned
expectations about melody, tuning, rhythm, instruments, language, venue, and
genre. A dominant chord may sound like an urgent implication to someone steeped
in tonal harmony and like one color among others to someone following the
percussion. A quotation can seize the attention of a listener who recognizes
it while passing unnoticed by everyone else. The opening of a concert piece may
sound like a beginning because people have learned when to become quiet.

This does not make composition hopelessly subjective. It makes the listener's
past one of the conditions under which a choice operates. We already compose
with such conditions whenever we rely on a cadence, a recurring groove, a
familiar instrumental role, or the weight of a sung word. The trouble begins
when one learned response is mistaken for a property of the notes themselves.

Consider a gradual change in tuning. One listener follows the changing interval
as a directional event: the pitches are moving toward alignment. Another hears
the same passage chiefly as a change of roughness. A performer may attend to
the bodily adjustment needed to maintain the relation. These hearings are not
arbitrary. Each selects different evidence from the same sounding process.

A composer can work with several hearings at once. Make the changing interval
part of a stable rhythmic field and it may offer harmonic direction to one
listener, changing color to another, and a reliable pulse to a third. The aim
is not to design a response for every possible person. It is to avoid making
the passage depend unknowingly on a single kind of expertise.

When possible, test attentional assumptions with other listeners. Do not ask
only whether they liked the passage. Ask what first drew notice, what receded,
what seemed to continue when attention wandered, and what they were waiting
for. A surprising answer is useful evidence. If everyone follows a high voice
you intended as atmosphere, perhaps register is doing more than dynamics. If a
rare low attack goes unnoticed, perhaps the surrounding harmony has not made it
consequential. If trained performers and new listeners hear different
boundaries, the piece may contain two viable forms.

Familiarity also changes attention over repeated hearings. A first encounter
may be dominated by novelty. Later, the listener knows the large events and can
hear into their construction. Or the reverse occurs: a once vivid gesture loses
salience, revealing that it depended entirely on surprise. This is not a reason
to write music that reveals all its value immediately. It is a reason to ask
what remains available after recognition has done its first work.

Attention has a biography. A composition meets it partway.

## When Attention Becomes Coercion

It is easy for composers to romanticize released attention. We imagine music
generously coexisting with work, travel, conversation, sleep, or architecture.
But background music is not always chosen by the people who hear it. A passage
that seems discreet in the studio may become an inescapable demand when it is
repeated all day in someone else's workplace. Quiet sound still occupies space.
Regularity can soothe one person and exhaust another. A narrow register may
recede for one listener and cause physical distress for another.

This is not an argument against music in shared places. It is an argument for
treating the listening situation as part of composition. Who has chosen to be
there? What else must they do? Can they move away, stop the sound, speak over
it, or alter their attention without penalty? How long will performers and
workers inhabit the condition? The answers do not determine the notes, but they
change what those notes do.

The same caution applies to focused listening. A composer may build a work that
rewards extraordinary concentration, but cannot prescribe awe, stillness, or
transformation. Silence does not make an audience contemplative by itself. It
may reveal a room, intensify embarrassment, invite rest, or produce impatience.
A claim that listeners *must* enter a certain state often protects the work from
the evidence of actual listeners.

Nor should wandering attention be treated as a failure of character. Sometimes
the music has stopped giving attention anything to do. Sometimes a listener is
tired, worried, unfamiliar with the practice, or attending to something more
urgent. Sometimes drifting is the intended mode. The composer's responsibility
is not to blame attention into obedience, but to understand the invitations the
piece extends and the pressures it applies.

Filling every moment is one response to the fear of losing a listener. It is
usually a costly one. When every entrance is emphasized, no entrance is rare.
When each chord announces the next, the listener has no permission to inhabit
the present one. Constant capture exhausts the very attention it seeks.

Better to decide what kind of absence the music can tolerate. Can a listener
miss one attack, one phrase, one minute? Is the loss recoverable? If not, is the
irreversibility essential to the piece or merely an unexamined habit? These are
formal questions, but also gestures of hospitality.

## Three Experiments

### 1. Three Widths of Attention

Choose a place where you can hear at least five distinct sounds. Spend one
minute following a single sound as closely as possible. Notice its internal
changes, interruptions, and relation to silence. Spend the next minute hearing
the widest field you can manage without naming its parts. In the third minute,
move deliberately between one sound and the whole.

Then write a short passage that asks performers or listeners to make the same
three changes of width. Do not label the sections *focus*, *global*, and
*distributed*. Achieve the movement musically. You might isolate one register,
broaden the range, then let distinct layers remain simultaneously legible. Or
you might begin with one clear pulse, surround it with unmeasured tones, and
eventually remove the pulse without removing coordination.

The result need not be quiet. The question is whether the width of listening
changes.

### 2. Foreground Without Force

Write a texture of three continuing layers. Give them similar dynamics. Make
each one distinct through some other feature: register, articulation, rate of
movement, harmonic function, or rarity.

Now bring each layer to the foreground once without making it louder. Change as
little as possible. A rest in the other layers may be enough. So may one
unexpected pitch, a new placement against the pulse, or the first long tone in
a field of short ones.

Afterward, remove any cue that merely announces, “Now listen here.” Preserve
only the relation that made the change audible.

### 3. Permit the Exit

Compose three minutes of music. Design it so that a listener may miss any
thirty-second interval and still return to something intelligible.

This does not require an unchanging field. Choose what the return will offer:
continuity, evidence of change, or a clearly established new state. Then make a
second version in which one thirty-second interval is genuinely indispensable.
Compare the two. What creates the cost of absence: a unique event, a causal
sequence, a change of rule, an unrepeated harmony?

The exercise is not a contest between generosity and rigor. It reveals where
your form asks for continuous witness, and whether that demand is intentional.

## The Audience Refuses

Return to the Galerie Barbazanges. The musicians are distributed around the
room. The guests have been told to talk, move, and treat the music as part of
the furnishing. Instead, they become quiet. Satie crosses the gallery trying to
repair the intended relation with words.

It would be easy to conclude that the audience simply misunderstood the piece.
But misunderstanding is not outside musical life. Their behavior reveals the
strength of another composition already in progress: the arrangement of
players and listeners, the convention of beginning and ending, the etiquette
that turns sounding instruments into an object of attention. Satie had written
notes against a social score and discovered that the social score was louder.

The lesson is not that background music is impossible, nor that listeners must
be allowed to do anything at any time. It is that the composer's material
includes cues about notice: contrast and continuity, rarity and recurrence,
the sharpness of boundaries, the position of performers, the listener's other
activities, and the consequence of looking away. Music can call attention,
hold it, divide it, release it, or make its wandering audible. It cannot own
it.

So before asking what notes should begin a piece, we might ask a quieter
question. When the first sound enters the room, what does it permit the listener
to do?

Once attention settles—or fails to settle—another condition becomes audible.
Some sounds pass quickly; others seem to suspend the clock. A repeated event
can shorten an hour or make a minute feel enormous. Attention tells us where we
are listening. Duration will tell us what it means to remain.
