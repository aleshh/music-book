# Section 2: Experience time

## 13. Making duration perceptible

About three months before a concert in 1999, four packages arrived for the
members of the FLUX Quartet. Each contained a thick, bound, oversized score.
The music was Morton Feldman's *String Quartet II*, written sixteen years
earlier. The current published score runs to 130 large pages. Depending on the
performance, the work may last between three and a half and more than six
hours.[^1]

The packages announced the problem before a note sounded. A quartet rehearsing
a conventional movement can hold its broad route in memory: the opening theme,
the change of key, a turbulent middle, a return.

In Feldman's second quartet, a cell may repeat thirteen times, a section may
continue for fifteen minutes, and recognizable material may return after an
hour.[^2] The music exceeds the scale at which formal memory usually feels
dependable.

FLUX gave a landmark complete performance at Cooper Union in New York. Earlier
performances by the Kronos Quartet, which had premiered the work in Toronto in
1983, used cuts, and Feldman did not live to hear the entire score
realized.[^3]

The distinction between a premiere and a complete performance is already a
lesson in duration. The piece existed on paper, had been heard in public, and
had entered musical history. Yet part of its central proposition—the experience
of carrying its whole span—remained untested.

We often treat duration as a measurement made before composition begins. This
piece will be twelve minutes; that one will fill a concert half. Then we put
music inside the selected container. Feldman's quartet resists that picture.
Its duration does not merely surround the music.

Duration changes what a motif can mean, what a listener can remember, how a
performer uses the body, and what an institution must provide. A six-hour
quartet is not a conventional quartet with extra minutes attached. It is a
different scale of musical life.

This section asks how such scale becomes perceptible. The answer is not simply
to write slowly, sparsely, or at great length. A long piece can feel hurried. A
short piece can make each second heavy.

A dense passage may fly by while leaving many landmarks in memory; a sparse
passage may feel immense while sounding and then collapse afterward into one
undivided field. Tempo, length, event rate, rate of change, felt passage, and
remembered span are related. They are not the same thing.

Duration is not an empty container. Music gives us clocks.

Once we hear those clocks separately, duration becomes something we can shape
rather than merely endure.

## 14. Six hours is not a tempo

Set a metronomic pulse aside for the moment and imagine three passages, each
exactly one minute long.

In the first, a pianist plays sixty evenly spaced notes. In the second, she
plays three notes: one at the beginning, one somewhere near the middle, and one
near the end. In the third, she sustains a chord while its balance changes so
gradually that no single instant feels like the change.

The passages have equal clock duration. They do not give the listener equal
time. The first supplies a frequent unit. Even without counting, we can feel
the regularity and estimate our position.

The second turns each attack into a landmark and each silence into an interval
whose length must be judged against the others. The third weakens the
distinction between event and interval. If no clear boundary marks progress, we
may know that time has passed chiefly because the chord is no longer what it
was.

Now alter only the tempo of the first passage. At thirty notes per minute, the
pulse is slower, but the passage is not longer. Double its length while keeping
the original pulse and it becomes longer without becoming slower.

Remove every other attack and the event rate changes; whether the remaining
notes feel like the same pulse at a slower tempo or a different grouping
depends on accent and memory. Keep all sixty attacks but change their harmony
only once, and the rate of harmonic change is slower than the rate of
articulation.

These distinctions are basic, but discussions of minimalist and ambient music
regularly collapse them.

Long becomes slow, slow becomes sparse, sparse becomes quiet, and quiet becomes
profound. None of those implications is secure.

**Length** is measurable clock time. **Tempo** is the rate of an inferred beat
or performed pulse. **Event rate** is how frequently something begins or
becomes newly distinct. **Rate of change** is how quickly a musical condition
becomes different. A tremolo may have a high event rate and almost no change of
state. One chord entering after four minutes may create a low event rate but a
large change. A slow tempo can contain rapid ornament. A long piece can cross
many states or remain inside one.

The distinctions give composers more room. If a passage feels stalled, the
answer need not be a faster tempo. Perhaps its events are too similar to divide
time. If it feels crowded, the answer need not be fewer notes.

Perhaps the harmonic state changes with every articulation, making each note
carry too much formal weight. If a desired long span feels inflated, the
problem may be that the material still speaks in short units whose boundaries
have merely been stretched apart.

Length is a condition. Musical scale is the network of relations that makes
that length consequential.

A long work earns its scale when small relations begin to do work that shorter
time could not give them.

## 15. The clocks music gives us

A clock does two things: it supplies a recurring unit, and it lets us compare a
present interval with that unit. Music can provide many such measures.

A **pulse** is the most obvious. Regular attacks make time countable even when
we do not count consciously.

**Meter** groups those pulses, giving some positions greater weight and making
return occur at more than one scale. A three-beat measure inside a four-measure
phrase provides both a near clock and a larger one.

**Breath** supplies another clock. A wind player's phrase may last as long as
one exhalation, but the listener need not know the exact capacity of the lungs
to hear effort approach a boundary.

A singer's renewed breath can divide a line even when no rest is written.
String players and pianists breathe too, though their sound need not stop when
the lungs demand it. The mismatch can itself shape time.

**Decay** measures duration by loss. Strike a bell or piano string and the
sound's changing body tells us how long it has lived. Another attack before the
first has faded overlaps one clock with the next.

Waiting until near-silence makes decay a phrase. In a dry room, the same
interval may feel empty sooner; in a resonant one, the departing sound
continues to occupy it.

A **phrase** supplies a clock through contour and implication. We sense a
beginning, continuation, and some degree of completion. The phrase need not be
periodic. Once we have heard its manner, we compare later spans with it: this
one was cut short; that one extended beyond its usual breath.

**Harmonic rhythm** is the rate at which harmonies change function or identity.
A surface can be busy above a harmony that remains stable for minutes.

Another passage may use whole notes while the harmonic relation changes at
every attack. The notated rhythm looks slower in the second passage; harmonic
time moves faster.

**Recurrence** measures distance through memory. When an event returns, it
draws a line to its prior appearance.

The fuller theory of repetition belongs elsewhere, but here its temporal
function matters: a return can tell us how far we have traveled even when no
steady pulse was present.

Finally, the **body** keeps time. A bow changes direction. A hand tires. A
standing listener shifts weight.

A player turns a page, and the accumulation of pages becomes a coarse measure
of progress. These clocks are not incidental to the musical one. They are among
the means by which duration is lived.

A piece can align its clocks. Pulse, harmonic change, breath, phrase, and
recurrence may all arrive at the same boundary, producing a strong sense that
one unit has ended and another begun. It can separate them: the harmony changes
across the bar, a phrase continues past the breath, or a recurring sonority
returns at an unfamiliar point. It can remove clocks one by one until only
decay or bodily effort remains.

Before extending a passage, ask which clock the listener will use. “Very slow”
is not an answer unless something makes slowness audible.

Choose the clock, and slowness becomes a compositional action rather than a
number.

## 16. Midnight

At the 1983 premiere, Kronos brought an ordinary watch onstage. The performance
was being broadcast by the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation, and the broadcast
had to finish at midnight, when “O Canada” was scheduled to begin.

David Harrington later remembered the players casting worried glances at the
watch and accelerating as the hours passed. They finished in just under four
hours.[^12]

The scene would be merely comic if it did not expose a central tension. The
quartet proposes one scale; the broadcast schedule imposes another.

A tempo that appears as a musical instruction on the page becomes flexible
under the pressure of a national clock. The performers are not only realizing
duration. They are negotiating between incompatible kinds of time.

Every performance contains such negotiations, though most are less visible. A
concert must end before the building closes. A dancer's entrance waits on a
breath. A ceremony has a fixed sequence. A listener knows when the last train
leaves.

These facts do not automatically damage a work. They become part of its
temporal meaning. A ten-minute piece played when everyone expected five feels
different from the same piece in an open afternoon.

External clocks can also be composed with. A work may begin at sunset, share a
span with a meal, allow performers to rotate, or fit inside a repeated daily
activity.

The point is not to make every piece site-specific. It is to notice that a
desired duration always meets durations already in progress.

At Toronto, the watch did more than measure the quartet. It changed the speed
of the music it was meant to measure. A clock is never entirely outside a
performance once someone must obey it.

The surrounding clock can become counterpoint, resistance, or part of the
score.

## 17. Passing time and remembered time

After a journey, we sometimes say that it went quickly and then recount a dozen
distinct episodes. Another afternoon seemed endless while it occurred but
leaves almost no internal divisions in memory. Musical time can separate in a
similar way.

There is the time we anticipate before an event, the time we feel passing while
music sounds, and the span we reconstruct afterward. Research on experienced
musical time suggests that grouping, event density, attention, expectation, and
bodily or emotional response all matter, but not as a simple formula.[^4]

More events do not automatically make time feel faster or slower. Their
distinctness, organization, and relation to what we expect are equally
important.

Suppose one minute contains twelve clearly different gestures. While listening,
the succession may feel quick because each event replaces the last before we
have fully entered it. Afterward, those gestures provide landmarks; the minute
may seem full.

Another minute contains one continuous sonority. It may feel slow because we
wait for a boundary that does not arrive. In memory, it may compress into “the
held chord,” a single object with little internal chronology.

Reverse either result by changing the listener's task. If the continuous
sonority contains an audible transformation, close listening may reveal many
internal stages.

If the twelve gestures belong to one familiar figure, they may fuse into a
single episode. Event count alone predicts very little.

This gives us two useful compositional questions:

1. What makes time perceptible while it passes?
2. What will allow the listener to reconstruct that passage later?

Pulse and expectation often answer the first. Contrast and segmentation often
answer the second. A boundary—a silence, cadence, registral shift, new
articulation, or change of ensemble—cuts a span into memory. Too many boundaries
can make time nervous or granular. Too few can make a long form hard to recall,
which may be a failure or the point.

Continuity weakens segmentation. Elide one gesture into the next, keep the
register stable, and make changes gradual enough that no instant declares a new
section.

The passage may be understood retrospectively: we notice that we have arrived
somewhere else but cannot say exactly when we left.

This is one reason claims that music “stops time” should make us suspicious. A
piece may represent suspension by withholding pulse, cadence, or visible
progress. It may induce some listeners to lose track of clock time.

Those are different claims.[^5] The first can be analyzed in the music. The
second varies with listener, occasion, and attention. A work need not literally
abolish the clock to compose a powerful experience of suspended direction.

The gain is more precise than timelessness: a composer can loosen direction
without pretending time has disappeared.

## 18. The weight of a rare event

Write nine attacks across four minutes. No tremolo, no sustained rearticulation:
nine beginnings. The restriction immediately makes spacing part of the music.

If the attacks occur every thirty seconds, the listener may infer a slow pulse.
If the first four are close together and the next is delayed, the long gap is
measured against the established activity.

If eight occur in the first minute and one in the last three, the final attack
carries the pressure of an entire empty span. But its weight will depend on
what it changes.

An **event** is not merely an attack. It is a difference the listener can use.
A repeated quiet note may begin as a sequence of events and gradually become a
state. A chord change without a new onset may become the largest event in a
passage. We can therefore separate **event rate**—how often something registers
as new—from **event magnitude**—how much the event alters the current musical
world.

Sparse music often fails through uniform magnitude. Every isolated note is
surrounded by ceremonial silence, placed in the same register, given the same
attack, and allowed the same decay.

The first event establishes a world; the remaining eight confirm it. Rarity
alone cannot keep granting equal importance.

To differentiate the nine attacks, change their relations rather than
decorating each one. Let one arrive before the previous sound has decayed.
Place another after complete silence. Make two share a pitch so the second is a
return rather than new information.

Let a low attack recast the harmony of a still-ringing high one. Put three
close enough to form a phrase, then make the next gap long enough that the
phrase becomes a unit of measurement.

The longest silence need not be the most consequential. A wait becomes shaped
when the music gives us something to wait with: a decay, an expectation, a
remembered interval, a visible effort, an unresolved relation.

Without such a clock, a gap may be spacious, tense, restful, or simply
unarticulated. Duration does not acquire meaning merely by being left open.

Give the silence something to transform, and waiting becomes an event the
listener can enter.

## 19. After ninety minutes, scale

Feldman once suggested that after roughly an hour and a half, “form” gives way
to “scale.”[^6] The wording is provocative, not a scientific threshold. A
ninety-one-minute piece does not enter a new metaphysical category.

Feldman was trying to name a practical change: beyond a certain span, the
relationships that organize a conventional form can no longer be grasped in the
same way.

Think again of *String Quartet II*. A short motif repeated thirteen times is
locally intelligible. A section lasting fifteen minutes may also contain clear
internal relations. But when related material returns an hour later, recognition
is less like identifying the recapitulation of a sonata theme and more like
meeting someone whose face is familiar before you remember why.

The imprecision is compositional. The listener may recognize a registral shape,
a kind of chord, an articulation, or the spacing of attacks without retrieving
the exact earlier passage.

Memory supplies a degree of sameness rather than a binary answer. Feldman's
large scale allows these uncertain recognitions to matter.

The quartet is not six hours of undifferentiated quiet. Its cells and textures
change; rates of activity vary; recurrences produce local fields. Yet the
duration alters the status of those materials.

A gesture can be very clear without revealing where we are in the whole. A
return can feel intimate without functioning as recovery. The work does not
merely test how much listeners can remember. It composes with what memory
cannot securely hold.

This suggests a distinction between **formal map** and **formal climate**. A
map lets us locate an event within an ordered route: opening, transition,
return, ending. A climate is known through recurring conditions and gradual
shifts.

We may not know our exact coordinate, but we recognize what kind of musical
weather we inhabit. Long music can use both. Feldman's quartet offers local
maps—cells, sections, recognizable textures—inside a scale where the total form
is often held as climate.

The metaphor has limits. A climate sounds passive, while performers make every
instant. The score demands continuous decisions about attack, duration, blend,
count, and concentration. Large scale is not a fog that excuses detail. It
changes how detail participates in the whole.

Composers sometimes imitate the outer signs of such scale by stretching
material that still thinks in short phrases. A four-bar idea is played at a
quarter of its former speed; silences are inserted; the result lasts twenty
minutes. This can work, but duration has not necessarily become structural.

Ask whether relationships that were trivial at the shorter length have become
audible. Has memory changed its role? Has performer effort entered the form?
Does a recurrence now cross enough time to alter recognition? Has the listener
been given another clock, or merely asked to tolerate a slower one?

Automatic inflation changes the ruler. Scale changes what the ruler can measure.

When scale changes what can be heard, length stops being an expense and becomes
a source of form.

## 20. Memory that cannot quite repeat itself

Feldman's *Triadic Memories* approaches the problem from a smaller but still
extended frame. In one passage, chords recur in unequal numbers.

Feldman described forgetting the previous chord, reconstructing the sequence,
then rearranging it and changing the counts. He called the result a way of
“formalizing a disorientation of memory.”[^7]

The phrase is exact because the memory error was not left as private accident.
It became a principle of form.

Chords return often enough to establish familiarity, but their counts and order
resist an easy periodic map. The listener is neither wholly lost nor securely
oriented. Recognition continues; placement wavers.

The method differs from hiding all repetition. If no material returns, memory
has no object to misplace. It also differs from a regular cycle, in which each
return confirms our location.

*Triadic Memories* offers evidence that something has happened before while
weakening our confidence about when, how often, and what should follow.

This section is not the place for a full theory of repetition. What matters is
the temporal use of imperfect recurrence. A composer can make memory active by
giving it a task just beyond certainty. Repeat a chord three times, another
five, the first twice, a third four times. Later, bring back the sequence with
one count altered. A listener need not identify the arithmetic to experience
the unstable familiarity.

Too little resemblance and the passage becomes succession. Too much regularity
and the temporal map becomes secure.

Between them lies a useful region where memory recognizes the neighborhood but
cannot name the street.

That uncertain neighborhood can sustain return without reducing it to
confirmation.

## 21. Change without a moment

Éliane Radigue's *Trilogie de la Mort*, created between 1985 and 1993, lasts
nearly three hours across its three parts: *Kyema*, *Kailasha*, and
*Koumé*.[^8] Its temporal proposition is very different from Feldman's.

Instead of presenting many discrete cells whose returns trouble memory, Radigue
works with sustained fields whose internal proportions change so slowly that
the listener may not be able to point to the instant of transformation.

Radigue described the drone not as an end in itself but as a means of changing
the balance among a sound's constituents through nearly imperceptible
paths.[^9]

A fundamental tone may seem stable while harmonics, beating, and pulsations
gradually acquire presence. The acoustical details deserve their own discussion
elsewhere. Here we are concerned with the timescale of the change.

Imagine two clearly different states. In the first, a low fundamental dominates
and a higher band is barely present. In the second, the higher band has become
the surface while the low tone acts as foundation. A conventional transition
might mark the exchange: one part crescendos as the other recedes. A sufficiently
slow transformation denies us that clear crossing point. At minute ten, the
field resembles minute nine. At minute twenty, it no longer resembles minute
ten. Change is undeniable in retrospect and elusive in the present.

This is **threshold time**. The music crosses many small perceptual thresholds,
but no single one has to declare a new section. Different listeners may notice
the high band at different moments.

Someone attending to the fundamental may hear continuity while another,
following pulsation, hears a new state emerging. The form can be definite
without providing one public instant of arrival.

Such music requires more than turning a control slowly, just as a gradual
crescendo requires more than waiting. The beginning and ending states must be
distinct enough to establish a transformation.

The path must offer relations worth hearing: new partials emerge, roughness
changes, a pulse becomes available, a register separates. If all dimensions
change at the same rate, the result may feel like one long fade. If nothing
crosses perceptual relevance, slowness becomes concealment.

Radigue's practice gives us another answer to the problem of long duration. We
do not always need landmarks that divide time. We can compose evidence that
time has acted.

A process can remain almost hidden and still leave the listener with
unmistakable evidence of motion.

## 22. The abyss and the bird

Olivier Messiaen's *Abîme des oiseaux*, the third movement of *Quatuor pour la
fin du Temps*, reaches an expansive experience of time in a much shorter span.

It is written for solo clarinet and places extremely slow, long-breathed lines
and broad silences against rapid birdsong figures.[^10]

Messiaen's own program identifies the abyss with time, sorrow, and weariness,
and the birds with a desire for light.

We can respect that theological-poetic account without treating it as the only
way the music means. The score gives us audible evidence: two temporal
characters with sharply different clocks.

In the slow material, breath becomes a boundary. A tone's duration and dynamic
shape make the player's effort perceptible. The spaces between phrases do not
feel like ordinary rests inside a steady meter; they extend the scale of
waiting. Pitch movement is consequential because it is exposed.

The birdsong material changes the body of time. Attacks arrive quickly.
Register and contour move with agility. A listener who had been measuring
duration by breath and silence receives many landmarks at once.

The contrast does not merely alternate slow and fast tempos. It changes what
counts as an event, how we group events, what we expect from continuity, and
how the performer's body appears in the sound.

When the slow character returns, it does not restore the opening. The rapid
music remains in memory as another possible clock. Silence after quick motion
is not the same silence as before it.

The movement demonstrates that a piece does not need several hours to create
temporal scale. It needs relations strong enough to make one kind of time
revise another.

This is useful for composers who admire long music but do not yet know whether
their material requires length. Put two clocks in a shorter span. Let them
interrupt, expose, or recolor one another.

If the contrast produces the desired experience, thirty minutes may add
nothing. If the relation needs acclimation, forgetting, or bodily accumulation,
then length may be structural.

Length then becomes available as a real choice, reserved for relations that
need time to become themselves.

## 23. Anticipation without climax

Waiting is often treated as a promise. A dominant chord waits for resolution. A
crescendo waits for arrival. A repeated pattern waits for the interruption that
will justify its persistence. These expectations can organize long spans, but
they can also burden every quiet passage with a climax it does not need.

Music can project several kinds of future.

It can promise **return**: a familiar event will come back, and we measure time
against its absence. It can approach a **threshold**: a process is changing,
but we do not know when the new state will become clear.

It can reveal a **process**: the next step is partly predictable even if the
endpoint is not. Or it can offer **continuation**: the present condition
appears capable of going on, and our attention turns from destination toward
the differences within remaining.

Continuation is not the absence of expectation. We may expect the field to
persist and become sensitive to anything that violates it. A sustained harmony
can therefore produce tension without behaving like a conventional suspension.

The tension may concern fragility rather than resolution: *How long can this
condition remain itself? What degree of change will end it?*

For a long passage, decide which future is active. If a process clearly points
toward a threshold, postponing it for twenty minutes may enlarge anticipation
or simply exhaust it.

If the passage offers continuation, a sudden climax may feel imported from
another formal language. If returns establish time, their gradual disappearance
may be more consequential than any culminating chord.

Anticipation becomes oppressive when every detail is treated as a clue. Not all
sounds need to promise something later. Some can complete their work locally.

The balance between local sufficiency and long implication determines whether
duration feels inhabited or merely deferred.

When local gestures can stand on their own, anticipation gains room to breathe.

## 24. Boredom is not a verdict

Sooner or later, any honest discussion of extended music reaches boredom. The
subject attracts two unhelpful responses. One declares boredom proof that the
piece has failed. The other treats it as proof that the listener has not yet
become sufficiently enlightened.

Boredom is information, not a verdict.

It can signal that the music has stopped supplying perceptible relations. The
events may be too similar, the state too uniform, or the timescale of change
too small for the listener to find.

This is the problem of **event famine**: not simply few events, but too little
evidence by which duration can be shaped.

Boredom can also arise because the music has removed a familiar listening job.
There is no tune to follow, cadence to predict, or pulse to count. The first
response may be restlessness.

If another scale of activity becomes audible—a change of tone, an uncertain
recurrence, the movement of attention itself—the restlessness may give way to a
different engagement. That passage through boredom can be part of the form.

Or the listener may remain bored. No theory grants the composer an appeal.
People differ in familiarity, energy, need, and willingness.

A six-hour work does not become important because six hours were demanded. Nor
does one listener's absorption prove that another's fatigue is mistaken.

The useful compositional test is specific. When attention loosens, what becomes
available? Is there another relation to hear, another scale to enter, a change
that can be recognized retrospectively? Does the piece permit departure and
return? Or does it require continuous attention while offering no consequence
for that labor?

Do not add events merely to prevent boredom. Added incident may destroy the
scale the passage needs. But do not use purity of concept as an alibi.

A process can be perfectly consistent and temporally dead. The composer remains
responsible for what consistency does.

The useful response is curiosity: what has stopped changing, and what small
decision could make time active again?

## 25. Bodies keep time

In 1996, the Kronos Quartet planned another performance of *String Quartet II*.
They cancelled after rehearsals caused serious back and shoulder problems.[^11]

The fact should not be turned into heroic legend. Injury does not prove musical
depth, and endurance is not an aesthetic argument. It does reveal that duration
is borne by bodies.

A string player sustains the instrument asymmetrically. A wind player must
organize breath. A pianist repeats hand shapes; a percussionist remains
standing; a singer's attention to pitch changes as the voice tires.

Quiet playing can be more physically exacting than loud playing because small
instabilities remain exposed. Long rests can provide recovery, or create
another strain: the player must remain ready without losing the scale of the
piece.

Composers sometimes treat performer stamina as an implementation detail. It is
part of form. Rest, rotation, changing posture, altered activity, and cueing can
be composed. So can permission to stop. An ensemble may share a continuous
field while individuals enter and leave it. A solo work may acknowledge the
body's limit by allowing the sound to fray rather than demanding an impossible
purity.

Consent matters too. A performer who chooses a demanding practice after
understanding its conditions is not the same as one surprised by preventable
harm.

Rehearsal duration, breaks, page turns, concentration, venue schedules, and the
trip home belong to the real temporal ecology of a work. None is less musical
because it does not appear as a pitch.

Listeners also carry duration. Seating, movement, access to water, the ability
to enter and leave, and knowledge of the expected span change how time is
experienced.

A long installation that permits wandering proposes a different contract from a
long concert that closes the doors. Neither is inherently more serious. Each
makes a different demand.

The score may say five hours. The music asks who will live them.

Treating those lived hours as material can make duration more humane and more
exact at once.

## 26. Designing the span

Before deciding how long a work should be, identify the relation that requires
time.

Perhaps a texture needs **acclimation**. A rough sonority that first arrives as
one block will reveal internal voices only after the ear has lived inside it.
Perhaps the work needs **accumulation**. Repeated actions must gather enough
history for a small deviation to matter. Perhaps it needs **transformation**.

Two states are meaningful only if their nearly imperceptible connection can be
experienced. Perhaps it needs **forgetting**. A recurrence must cross enough
time that recognition becomes uncertain. Perhaps it needs **bodily scale**. The
performer's effort or the listener's change of posture is part of the form.

Each requirement suggests a test.

If the passage depends on acclimation, shorten it until the internal detail no
longer emerges. If nothing changes, the original duration may have been an
aspiration rather than a necessity.

If it depends on accumulation, remove several repetitions. Does the later
deviation lose weight? If not, the extra returns may be padding.

If it depends on transformation, make the change twice as fast. Does it become
an ordinary transition rather than a state discovered retrospectively? Then
slowness is doing structural work.

If it depends on forgetting, place the recurrence closer. Does it become a
simple reprise? Move it farther away.

Does recognition disappear completely? The needed span may lie between secure
memory and unrelated succession.

If it depends on bodily scale, rehearse the actual duration. Do not extrapolate
from ten minutes of playing and assume the fifth hour is merely more of the
same. Bodies alter technique, attention, and sound. That alteration may belong
to the work, or it may reveal that the work has not cared for its performers.

These tests will not produce one correct length. They replace the prestige of a
number with audible reasons.

Long duration can become a badge, especially in music whose seriousness is
associated with patience. The advertised span then arrives before the musical
question: a four-hour work is assumed to be more radical than a forty-minute
one.

But duration has no moral value. A brief piece can transform our sense of time;
a day-long piece can merely occupy a day.

The opposite prejudice is equally narrow. Extended work need not justify every
minute through constant novelty.

Its purpose may be to let the listener change while the material remains, to
move below the threshold of immediate detection, or to make memory unreliable.
The standard is not efficiency. It is necessity at the chosen scale.

Necessity does not make the work austere; it gives every minute a reason to
belong.

## 27. Three experiments

### 1. Passing and remembering

Choose two passages of equal clock length and contrasting event density. Listen
without checking the time.

Immediately afterward, write two notes about each: how quickly or slowly it
seemed to pass, and what internal divisions remain in memory.

Repeat the comparison on another day while listening for a different feature:
first harmony, then attacks, then changes of register. If your reports change,
that is not a flaw in the exercise. It reveals that experienced duration is
partly shaped by what attention is asked to measure.

Do not derive a universal rule such as “more events make time go faster.” Use
the results to identify the clocks you actually heard.

### 2. Nine attacks

Write a four-minute study containing exactly nine attacks. Shape it with
spacing, decay, register, dynamics, and the relation among attacks. At least one
long wait must feel structurally different from the others.

Make a second version using the same nine sounds in a different order and at
different intervals. Keep the total length fixed.

Which version feels longer while sounding? Which leaves a clearer map
afterward? What made one silence a continuation and another a boundary?

If every attack carries equal weight, revise their relations before changing
their sound.

### 3. Change without a moment

Make a five-minute transformation between two clearly different states. Let the
change be continuous enough that a listener can hear that it occurred but
cannot confidently identify the instant of crossing.

Then make a second version with one explicit boundary: a rest, attack, harmonic
shift, or registral break. Compare the two forms.

The first may feel longer in passing but less segmented in memory. The second
may make the route clearer but reduce the transformation to a before and after.
Neither result is prescribed.

Finally, shorten the continuous version until its transformation becomes
obvious.

The shortest duration that preserves uncertainty tells you more about the
work's scale than the longest duration you can defend.

That threshold is a practical discovery: it tells you how long uncertainty
needs in order to live.

## 28. Four scores, several hours later

Return to the FLUX Quartet several hours into *String Quartet II*. The
oversized scores are no longer startling objects.

They have become pages already turned, pages remaining, changes of posture,
remembered cells, uncertain recurrences, and thousands of small decisions that
cannot be stored as one overview.

A familiar configuration arrives. Perhaps a player recognizes it from an hour
ago; perhaps the recognition is only a feeling that this kind of sound has been
inhabited before. The passage returns, but it does not restore the bodies that
first played it or the listeners who first heard it. Shoulders carry more time.
Attention has widened and narrowed many times. Memory has kept some relations,
altered others, and quietly released the rest.

That is what scale can do. It makes duration an agent rather than a container.
The music is not simply longer than memory or the body. It composes with their
changing capacities.

We do not need six hours to begin this work. A single decaying tone can supply
a clock. Nine attacks can make four minutes feel divided, suspended, or
crowded.

A short movement can place breath time beside bird time and make each revise
the other. What matters is that length, tempo, event rate, rate of change,
memory, and effort are allowed to become separate choices.

Once they separate, duration stops being a number written at the top of a
sketch.

It becomes a question asked of every sound: how long must this relation live
before we can hear what it is doing?

The next question is what that span can afford to contain. Every musical world
is made from exclusions as well as choices. To discover a necessary duration is
therefore also to discover a limit.

A well-shaped span gives the next limit real force: whatever enters now has
time enough to matter.
