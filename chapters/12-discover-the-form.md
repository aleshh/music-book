# Chapter 12: Discover the Form

## Continuing and Leaving

In 1969, Gavin Bryars made a sketch for an exhibition at Portsmouth College of
Art. He was looking for a musical equivalent of conceptual art: a work whose
governing idea could survive more than one realization. The subject he chose
was the *Titanic*.

The ship's band had continued to play as the vessel sank. What it played at the
end is uncertain. Harold Bride, the junior wireless operator, later said that
the last piece he heard was a hymn called “Autumn”; other testimony and later
tradition point elsewhere. Bryars did not resolve the contradiction. He made a
work that could contain it.

His premise moves from report into speculation. Sound travels efficiently in
water. Imagine, then, that the band's music did not end when the deck passed
beneath the surface. It continued to reverberate, descending with the ship,
repeating and changing as it entered another medium. The players stopped. The
music did not.[^1]

This is not physics offered as documentary proof. It is a compositional idea
about what an ending changes. A melody crosses a threshold. The conditions of
its continuation alter. Memory, water, distance, testimony, and loss become
part of its orchestration.

Bryars calls *The Sinking of the Titanic* a work of “1969–.” He made a
performing version in 1972, recorded one for Brian Eno's Obscure label in 1975,
and has continued to revise the work for different occasions and spaces.[^2]
The dash after the date is small, but it describes the form unusually well.
This is a piece about persistence that has itself persisted by changing.

We have reached the last chapter, so it is tempting to look for a conclusion:
a method that gathers the subjects of this book and places a double bar after
them. Form rarely behaves so politely. A piece may end by arriving, but it may
also end by dispersing, being interrupted, exhausting a procedure, returning
to an earlier state, or handing its attention back to the room. A final sound
can close a path or open a distance.

The more useful question is not “How do I finish?” It is this:

**What has made continuing meaningful, and what kind of ending belongs to that
reason?**

Form is the changing reason to keep listening.

## A Shape Is Not Yet a Form

We often speak of form as a shape: arch, line, circle, wave. Shapes are useful
because they let us hold a long span in the mind. They also conceal nearly
everything that makes music temporal.

Draw an arch on paper. Its apex exists at the same moment as its beginning and
end. Hear an arch in music and you must remember the ascent while the descent
occurs. You may miss the peak. You may decide later that the important turn
happened before it. The form is not only the arrangement of events. It is the
succession of understandings those events make possible.

Suppose a piece begins with a soft five-note figure. It returns ten times.
Nothing about that description establishes a form. The returns could be
interchangeable. They could mark a gradual registral climb. They could become
a ground beneath changing lines. The fifth return might be absent, making the
sixth sound like recovery. A loud interruption might divide all later returns
into remembered and present versions.

The inventory remains one figure, ten statements, perhaps one interruption.
The form lies in changing consequence.

This distinction matters especially in music with few events. A dense sonata
can borrow momentum from contrast, harmonic departure, and thematic argument.
A sustained field cannot assume that duration will organize itself. Low event
density is not form. It only makes the poverty or strength of the relations
easier to hear.

Nor is a plan identical to the heard result. “Add one instrument every two
minutes” is a plan. It becomes form only if each addition alters the field in a
way a listener can register: perhaps the center shifts, a hidden rhythm
appears, or an apparent background becomes a line. If the seventh entrance
means no more than the sixth, the procedure is still operating while the form
has gone idle.

A compositional diagram should therefore include verbs as well as shapes.
Not merely *A–B–A*, but *establish, estrange, recognize*. Not merely *thin to
thick*, but *separate, entangle, overwhelm, uncover*. The verbs describe what
the changes do.

## Five Models of Continuation

Most pieces combine several kinds of form. It is still useful to distinguish
five models because each gives continuation a different purpose.

A **path** goes somewhere. Its present is understood partly through departure
and destination. Functional harmony often supports path form, but a path need
not be tonal. A steady climb in register, a migration across a performance
space, or the gradual removal of noise from a tone can create direction.

A **state** persists. Its identity lies in conditions rather than itinerary: a
pitch field, density, tuning, texture, activity, or kind of attention. State
form is not necessarily static. A forest is a state at one scale and thousands
of events at another. The composer's problem is to decide which changes the
state can absorb and which produce a new one.

A **process** applies a consequential operation. Copies move out of phase.
Notes are removed. A resonance is recorded and returned to a room. The process
gives us a way to anticipate change, although we may not know its exact sound.
Its formal question is whether hearing the operation continues to reveal
something.

A **circle** returns. The return may confirm a stable cycle, create ritual
presence, or allow the listener to leave and rejoin. Circular form makes no
promise that later is better. Its danger is replaceability: if any cycle could
be the last, the actual ending may feel administratively chosen.

A **spiral** returns with consequence. Material comes back, but memory,
register, density, harmony, place, or bodily condition has altered its meaning.
The notes at the end might match the opening exactly. The listener does not.

These are not genres. A drone piece may be a state whose spectrum follows a
path. A repeating pattern may form a circle locally while additions create a
large process. An open ensemble score may spiral because every return carries
the memory of choices the performers made.

Choose the model that describes why the next minute should exist.

If the work is a path, what changes our distance from somewhere? If it is a
state, what can we learn by remaining? If it is a process, when does the rule
become audible and when does it stop disclosing? If it is a circle, why this
many turns? If it is a spiral, what does each return know that the previous one
did not?

The answer may change during the piece. That change is often the form.

## How a World Begins

The first event has two jobs. It makes a sound, and it teaches us how beginnings
will work.

A piece can begin by **declaration**. A chord, pulse, or figure arrives with
enough definition to establish identity. Declaration is useful when later
events will question or redistribute something clear. It need not be loud. A
single quiet attack can declare a register, interval, and scale of action.

It can begin by **emergence**. A tone enters near the threshold of hearing;
several unrelated sounds gradually reveal a shared field; resonance becomes
distinguishable from the room. Emergence makes the act of recognition part of
the opening. Its risk is a generic fade-in that postpones the first decision.
Ask what becomes perceptible, not only what becomes louder.

It can begin by **generation**. We hear a rule produce its first consequences:
one player starts a pulse, another derives a phrase from every fifth attack, a
third sustains the pitches where their cycles meet. The opening teaches not an
object but a behavior.

It can begin by **interruption**. The music cuts into an existing activity or
seems to enter mid-sentence. An interruption gives the opening a past we did
not hear. That can create urgency, humor, or the sense that the work exceeds
its performed boundaries.

It can also begin by **framing what is already present**. The first composed
sound makes ventilation, footsteps, rain, or audience movement newly relevant.
The piece begins when a relation begins, not necessarily when the room first
contains sound.

Try the same material with each opening. Take a low dyad, a high recurring
note, and a breath-length line. State them together. Then let them emerge one
by one from silence. Then let the high note generate entrances. Then begin with
all three already active. The materials do not determine the form. The manner
of entry tells us whether to hear an object, discovery, system, or fragment of
a longer life.

An opening should establish a question large enough to sustain its answer. It
need not reveal everything. It must give later events something to change.

## Four Places Where Form Can Move

When composers feel a passage losing energy, they often add material. A new
chord, instrument, rhythm, or layer enters. Sometimes this is exactly right.
Often the music needed a new relation rather than a new object.

Form can move in at least four places.

First, **the material can change**. A note is added, a figure shortens, the
register widens, attacks become rougher. This is the most visible kind of
change and therefore the easiest to overuse.

Second, **relationships can change while material remains**. The same note
moves from inner voice to bass. Two cycles that began together separate. A
background grows near enough to mask a line. A pitch that sounded ornamental
becomes the center because everything else starts returning to it. No new
inventory is required.

Third, **the listener can change**. Repetition teaches a pattern. Scarcity
increases the weight of a rare event. A sound first heard as decoration becomes
a cue once its consequence has happened twice. The score may repeat exactly
while memory makes the repetition new.

Fourth, **the context can change**. The players move, daylight recedes, the
room empties, an activity begins nearby, or a long resonance allows a past
event to overlap the present. Context is not a license to credit every accident
as composition. It is formal when the work has established a way of hearing or
answering it.

These locations can substitute for one another. If a repeated harmony seems
stalled, do not change the harmony yet. Change who holds its bass. Let one tone
arrive late. Remove the attack that has been confirming the meter. Place a
silence after a return so that memory must complete it. Move the listener to a
position where two layers rebalance.

The central compositional skill is not generating more things. It is hearing
where change will matter.

## Several Clocks at Once

A whole form rarely moves at one rate. A useful draft hears at least four
timescales.

The **gesture** is the local action: an attack and decay, a breath, a turn of a
line. The **cycle** is the interval at which a behavior recurs. The **section**
is the span during which a set of relations remains operative. The **whole** is
the memory a listener can make of the piece's large difference.

Trouble begins when all four clocks agree too neatly. Every four-note gesture
fills one phrase; every four phrases make a section; every four sections make
the piece. Proportion can clarify, but automatic nesting makes each scale
confirm what the others already say.

Let one clock cross another. A long line can continue through a sectional
boundary. A cycle can return once after the texture that supported it has
vanished. A slow registral descent can span many local accumulations. The
listener then hears both the event and the larger condition it has not yet
completed.

This is one reason minimalist forms can feel large without containing many
themes. Their scales are legible but not identical. In *Music for 18
Musicians*, for example, pulse, breath-length phrases, cycles of harmony, and
the work's large return operate together without collapsing into one measure.
We need not repeat the earlier analysis of those clocks to carry its formal
lesson forward: shared duration does not require shared units.

Make a map with four horizontal lines. Mark gestures on the first, cycles on
the second, relational changes on the third, and the work's broad trajectory
on the fourth. If every mark aligns, break one alignment. If none align, decide
how the scales will become audible as parts of one work.

Coordination is not the same as synchronization.

## Renewing a State

State-based music presents a special problem. If the state changes too little,
continuation becomes replaceable. If it changes too much, the state was merely
an introduction to a path.

The answer is not a prescribed amount of variation. It is **renewal**: an event
or shift that lets the listener discover another aspect of the same condition.

Imagine a field built from D, A, and E. A low D sounds continuously. A and E
enter in several registers. The obvious way to continue is to add pitches. But
the field might renew when E first appears below D, making the former floor
sound suspended. It might renew when attacks disappear and only staggered
breaths preserve the tones. It might renew when A, previously abundant, is
absent long enough for its return to feel structural.

Renewal needs difference and retention. Something must change; something must
let us recognize what the change belongs to.

This balance can be heard in the distinction between variation and incident.
A variation exposes another possibility within an identity. An incident asks
for attention mainly because it is new. Incidents are not forbidden. A sudden
crash may be the form's necessary wound. But if every loss of attention is met
with another surprise, the piece trains us to wait for replacement instead of
hearing consequence.

Ambient music often fails here by confusing consistency with continuity. The
same agreeable color remains, new layers enter at prudent intervals, nothing
disturbs the premise, and no minute becomes necessary. The music can accompany
an activity successfully. As an autonomous form, it has not yet found a reason
for its length.

Ask of every continuation: what can be heard now that could not have been
heard earlier? The answer can be modest. “The low tone has become a memory” is
enough—if the piece has actually made that change audible.

## When a Procedure Becomes a Character

A procedure begins as an instruction. Add one note to the pattern. Lengthen
each silence. Let two copies separate. Remove the loudest event after every
cycle.

At first we may hear the rule itself. Later, if the music is working, we hear a
kind of behavior: insistence, hesitation, erosion, gathering, refusal. The
procedure has become a character.

This does not mean concealing the mechanism. An audible process can remain
clear while its consequences exceed our summary of it. Alvin Lucier's room
process is easy to describe; the particular resonances that rise are not
therefore emotionally or acoustically exhausted by the description. Julius
Eastman's accumulations can be procedurally direct while their pressure is
anything but neutral.

The transition from rule to character depends on selection. What material does
the procedure act on? At what rate? What happens near a perceptual threshold?
Who bears its physical cost? When is it interrupted? Which byproducts are
allowed to remain?

A process is not an alibi. “The rule produced it” does not answer whether the
result deserves thirty minutes, places an impossible demand on a performer, or
repeats an unexamined expressive habit. The composer designs the family of
consequences and listens when an actual member arrives.

Test a procedure at its extremes. If one note is added each cycle, continue
until the texture is saturated. If the work cannot survive saturation, decide
whether that failure is its ending or a condition the rule should prevent. If
silences lengthen, continue until performers and listeners can no longer hold
the previous event in working memory. What begins beyond that threshold: a new
section, a different piece, or nothing useful?

A strong procedure includes the possibility of contradiction. It can reach a
place where obeying it reveals why it must stop.

## The Piece Discovers Its Length

Duration is often the first number attached to a commission and the last fact
understood in a draft. We may be given twelve minutes before we know what those
twelve minutes must contain.

There is no morally correct length for reduced music. A miniature can reveal a
field in forty seconds. An hour may be necessary for a pitch to lose one
function and acquire another. Long duration is not proof of seriousness, and
brevity is not a failure of attention.

Length grows from several demands.

A distinction needs enough time to become perceptible. Two nearly equal pulses
must separate far enough for their relation to change. A rare event needs a
long enough field to become rare. A listener needs repeated evidence before a
sound can become a cue.

Material has a rate of disclosure. A complex spectrum may change while the
score holds one note. A plain diatonic figure may need context, register, and
memory before its ambiguity becomes active.

Performers have bodies. Breath, fatigue, balance, repetitive strain, and the
quality of sustained attention shape what a duration means. An eight-minute
tone distributed among an ensemble is not the same form as one player's
unbroken demand.

Places have schedules and publics. Sunset, traffic, access, weather, work, and
the possibility of exit make a span consequential beyond the page.

Finally, an ending needs preparation. Interruption can be immediate, but even
an abrupt stop gains meaning from what it interrupts. Dispersal needs enough
layers to make departure perceptible. Return needs enough distance for memory
to have work to do.

When a required duration exceeds the material's present capacity, do not
inflate every section. Add another formal scale. A six-minute state might
support a twenty-minute work if three appearances place it in different
relations. Or compose a genuine interval of absence rather than stretching
the state until it thins.

When the required duration is shorter, do not play a long process faster. Ask
which threshold contains the argument. A complete form can end before a system
exhausts its possibilities.

## An Action Ends; Its Sound Continues

Tōru Takemitsu's *Rain Tree*, composed in 1981 for three percussionists,
takes its image from Kenzaburō Ōe's rain tree: abundant leaves hold water and
release droplets after the shower has passed.[^3] The image offers a precise
formal distinction. Cause and consequence occupy different spans.

Strike a crotale and the player's action ends almost immediately. The tone
does not. Strike a marimba and its shorter decay defines another measure of
aftersound. Let a vibraphone tone continue under later attacks and the earlier
event becomes part of their harmony.

In *Rain Tree*, resonant metals, wooden bars, spacing, and silence make us hear
this difference repeatedly. An attack may initiate a gesture, but decay decides
how long that gesture remains available to other sounds. The section does not
consist only of what the performers do. It includes what their actions have
not finished doing.

This gives us several possible endpoints.

There is the **last action**, when a performer stops adding energy. There is
the **last intended sound**, which may continue after that action. There is the
**threshold of audibility**, where resonance becomes difficult to distinguish
from the room. There is the **formal end**, when no later consequence changes
our hearing of the work. And there is **release**, when listeners understand
that they may move, speak, or applaud.

These thresholds need not coincide.

A score that ends on the last attack may cut off its own consequence. A score
that asks performers to wait through decay composes attention into the
aftersound. A score that includes a measured silence after decay makes the
absence of resonance a new state. A performance that leaves release unclear
may create useful suspension—or merely strand the audience in etiquette.

Silence after sound is not automatically profound. Its force comes from a
relation the piece has prepared. If decay has carried harmony throughout the
work, attending to the last decay completes an argument. If silence has never
had a formal job, adding thirty seconds at the end may feel like a request for
reverence.

The tree does not release water forever. Continuation has a scale.

## Seven Ways to End

An ending is not one gesture. It is a change in the work's right or ability to
continue. Seven strategies cover a useful range.

**Fulfillment** completes a stated tendency. A registral ascent reaches its
limit. Two drifting parts realign. A collection finally gives its absent
pitch. Fulfillment need not be triumphant; it can be the quiet satisfaction of
a relation becoming whole.

**Exhaustion** allows a procedure or body to spend what it has. A pattern loses
notes until it cannot identify itself. A repeated breath shortens. A tremor
continues until the player can no longer sustain its quality. Exhaustion is
formal when the resource being spent has been audible.

**Dispersal** lets parts leave at different times. Density thins, registers
separate, pulses cease to find one another. Dispersal suits music whose
identity has been social or layered. If every voice stops on one cue, the
ending may contradict the independence the work established.

**Interruption** stops a continuation that could have persisted. The cut makes
the absent future audible. Interruption is powerful when the listener knows
what has been denied. Used without that knowledge, it is merely short.

**Evaporation** moves across a threshold until the work can no longer be
distinguished from silence or surroundings. This is more specific than a
fade. Volume may remain while attacks disappear; pitch may remain while
identity diffuses; the surrounding field may rise into the foreground.

**Return** brings back an earlier state. In a circle, the return confirms
continuity. In a spiral, it exposes difference: the opening chord now carries
everything heard between. Literal restatement can create the largest change
if memory is doing the work.

**Reframing** leaves sound active but changes what contains it. Performers walk
away while a public activity continues. A recurring tone ceases to be counted
as part of the piece. The doors open. A listener realizes that rain heard
throughout the performance was not accompaniment but the condition to which
the music is being returned.

These endings can combine. A process fulfills a limit and disperses. A return
is interrupted. Resonance evaporates into a reframed room.

Choose an ending by asking what kind of continuation has governed the work. A
path can arrive or be interrupted. A state can disperse or be reframed. A
process can fulfill or exhaust itself. A circle can stop at a marked return. A
spiral can reveal that the beginning has become another place.

The ending belongs to the verb.

## Four Measures Beyond the Last Note

The second movement of Arvo Pärt's *Tabula rasa* is called *Silentium*. It is a
slow prolation canon: related voices proceed at different rhythmic speeds,
with the bass moving through the fastest values and the first solo violin the
slowest.[^4] The movement asks us to hear duration not as empty distance but as
the exact relation among long tones.

Near the end, the music descends and thins. Its continuation becomes exposed.
There is less activity to confirm where we are, so each remaining event carries
more formal weight. Then the sounding stops.

Pärt wrote four unusually long measures of rest after the last notes. At the
1977 premiere in Tallinn, remembered accounts describe the audience allowing
the silence to continue beyond the written measures before applause began.[^5]

The distinction is important. Pärt did not leave the end blank and hope that
solemn listeners would repair it. He notated a duration in which performance
continues without new sound. The rests belong to the piece. The audience's
additional waiting belongs to that occasion.

The two silences touch but are not identical. One is composed; the other is a
collective response. Neither is literally silent. Breathing, the room, and the
effort not to break the threshold remain audible. More important, both are
shaped by what precedes them. After a movement devoted to long, exposed
relations, waiting is not an imported ceremony. It is the last operation.

An ending can therefore compose a transition from controlled duration to
uncontrolled duration. The score determines four measures. It cannot and need
not determine how long a particular audience will hold what follows.

This is a useful limit on authorship. You can prepare release. You cannot
guarantee what listeners will feel, how long a room will remain still, or what
sound will answer the last one. Form ends partly by surrendering those facts.

## The Continuation Test

When a draft is complete enough to play, examine every major span with one
question:

**What does more time make newly audible?**

Do not accept “the texture continues” as an answer. Name the consequence. The
center becomes less certain. A hidden pulse emerges. The performer reaches an
audible edge of breath. A rare event gains weight. Two rates approach. The
listener can leave and return without losing the state. A room resonance
overtakes its source.

If you cannot name the consequence, remove some duration. Listen again. The
passage may become too short for its relation to register. Restore only what
the ear can defend.

Then test the opposite. Extend a strong passage beyond your instinct. The
extra time may reveal that you were protecting the listener from the premise.
It may let a repetition cross from confirmation into pressure. It may expose a
performer's fatigue as a neglected formal fact. Or it may prove that the
process ran out long before it stopped.

The test is not a demand that every second announce novelty. A sustained state
can make the passage of time itself newly audible. But that claim must be heard
in the relation among sound, body, memory, and context. It cannot be granted by
the tempo marking.

Finally, ask the test of the ending. What does stopping make newly audible?
What future does interruption imply? What residue does dispersal leave? What
does return now mean? What part of the world becomes present when the work
releases it?

An ending is strong when it changes the past as well as the future.

## Three Experiments

### 1. Four Locations, One Form

Make a one-minute state from no more than four sounds. Continue it four times.

In the first continuation, change only the material: add, remove, or alter one
sound.

In the second, restore the original material and change only its relationships:
spacing, register, simultaneity, masking, or responsibility.

In the third, repeat the original state exactly but prepare the listener to
hear it differently. Establish one event as a cue, create an absence, or let a
rare detail gain weight.

In the fourth, change the context: position, surrounding activity, resonance,
or the listener's route.

Which changes create a new section? Which deepen the same state? Which fail to
matter? Build a form from the two strongest, even if they occur in different
locations.

### 2. Five Endings, Same Passage

Write or choose a two-minute passage with a clear mode of continuation. Make
five endings without changing its central material.

Let one tendency fulfill itself. Let one resource become exhausted. Disperse
the parts. Interrupt the passage at the point of greatest implied future. Then
reframe its last sound as part of the surrounding world.

Play all five. Notice that the endings ask for different preparation. Revise
no more than the preceding thirty seconds to make each ending belong.

The exercise is not a contest. Keep the version that reveals the most about
what the passage had been doing all along.

### 3. The Continuation Test

Mark every section of a draft with its current duration. After each mark,
complete the sentence: “This must continue because more time allows us to
hear…”

Delete any answer that merely renames duration: “it settles,” “it develops,”
“it becomes immersive.” Replace it with an audible relation.

Cut twenty percent from the passages with weak answers. Add twenty percent to
the passage with the strongest answer. Rehearse both changes before restoring
your original proportions.

Now identify the last action, last sound, formal end, and audience release. If
they coincide, separate two of them. Listen to the interval you have created.

## Leave Something Open

Return to Bryars's submerged orchestra. A hymn is heard on deck, if the
testimony is right. The ship descends. The imagined sound enters water and
continues under changed conditions. Decades later, the work takes another
form, collecting another space and another present around its durable premise.

The story illuminates form because it refuses the easiest equivalences. The
players' ending is not the sound's ending. The historical event is not the
work's last version. Persistence is not sameness. Uncertainty is not absence
of structure.

The pieces considered throughout this book offer many reasons to continue. A
tone continues so that we can enter its interior. A limit continues until
small differences acquire cost. A pattern returns so that context and memory
can change it. Clocks separate so that relation becomes rhythm. Layers gather
so that a line can become a field. Performers decide so that a work can exceed
one person's hearing. A place answers so that music does not pretend to occur
nowhere.

None of these methods guarantees a form. They make consequences available.
The composer's task is to hear which consequence belongs, how long it needs,
and when another event would deepen the relation or merely delay the end.

A work can leave something unresolved without being unfinished. The unresolved
thing must be specific. A second center remains credible. A pulse continues in
memory. A final resonance crosses into the room. A performer still has a
choice the audience will not witness. Openness is not the absence of decision.
It is a decision about what may continue beyond the frame.

This book should end in the same way. Not with a law of ambient or minimalist
music, and not with a list of approved surfaces. It has proposed ways of
hearing and making relations: attention, duration, boundary, continuity,
gravity, recurrence, drift, layer, color, agency, place, and form. They are not
steps in a method. Any one of them can alter the others.

Listen before the first sound. Make one decision clear enough to have
consequences. Let it continue long enough for those consequences to become
audible. Then notice when the conditions have changed.

The next sound need not be large. It must enter a relation worth continuing.
