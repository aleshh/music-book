# Section 7: Drift

## 83. When the Copies Separate

In 1964, Steve Reich recorded a Pentecostal preacher known as Brother Walter
in San Francisco's Union Square. The sermon concerned Noah, the flood, and the
warning that gives Reich's later piece its title: *It's Gonna Rain*.

At home, Reich made two identical loops of a short phrase and placed them on
two inexpensive recorders. He intended to offset them in a fixed pattern.

When he began listening, they were aligned. Then one machine moved almost
imperceptibly ahead of the other.

The voice thickened before it divided. A slight roughness became an echo. The
echo widened until the words began to answer themselves. Consonants that had
belonged to one cadence formed new rhythms across the two copies.

As the gap continued to grow, recognizable speech became a counterpoint of
attacks, vowels, pitches, and warnings. The machines had failed to maintain
unison. Reich heard a form.[^1]

The discovery is often summarized as a technical accident. That is true and not
yet musically useful. The mechanism matters because it changed one thing: the
temporal relationship between two copies.

The material remained recognizably related at every stage. Without that
identity, there would have been no alignment to lose and no separation to
follow.

Nor was the material neutral. It remained Brother Walter's voice, with the
melodic pressure of a Black Pentecostal sermon and an apocalyptic warning.
Reich has said that repetition intensified rather than erased the meaning.
However we judge the later use, a rhythmic account that turns the voice into
ownerless syllables has stopped listening too soon.

Drift begins in that tension. Parts can become independent without becoming
unrelated. Their encounters can generate events no one part contains.

A shared pulse may remain while phrase lengths breathe. Two cycles may meet,
separate, and meet elsewhere. Several performers may occupy one duration
without agreeing on its subdivisions.

The task is not to release music from time. It is to compose relationships
among times.

## 84. Drift Begins with Agreement

Imagine two percussionists repeating the same five-attack figure in unison. One
gradually becomes a little faster. At first the disagreement may sound like
untidy playing. Then the attacks separate clearly enough that a second line
appears.

Later, one player's third attack falls near the other's second, creating a new
accent neither has been assigned. The original unison becomes one stage in a
changing relation.

Now imagine two percussionists playing unrelated materials at unrelated speeds.
Their attacks also coincide and separate.

We may hear a texture, but not necessarily drift. Nothing has established what
kind of relation is changing.

Drift needs an invariant.

The invariant might be identical material, as in phase music. It might be a
shared pitch field, contour, interval family, pulse, cycle length, register, or
total duration.

It may even be a common instruction: continue until you hear another player's
low note, then begin again. Some feature must let us hear the parts as members
of one situation.

The invariant need not be obvious at once. A slowly recurring pitch can reveal
that two wandering lines share a center. A common phrase ending can emerge
after several differently timed approaches. But if the relationship remains
entirely private—visible in the score and absent from both listening and
performing—it cannot give temporal separation much consequence.

This distinguishes independence from indifference. Independent performers
keep their own time while remaining responsible to a shared musical condition.
Indifferent performers merely happen at once.

The difference is not moral. Indifference can be a deliberate sound. A piece
may want simultaneous activities that refuse mutual adjustment.

But if the composer wants drift, there must be something against which distance
can be felt.

## 85. Pulse Is Not Meter

A **pulse** is a recurring temporal interval we can use as a reference. A
**meter** groups pulses into a hierarchy: stronger and weaker positions, bars,
recurring patterns of expectation.

Music can have pulse without an audible meter. A sequence of equally weighted
attacks may provide continuity while refusing to announce where “one” is.

This is useful for independent timing. Two parts can share a pulse but group it
differently. One repeats a three-pulse figure, another a five-pulse figure.

If their lengths remain exact, the relation is a stable polyrhythm: its
composite pattern recurs after fifteen pulses.

Drift begins when the relation itself changes. Perhaps one player lengthens a
single interval each cycle. Perhaps one breath determines a phrase that lands
near, but not exactly on, the common pulse. Perhaps two nominally equal pulses
have slightly different speeds. The parts no longer return to the same vertical
arrangement at a predictable common measure.

This does not make stable polyrhythm less sophisticated. A fixed ratio can
support an entire musical world. It simply answers another question.

In stable polyrhythm, the relationship repeats even when its local crossings
are complex. In drift, the relationship has a history.

Meter can remain present during drift. A string player may repeat a four-beat
figure while a pianist's five-beat cycle gradually lengthens.

Each part has a clear local meter; the ensemble has no permanent common
downbeat. Conversely, several performers may share a pulse and avoid meter by
using irregular or changing groupings.

The useful distinctions are therefore not strict versus free, or measured
versus unmeasured. Ask three separate questions:

What temporal reference does each part use?

Which references are shared?

Does the relation among them repeat, or change?

## 86. Phase: Hearing the Offset Move

**Phase** names a particular changing relation between periodic events. Two
copies of a pattern share a cycle and begin at the same point. One changes
speed slightly, causing their relative positions to move.

When it has advanced by one unit of the pattern, a new alignment appears.
Continue far enough and the copies pass through a series of offsets before
returning to unison.

Use a four-attack pattern to hear the simplest case:

> long — short — short — rest

Two players begin together. One holds the pattern steady. The other very
gradually advances until its long attack coincides with the first player's
first short attack. The written material has not changed. The composite rhythm
has.

At the next offset, the long attack meets the second short attack. At another,
it falls against the rest and becomes exposed. Each alignment is a different
vertical hearing of the same horizontal figure.

The process creates **resultant patterns**: rhythms or melodic contours the
listener groups from attacks distributed across both parts. No performer plays
the resultant as a line. It occurs between them.

Resultants are one reason phase music can feel more active than its materials
suggest. Attention moves among possible groupings.

A high note may seem to belong to one emerging melody, then another, even
though both players continue their patterns. The listener participates by
selecting temporary figures from the composite.

This also means phase is sensitive to articulation and balance. If one part is
much louder, we may hear a melody with an unstable echo rather than one
composite field. If attacks are too blurred, the offsets may change without
producing legible patterns. If timbres are too different, each line may remain
so separate that the vertical result never coheres.

The desired degree of fusion is a compositional choice. Phase does not require
the parts to become anonymous. It requires their changing offset to be
available to the ear.

## 87. A Process Enters the Body

After the recorded-speech pieces, Reich wanted to transfer gradual phase to
live performance. He first recorded a short piano pattern and tried to play
against it as if he were another recorder.

To his surprise, he found that he could move gradually from one alignment to
the next. The machine's imperfection had revealed the process; the performer's
imperfection made it musical in another way.

In *Piano Phase*, two pianists begin by repeating the same pattern together.
One remains steady. The other accelerates slightly until the parts reach the
next alignment, then settles into that shared speed.

The procedure continues through successive offsets and later patterns until the
players return to unison.[^2]

Reich emphasizes that the form is worked out and not improvised. Yet the
performance is not mechanical obedience. The moving player must make the speed
difference small enough that the transition can be heard but large enough that
it completes.

The steady player must remain stable without becoming deaf to the composite.
Both must recognize when a new alignment has become clear enough to inhabit.

The score determines the path. Listening determines how the path is walked.

This is a useful model beyond Reich's procedure. A composer can specify states
and direction without fixing the duration of every transition. “Move from
unison to the next eighth-note displacement over several repetitions” gives a
goal, a relation, and a tolerance. The performers make the continuous time
between those points.

Such notation should not disguise difficulty. Gradual phasing demands an
unusual division of attention: hear your own repeated action, the partner's
stability, the speed of separation, and the patterns emerging between you.

Repetition reduces the burden of recalling new notes while increasing the
burden of relational hearing.

This is why a simple pattern is not merely minimalist branding. It creates
enough cognitive room for time itself to become performable.

## 88. Several Ways to Leave Togetherness

Phase is only one member of a larger family. Independent time can be designed
through several different relations.

In **fixed-ratio counterpoint**, parts maintain different but stable tempos.
One may play four pulses in the duration of another's five.

Their alignments recur according to the ratio. This is not drift in the
strictest sense, but it can establish the common field from which drift
departs.

In **gradual offset**, parts share nearly the same speed while their relative
position moves, as in classic phase.

In **tempo trajectories**, each part changes speed along its own path. One
accelerates, another slows, and their meeting may occur only once.

In **elastic cycles**, the order of events remains while each cycle expands or
contracts.

A performer may repeat a phrase with one longer silence each time, causing its
meetings with another fixed cycle to migrate.

In **body-measured time**, a breath, bow, gait, or comfortable gesture sets the
length of a phrase. The durations vary because bodies vary, not because time is
absent.

In **cue-linked asynchrony**, performers keep independent local clocks but
respond to particular events. A low note from one player may release the next
cycle of another. The parts do not share a grid, yet their histories remain
causally connected.

In **common-span asynchrony**, several parts begin and end together but divide
the interval differently.

One fits seven events into the span, another repeats a figure until a cue, and
a third sustains one line. Their internal clocks are independent; their formal
boundary is shared.

These relations can combine, but they should not all be introduced because the
composer has discovered a menu. Choose the form of independence that makes the
musical question exact.

If the piece concerns gradual loss of alignment, stable tempo ratios may be too
static. If it concerns bodily difference, a mathematically perfect phase may
solve the wrong problem.

## 89. The Tempos Cross

Conlon Nancarrow spent much of his mature career composing for player piano,
marking rolls so that rhythmic relations beyond ordinary human performance
could sound with precision. In Study No. 21, often called *Canon X*, two voices
follow opposing tempo trajectories.

The upper voice begins extraordinarily fast and decelerates. The lower begins
slowly and accelerates. Their speeds meet near the center, cross, and continue
exchanging their original extremes.[^3]

The X is temporal. One diagonal descends from fast to slow while the other
rises from slow to fast.

Unlike phase at almost equal speeds, the voices do not proceed through a cycle
of regular offsets. Their distances contract and expand at changing rates. At
the beginning, the fast part can seem like a spray of sound above a deliberate
line.

Near the crossing, their gestures become mutually legible in a new way.
Afterward, the identities reverse: what was ground becomes velocity; what was
velocity becomes space.

Tempo is not a marking applied to the material. It is the subject of the
canon.

The player piano is an essential condition, not a recommendation that
performers become more machine-like. Nancarrow wanted curves and densities
that hands could not reliably execute. The mechanism lets two exact temporal
impossibilities occupy one instrument.

Human performers can borrow the relational idea without pretending to achieve
the same extremes. One player can accelerate across a minute while another
slows. The point of rehearsal is not to reproduce an abstract X perfectly.

It is to decide which crossing must be heard: equal pulse, similar event
density, registral exchange, or the moment each line becomes intelligible at
the other's former scale.

This reveals a broader principle. A convergence point matters only if the
music gives us a way to recognize convergence.

## 90. Breath Against Pulse

In *Music for 18 Musicians*, Reich places two measures of time beside one
another. Pianos and mallet instruments maintain a regular pulse.

Voices and wind instruments take a comfortable breath and sustain pulsing notes
for as long as that breath allows. Strings, which do not need to breathe in the
same way, follow the swelling and release of the bass clarinet.[^4]

The breath phrases do not drift away from the pulse. They are counted through
it. What changes is the length of each bodily span and the shape it gives the
surface.

Breath after breath rises and falls against a grid that continues without
needing every phrase to be the same size.

This is a valuable counterexample. Independent time does not always mean that
everyone loses the beat. One parameter can remain exact while another stays
elastic. A singer may enter on the pulse, continue for a comfortable breath,
release, and reenter without turning respiration into a fixed number of
counts.

The relation is social as well as bodily. Performer cues move the ensemble
through the work's sections. The absence of a conductor does not eliminate
leadership.

It relocates it. At different moments, players must recognize a cue, transmit a
change, maintain a pulse, or shape the breathing field.

This prevents the familiar error of calling flexible time “organic,” as if
bodies were gardens and metronomes were office furniture.

A breath is variable but trainable. A pulse is measured but can be played with
warmth. Neither is morally superior. Their relation creates the music.

For a composer, the practical question is which variable benefits from bodily
measure. Phrase length may. A complex sectional proportion may not.

Asking a wind player to use a comfortable breath gives useful responsibility;
asking the same player to intuit five minutes while carrying a demanding line
gives them a hidden stopwatch and less air.

## 91. Coincidence Is Not Yet Form

When clocks separate, unplanned vertical events appear. Two notes arrive
together.

A rest opens at the same moment in several parts. Independent lines form a
chord, then dissolve before anyone can repeat it.

These coincidences can be among the pleasures of asynchronous music. They can
also be overpraised. If every meeting is treated as revelation, the word has
lost its standards.

Decide what kinds of coincidence the piece can make consequential.

A **pitch coincidence** may reveal the shared harmonic field. Two lines that
have wandered independently arrive on the same note or form the interval the
piece has withheld.

An **attack coincidence** can briefly create a common pulse. If simultaneous
beginnings are rare, one may feel like a structural alignment even when it was
not scheduled.

A **registral coincidence** occurs when lines cross or occupy the same band.
The event may fuse them, mask one, or reverse their perceived order.

A **formal coincidence** joins larger conditions: two players end their cycles
near the same time, allowing the texture to clear without a central cue.

The composer can strengthen these possibilities without fixing their exact
moments. Give the parts a common pitch that each reaches by a different route.
Let one cycle shorten while another lengthens so their endings pass through a
zone of proximity. Reserve simultaneous attacks elsewhere. The meeting remains
contingent, but the conditions have made it legible.

Near-coincidences may be more interesting than exact ones. Two attacks miss by
a breath.

A unison almost settles, then opens again. The listener feels a relation
approaching without requiring the parts to lock.

Coincidence becomes form when it changes what can happen next. Perhaps players
switch material after they meet, one begins listening for another cue, or the
ensemble allows a new register to open.

Otherwise the chord is a passing gift. There is nothing wrong with accepting it
and continuing.

## 92. Give Independence a Boundary

Independent parts still need a score of responsibilities, whether or not that
score uses conventional notation.

Specify the invariant. If every player derives material from one contour, make
the allowable transformations clear.

If a pitch field is shared, say whether all notes are equal or some retain
particular roles. If the total duration is common, decide who marks it.

Specify the local clock. A part may use a pulse, a repeated physical action, a
breath, a phrase loop, an independent tempo, or a sequence of cues. “Free time”
is not a clock. It is an admission that the composer has not described one.

Specify the relation that must remain perceptible. Do players need to hear
successive phase alignments? Stay within a certain distance?

Avoid unison? Respond when cycles end near one another? The instruction should
identify an audible condition, not a hoped-for atmosphere.

Specify tolerances. If one player must remain steady, how much correction is
acceptable? If breaths measure phrases, may a player omit material rather than
strain?

If a convergence is important, can performers wait, repeat, or cue one another
to make it happen?

Finally, specify recovery. A phase performance can lose its alignment. A cue
can be missed. A performer may need to breathe early.

Decide whether the piece welcomes the new state, returns to the last known
state, or uses a shared event to reassemble.

Recovery is not an embarrassment added for less-than-ideal performers. It is
part of writing for events in time.

The wider questions of who should decide, how scores distribute authority, and
when chance changes authorship belong to a later section.

Here the principle is narrower: temporal freedom becomes playable when
performers know what relation their freedom must keep alive.

## 93. When Drift Becomes Blur

The first failure is insufficient difference. Two loops begin at nearly the
same speed, but the piece ends before their separation becomes audible. The
composer knows they have drifted by several milliseconds. The listener hears a
slightly untidy unison.

The second is excessive difference. Parts separate so quickly that identity
vanishes before the relation can be learned. What might have been a changing
offset becomes two unrelated tempos.

The third is decorative asynchrony. Entrances are loosened to make a passage
feel spacious, but no particular timing alters harmony, density, memory, or
performer behavior.

The parts could align without loss. Freedom has become a reverb applied to
notation.

The fourth is the polished blur. Performers are told to play independently, yet
the composer expects a continuous, elegant cloud: no exposed attacks, no
awkward silences, no accidental octave that sounds too tonal, no one player
briefly becoming foreground.

Real independence has been authorized only on the condition that it imitate a
predetermined surface.

The fifth is process without listening. A player executes a tempo curve while
ignoring the changing composite. The design may still sound, as Nancarrow's
mechanical work demonstrates.

But in an ensemble whose premise is relational, failure to hear the relation
removes the reason human independence was chosen.

The repair is usually not greater complexity. Strengthen the invariant. Widen
or narrow the rate difference. Reserve one kind of meeting. Give a cue
consequence. Remove a part that contributes activity but no temporal identity.

Drift should change how the parts mean one another.

## 94. Designing the Separation

Begin with a relation clear enough to lose. Unison is the most obvious, but not
the only one.

Two lines may share a contour in different registers, repeat the same interval
sequence, or end on one pitch while phrasing differently.

Choose the moving temporal variable. Will offset change while speed remains
nearly equal? Will one cycle lengthen?

Will both tempos travel? Will breath reshape phrase length against a fixed
pulse? Change one relation first.

Set the perceptual scale. A phase difference that becomes clear in ten seconds
creates one kind of attention; one that takes five minutes creates another.

The listener needs enough time to learn each state without mistaking the
transition for permanent roughness.

Decide whether alignments are destinations, passing states, or accidents. If a
new offset should settle, give it time.

If a convergence is a formal center, change something when it arrives. If
meetings are incidental, do not surround each with a halo in the programme
note.

Give the parts different responsibilities. One may maintain pulse, another
shape the rate of change, a third mark a larger span. Independence becomes
clearer when it is not distributed as an identical vague permission.

Listen to the composite at several distances. Up close, can the performers hear
what they must adjust? At a listener's distance, can the invariant survive the
room?

Does amplification, articulation, or register make one clock dominate so
thoroughly that the others become ornament?

Then let the process surprise you. A resultant pattern may deserve more time
than the plan allowed.

A near-coincidence may reveal the real climax. The composer remains responsible
for choosing which discoveries enter the work.

## 95. Three Experiments

### 1. Hear the Third Rhythm

Choose a short pattern of five to seven attacks with at least one distinctive
long interval. Two players repeat it in unison.

One remains steady. The other gradually moves to one new offset and stays
there. Do not continue through the entire cycle at first.

While listening, identify a rhythm that seems to appear between the parts. Sing
or tap it separately.

Then return to the phase relation and change articulation or balance until that
resultant becomes clearer, then less clear.

The exercise is not to produce a perfect Reich imitation. It is to hear that a
relationship can generate a third object without adding material.

### 2. Three Clocks, One Span

Give three parts the same total span and material derived from one interval or
contour.

The first repeats a fixed cycle. The second uses a cycle one event longer. The
third performs a set number of phrases whose internal durations are free, then
waits.

Begin and end together, but do not correct internal meetings. After one
performance, mark the coincidences you remember. In the second, reserve one
pitch or attack type so it can occur only when a player hears another part
nearby.

Compare the forms. Did the common ending feel discovered or merely scheduled?
Which invariant made the middle belong together?

### 3. Breath Against Pulse

Establish an unaccented pulse with one or two players. Winds, voices, bowed
instruments, or any performers working with a comfortable physical gesture
enter on that pulse and continue a figure for one natural span.

Do not standardize the span. Each performer rests, renews, and reenters while
the pulse continues.

Assign one player the responsibility for cueing a new harmonic field after
hearing three or more releases gather near one another.

Repeat with another player carrying the cue. Notice how leadership changes the
timing even though the rule does not.

If the pulse begins to sound like accompaniment, redistribute it among the
ensemble or allow its timbre to change.

Keep its temporal function while preventing one instrument from becoming
permanent furniture.

## 96. The Warning in the Counterpoint

Return to the two copies of Brother Walter. At the beginning they speak as one.
As they separate, the warning becomes echo, rhythm, density, and argument.

At their widest dispersal, the copies still share a source. That shared
identity is what lets distance remain audible.

Drift does not liberate the phrase from relation or history. It multiplies the
positions from which we hear them. The new patterns belong neither to one copy
nor the other, yet they could not exist without this voice, this cadence, and
this chosen act of repetition.

Once several clocks are active, the ear faces another question. Some patterns
emerge; others disappear inside the composite.

A line becomes an echo, an echo becomes a band, and a band briefly opens to
reveal one strand. Temporal relation has become texture. We must now decide
what, inside the layer, can be heard.
