# Section 8: Layer

## Chapter 97: Turning Relationship into Form

On November 14, 2024, Patrick Shiroishi stood alone at Cafe OTO in London with
a saxophone. He began plainly. A tone entered, showed its edge, and ended.

A short line followed. At first, the sparseness made every breath legible: one
player, one instrument, one action at a time.

Then an earlier action returned.

Shiroishi works in two solo modes. Sometimes he plays acoustically. Sometimes
he routes the saxophone through a small collection of devices that allow him to
distort, sustain, freeze, and repeat what he has played.

He has described the problem these devices help him address without
mystification: how can one performer make a thirty-minute span remain sonically
alive, and how can those sounds serve what he needs to say?[^1]

At Cafe OTO, looped refrains gradually formed between saxophone and voice. New
saxophone lines occupied the spaces inside them. Low tones swelled beneath
brighter activity. Circular breathing extended a line past the length of an
ordinary phrase; fragments of recorded speech entered and broke apart.

For a time the accumulated field cleared until little remained beyond breath
moving through the reeds. Then the layers returned, more forcefully, before the
whole mass was removed.[^2]

We could say that Shiroishi used one instrument to sound like many, but that
would describe only the quantity. The more consequential change was in the
status of each action. A phrase he had just played became a repeating refrain.
As soon as it did, it ceased to belong entirely to the present. Shiroishi could
answer it, harmonize it, cover it, leave it exposed, or make it sound like a
memory of a passage the performance had outgrown. The captured phrase could do
none of these things. It would continue to repeat the decision it had already
made.

The performance therefore contained an unequal ensemble: one musician able to
listen and several earlier versions of him unable to change their minds.

That inequality is musically fertile. A captured layer has exact memory but no
judgment. It can hold a place while the performer travels elsewhere. It can
also become a burden.

A phrase persuasive enough to preserve may, five minutes later, occupy the
register needed by a new idea. A low swell may make it newly tender, or merely
inaudible. The player does not add sound to an empty surface. Every new action
changes the function of what is already there.

This is the compositional work of layering. The word often suggests a stack:
put one thing down, place another on top, and continue until the desired
fullness has been achieved. But a stack is only a count of materials. Music
begins when the materials acquire relations.

One strand becomes a ground, another a figure, a third an interference. Two
fuse into a color neither owns. A line disappears without stopping. A mass
clears, and a detail that had been present all along becomes the subject.

A layer is not simply a sound that is there. It is a sound doing a job beside
other sounds. Layering becomes form when those jobs change.

## Chapter 98: Parts, Streams, and Layers

Imagine three performers.

One sustains a low, rough tone, renewing it with an audible breath. Another
plays a short three-note figure high above, leaving irregular gaps. A third
produces a continuous, soft tremor with no distinct pitch.

We are likely to hear three activities. Their registers differ. Their attacks
differ. Their rates of change differ.

Each has a recognizable continuity, so attention can leave it and find it
again. They are not merely simultaneous; they are separately legible.

Now change the relations. Move the three-note figure into the range of the
sustained tone. Lengthen each of its notes until its attacks become less
prominent. Replace the tremor's dry articulation with a smooth sound whose
resonance overlaps the others. Let all three swell and recede together.

There are still three performers and three written parts, but the listener may
begin to hear one composite body. No player has vanished. Perceptual
independence has.

It helps to keep several terms distinct.

A **part** is an assigned activity: what a player reads, remembers, or is told
to do. A **voice** often implies a line with some melodic or contrapuntal
independence. An auditory **stream** is a succession of events we hear as
belonging together.

A **layer** is a stream, voice, or blended group that performs a recognizable
role within a concurrent field. **Texture** is the total condition created by
their relations.

These categories do not line up neatly. A violinist alternating rapidly between
a very high note and a low one may produce one notated part but two auditory
streams: the high notes connect across the intervening lows, and the low notes
connect across the highs.

Conversely, ten string players moving in close rhythm and register may fuse
into one layer. The score tells us who does what. It does not settle how many
things the listener will hear.

Events tend to connect when they occur near one another and share qualities
such as register, timbre, loudness, spatial position, and manner of attack.
They tend to separate when one or more of those qualities changes sharply.
These are tendencies, not laws.

Performance balance can turn a planned blend into a solo with accompaniment. A
resonant room can join attacks the score keeps distinct. Familiarity with an
instrument may let one listener follow a line another hears only as part of the
mass.[^3]

For composition, the useful question is not *How many layers have I written?*
It is *What continuities can be heard, and what are they doing to one another?*

Suppose four instruments play the same rhythm in the same register. If their
attacks align and their sounds combine closely, they may create one enlarged
event. Stagger their entrances slightly and the chord acquires an internal
rustle.

Separate their registers and the event becomes vertically spacious. Give one
instrument a harder attack and it may supply an edge around the others. The
notes need not change. The number of players need not change. The audible
organization has changed completely.

This gives the composer a kind of counterpoint that is not limited to melodies.
The subjects can be bands of register, rates of activity, kinds of attack, or
degrees of fusion. One layer may move from independence toward blend while
another travels in the opposite direction. Their changing legibility can be
the form.

## Chapter 99: A Line Can Lose Its Name

A melody usually remains a melody because we can connect its successive events.
We hear one note as continuing the action of another.

Similar tone, reasonable proximity in register, and a coherent rhythm help
maintain that connection.

But melodic identity is not permanent. Repeat a short line quickly enough,
overlap it with several close variants, or surround it with similar attacks,
and its contour may cease to be the main fact.

Individual notes combine into a flutter, band, or grain. What had been a line
becomes texture.

The reverse is equally important. Begin with a dense, blended sonority. Let
most of its activity settle while one narrow contour continues.

A melody can emerge without entering. It was present as internal motion before
the field made room for us to follow it.

These transformations do not require a conventional solo. A layer comes
forward whenever it takes over the job of carrying difference. In a stable
field, the one changing strand may become foreground. In a restless field, the
one sustained tone may become the reference. If everything else disappears, a
formerly supportive figure becomes exposed even if it continues at the same
dynamic.

Foreground, then, is not the front row of an ensemble. It is a temporary
responsibility. A foreground may carry direction, exception, identity, or the
detail by which the rest of the music is measured.

A background may provide continuity, but it can also contain activity waiting
to become legible.

This matters in ambient and minimalist practice because new material is often
less necessary than a new relation to existing material.

A composer hears attention weakening and adds a melody. Then another. Soon
every layer is offering to rescue the piece, and the rescue party has blocked
the door.

Before adding, try changing what can be followed. Hold the busiest layer on one
tone. Move a supporting figure away from the register it has been masking.

Align two formerly independent rhythms so that their colors fuse. Let an
isolated line inherit the resonance of a layer that has stopped. The music can
acquire a new foreground without acquiring another object.

We can describe this as **relational form**. A form based on themes asks how
one musical identity returns, develops, or yields to another.

Relational form can keep the identities substantially stable and change whether
they are separate, fused, dominant, concealed, or absent. The event is not
always a new thing. Sometimes it is a new way the existing things can be heard
together.

## Chapter 100: Density Has More Than One Number

Density is often confused with the number of parts. The mistake is
understandable. Eight players can make more sound than two. But “more” has
several meanings, and they do not necessarily rise together.

Consider at least five kinds of density.

**Source density** is the number of sounding performers, instruments, voices,
or recorded strands. Twenty players in unison have high source density even if
they create one audible layer.

**Event density** is the rate at which attacks or changes register. One pianist
can produce a dense surface with a rapid tremolo and pedal; a large ensemble
can produce a sparse surface by sustaining a chord.

**Registral density** concerns how much pitch space is occupied and how
tightly.

Six notes compressed into the middle register may feel more congested than
twelve tones distributed across six octaves.

**Spectral density** concerns how fully sounds crowd one another within a band.

Closely related instrumental colors in a shared register can form an opaque
body even at modest volume. Contrasting colors distributed widely may remain
transparent.

**Dynamic pressure** concerns the physical and perceptual force of the field.
It is influenced by loudness, but not reducible to it.

A narrow, high, sustained cluster can exert considerable pressure without being
the loudest passage in a piece. Repeated hard attacks may feel denser than a
continuous sound with more acoustic energy because they keep declaring new
boundaries.

We might add **behavioral density**: how many different kinds of action ask to
be followed. Three layers with unrelated rhythms, phrase lengths, and contours
can be cognitively crowded while a much larger ensemble moving together feels
singular.

These dimensions give accumulation a more precise vocabulary. Adding a player
raises source density. It may or may not change any other kind. If the player
doubles an existing tone softly, the result may be a subtle thickening of
color.

If the player enters in an empty register with a contrasting attack, one
addition may divide the whole field into two distances. If several quiet parts
begin articulating independently, event and behavioral density may rise before
the total sound becomes louder.

This is why an accumulation need not behave like a crescendo. A piece can add
sources while becoming more transparent: widen the register, slow the attacks,
or give new parts a common contour that lets them group.

It can reduce the number of parts while becoming more oppressive: compress the
survivors into a narrow band, harden their articulation, and make their rhythms
compete.

Before writing an additive form, draw several lines instead of one. Let one
represent source density, another event rate, another registral occupation, and
another degree of blend. If they all rise together, that may be the form you
need.

But the drawing will expose the assumption. More often, consequence appears
when the lines diverge: sources increase while register opens; event rate
declines while dynamic pressure grows; the ensemble thins while the remaining
layers become harder to separate.

Thickness is a condition. Form requires a change in what thickness does.

## Chapter 101: Register Draws the Field

Register is one of the fastest ways to make layers separable, but separation is
only its first use.

Place a sustained bass clarinet tone low in its range and a violin harmonic far
above it. Even at similar dynamics, the two sounds establish different
distances.

The empty middle is not another layer, yet it becomes part of the texture: an
interval of unoccupied space through which an entrance would have to pass.

Now add an alto voice in that middle region. The new strand can behave as a
bridge. If its pitch and color share features with both extremes, the field may
sound less like two remote objects and more like one vertically continuous
body. Increase the voice's activity and it can instead become a screen,
directing attention toward the center and making both extremes feel
peripheral. One layer has changed not only the density but the apparent
distance between the others.

Composers often assign registers conventional jobs without noticing. Low sound
becomes foundation, high sound becomes light or detail, and the middle carries
the principal action. These associations have acoustic and cultural reasons,
but they are not obligations.

A rare low attack beneath sustained upper sound may behave as an interruption
rather than a ground. A continuous high tone can be the most stable object in a
piece. A middle register crowded with activity may make two quiet extremes feel
like the only available space.

To compose with register, ask what each region permits the listener to follow.
A wide gap can clarify two strands, but it can also make their relationship
seem remote. Close the gap and they may begin to rub, fuse, or mask.

Let two lines cross and their identities may weaken at the crossing, especially
if their tone and articulation are similar. The listener may follow the contour
through the crossing, switch allegiance by register, or briefly lose both. That
uncertainty can be the event.

Registral change can also carry a large form. A piece may gradually expand from
one narrow band; contract from several distances into a crowded center; migrate
upward without a crescendo; hollow out its middle; or invert its earlier weight
so that a high layer becomes ground for sparse events below.

These are not pictures placed over the music. They are changes in which strands
can meet, which can remain distinct, and where new sound can still have
consequence.

The physical instrument matters. Moving a line upward does not preserve its
color, effort, or projection. A bassoon in its high range and a flute at the
same pitch do not merely occupy one coordinate. Register changes the internal
identity of a tone as well as its relation to the field. The next section will
take up that internal change. Here the point is relational: register organizes
who can touch, who can hide, and who is left alone.

## Chapter 102: Eighty-Nine Parts, No Soloist

György Ligeti's *Atmosphères* begins with a proposition that is difficult to
reduce at the piano. A large orchestra sustains a densely packed sonority.

It is not presented as a chord whose root and quality promise harmonic travel.
Nor does one melody step forward to explain where the music is going. The
sonority is the place where the action occurs.

That surface is not made by asking the orchestra to produce an approximate
cloud. Each of the work's eighty-nine instrumentalists has an individual part.
Lines enter, move, and leave with considerable precision.

They are packed so closely and interwoven so continuously that their individual
melodic identities dissolve. Ligeti called the technique **micropolyphony** and
later suggested that “saturated polyphony” might describe it more
accurately.[^4]

The distinction is revealing. Polyphony usually invites us to follow several
voices. Saturated polyphony gives those voices enough independence to animate
the field but too much proximity for most of them to remain separately
available.

Counterpoint has not disappeared. It has moved below the threshold of ordinary
line listening.

This is one of layering's central paradoxes: exact independence in the parts
can produce perceptual fusion in the whole. If the players simply held one
block chord, the result would have less internal motion. If their lines were
more widely separated, the result would become contrapuntal in a familiar way.
Ligeti composes the region between those conditions, where we hear activity
without being able to assign every activity to a name.

His own account emphasizes what replaces foreground melody and motif. The form
proceeds through transformations of tone color and dynamics.[^5] Density
increases and decreases.

Registers narrow, open, rise, and fall. Areas of the orchestra acquire
different internal grains. A sonority does not necessarily leave so that
another can begin; one condition matures inside another.

Yet the work is not uniformly gradual. Near its middle, an extreme high band
clears and a low, busy region appears with startling abruptness.

The low activity has force partly because the high field has trained the ear's
scale. The event is not merely that low notes enter. An entire map of where the
music can exist has been redrawn.[^6]

At the 1961 premiere in Donaueschingen, conducted by Hans Rosbaud, the audience
demanded an immediate repeat.[^7] The story is useful because textural music is
often defended as though it must accept a loss of formal clarity.

Listeners may not have been able to sing a theme from *Atmosphères* after one
hearing, but they could hear its premise. Bands thicken and thin. Internal
motion changes grain. Registers are occupied and abandoned. A field can be
legible by its behavior even when its inhabitants cannot be counted.

The compositional lesson is not to imitate micropolyphony. It is to decide the
level at which identity should remain available. If individual lines matter,
protect the differences that let them be followed.

If a mass matters, give its interior enough activity that it is not merely a
held chord with an impressive head count. If the piece moves between the two,
compose the threshold. Let the listener hear a line lose its name, and later
hear another line recovered from the mass.

## Chapter 103: Masking Is Not Erasure

When one layer becomes inaudible beneath another, we often say it has been
masked. The word can imply that the hidden sound has simply been covered by a
louder one. Loudness matters, but masking is relational.

Two strands compete most strongly when they ask to occupy similar perceptual
territory. Put them in the same register, give them comparable attacks, align
their rhythms, and make their contours move together.

They may fuse, or one may obscure the detail of the other. Separate one feature
and legibility can return without a dynamic change.

Move one strand an octave. Lengthen its notes while the other remains active.
Let its attacks fall in the gaps. Give it a rough edge against a smooth field.

Have it continue while the other reaches phrase endings. Each decision creates
a handle by which the ear can recover it.

This suggests that masking can be composed in both directions. A line can
gradually disappear while it continues to sound. Increase the activity around
it.

Introduce close doublings that blur its edge. Occupy its register with similar
attacks. Shorten the gaps through which it had been visible. The line has not
faded; its separateness has.

To reveal it again, do not automatically make it louder. Remove the relation
that concealed it. One surrounding layer rests. A doubling detunes or moves
away. The field's attacks become less frequent. The return of legibility can
feel like an entrance even though the notes never stopped.

Subtraction is therefore not merely reduction. It is a way of interpreting what
remains. Remove a high shimmer and the midrange may sound heavier.

Remove a bass and an inner tone may lose its harmonic support and become
exposed. Clear all but one repeated figure and we may discover that it had been
holding the form together, or that it has nothing to say alone.

The last possibility matters. Not everything hidden by a rich texture becomes
valuable when revealed. Sometimes accumulation disguises weak material. A
clearing is an honest editor.

## Chapter 104: The Mass Changes While Staying

Phill Niblock's music offers another account of layering, built less from the
collision of active lines than from sustained tones placed in close relation.

He recorded instrumentalists, assembled many layers of their sound, and added
nearby tunings. The superimposed tones create a thick field in which beating,
roughness, apparent new pitches, and shifting prominence arise from
interaction.[^8]

In an interview, Niblock described *Early Winter* as using about fifty-five
tracks across roughly forty-five minutes.

Moment to moment, he said, the sound can seem nearly the same; compare points
five minutes apart and the differences are considerable.[^9]

This is not simply slow crossfading. A small change in one stratum alters the
network of interference across the whole mass. Remove one close pitch and a
beating pattern slows or vanishes. Add another and a region of the sound
becomes rougher.

Because the layers share instrumental families and sustain without offering
frequent new attacks, it is difficult to assign every effect to one source. The
relation is more audible than the components.

Niblock's field differs from Ligeti's in method and character. In
*Atmosphères*, many precisely active lines produce a composite whose large
changes include conspicuous shifts of density and register. In Niblock's
long-tone work, entrances and phrase contours may be far less available as
landmarks. A listener can settle into one apparent state and gradually notice
that an overtone has moved forward, a pulse of beating has formed, or the mass
now exerts pressure in another register.

The comparison reveals two paths toward a sound mass. One can saturate a field
with activity until lines lose individual identity. Or one can reduce the
activity of each layer until their minute differences become the principal
events.

In both cases, the composite is not a container holding the parts. It is
something the parts create together and none produces alone.

There is a temptation to call such a mass static. That word is harmless if it
means that the music withholds a familiar route of departure, conflict, and
return.

It becomes misleading if it means nothing changes. The change is distributed.
No single layer announces ownership of it.

For a composer, this kind of field requires unusual restraint and unusual
precision. If every small fluctuation is corrected, the sound may become
sterile. If close tunings and balances are treated casually, the desired
interactions may never appear.

The object is not “a drone with lots of tracks.” It is a set of intervals,
intensities, and durations chosen so that the relations continue producing
evidence.

## Chapter 105: Accumulation Has a Shape

Layering often enters a piece as a default narrative: begin alone, add parts,
reach fullness, stop. The shape works. It has worked often enough to become
nearly invisible.

Its danger is not predictability by itself. A familiar form can be exact and
moving. The danger is that addition begins to stand in for consequence. If
each new layer performs the same job at a higher level of volume, the piece may
grow in size without growing in meaning.

Several other profiles are available.

In a **rotating hierarchy**, the ensemble remains substantially full while the
foreground job moves.

One layer becomes active, then stabilizes as another separates. The form is
carried by changes of legibility rather than entrances.

In **gathering and clearing**, sources accumulate toward a threshold, but the
central event is what subtraction reveals.

The cleared material may be a residue, a cause, or a contradiction of the mass
that preceded it.

In **alternating opacity**, the same materials repeatedly blend and separate.
At one moment we hear a composite color; later its constituent rhythms become
distinct. The passage can breathe without changing its inventory.

In **erosion**, a dense state begins as the norm and loses strands. Each
departure changes the function of the survivors.

The last layer need not be the original foundation; it may be the detail that
had been least audible.

In **interrupted saturation**, the field approaches fullness several times but
is cut back before arriving.

The withheld mass becomes an expectation. When it finally appears—or fails
to—it carries the history of those approaches.

In **stable inventory**, all layers remain, but their registers, degrees of
blend, and rates of activity change. Nothing is added or removed. The piece
develops by redistributing space.

These profiles can coexist. A work may gather, clear to a residue, rebuild as a
rotating hierarchy, and end before saturation returns. The point is not to
collect shapes. It is to give each addition and subtraction a relational job.

Before a layer enters, ask what it will make newly audible or newly impossible.
Will it divide one register into two distances?

Will it fuse with a line and change that line's apparent instrument? Will it
conceal a pulse, add harmonic gravity, or occupy the silence in which another
phrase had been breathing?

Before a layer leaves, ask what the absence will expose. Will an inner rhythm
become readable?

Will the harmony lose its floor? Will a remaining resonance sound like memory,
or will the passage simply become smaller?

An entrance that changes no relation may still be pleasurable. Not every note
needs to submit a dissertation.

But if a long form depends on layering, the layers must do more than certify
that time has passed.

## Chapter 106: When Fullness Goes Slack

The first failure of layering is **addition without consequence**. A new strand arrives
at regular intervals, producing the outline of growth without the internal
consequences. The remedy is not a more complicated schedule. Stop after each
addition and ask what an earlier layer has become. If the answer is always
“the same, but under more sound,” the process is only accumulating inventory.

The second is **undifferentiated competition**. Every part occupies the same
register, uses the same kind of attack, and remains equally active.

Performers are then asked to solve with dynamics a problem created by the
material. Volume can rebalance a field, but it cannot supply identities the
composition has refused to imagine.

The third is the opposite: **compulsory clarity**. Each layer receives its own
register, color, rhythm, and space until no interaction threatens it.

The result may be readable but inert, like labeled specimens in separate cases.
Fusion, ambiguity, and masking are not defects when the piece gives them a
formal reason.

The fourth is **permanent halo**. One soft layer surrounds the whole work with
unchanging resonance. It may be lovely. It may also become a habit that gives
every passage the same distance and emotional lighting.

Remove it. If the piece's identity disappears, decide whether the halo is truly
structural or has been flattering material that cannot stand near the listener.

The fifth is **preassigned climax**. Saturation is treated as arrival and
clearing as release. Dense sound can be sheltering, comic, numb, or static.

A single exposed tone can be the most pressured event in a piece. Density does
not bring its own dramatic meaning; register, harmony, attack, duration, and
context create that meaning.

The last is **unexamined continuation**. A repeating or sustained layer remains
because it can. This is especially easy when a device will preserve it without
fatigue. But technical endurance is not formal necessity. A layer with perfect
memory may be the first one the composer needs to forget.

## Chapter 107: Three Experiments

### 1. When Two Become One

Choose three sustained sounds. They may be made by players, voices, resonant
objects, or recorded material. Begin with them clearly separated in register
and manner of attack.

Move them gradually toward one another by altering only one dimension at a
time:

- bring their registers closer;
- align or stagger their entrances;
- make their dynamics move together or independently;
- give them increasingly similar or dissimilar articulations;
- let one acquire the rhythm of another.

After each change, ask how many activities you hear. Do not use the number of
sources as the answer.

Find the point where three become a composite, then move back until one strand
separates again.

The exercise is not a test of correct perception. It teaches you which
relations create blend with these particular sounds, players, and room.

### 2. Mask Without Muting

Write three continuing strands, each with a clear identity. Over a short form,
make each strand disappear perceptually and later return without allowing it
to stop or change its written dynamic.

Use changes around it:

- occupy or clear its register;
- align surrounding attacks with its attacks;
- increase or reduce competing event density;
- introduce or remove a close doubling;
- change which layer reaches phrase endings;
- let another strand adopt its articulation.

If a line remains obvious throughout, the surrounding relations are not yet
strong enough. If it becomes physically inaudible, you may have solved the
problem only with balance.

Aim for the unstable region in which the sound is present but its identity can
be lost and recovered.

### 3. Archive and Answer

Create a phrase lasting five to fifteen seconds. Preserve it with live capture,
or ask another performer to repeat it exactly. Continue playing against it.

Add no new layer until you can name what the existing one has become. It might
be a ground, partner, interruption, harmonic pressure, pulse, or obstruction.

When a new layer enters, require it to change that role rather than merely
increase fullness.

After three or four layers, define one condition for clearing. It might occur
when no present action can remain distinct, when one earlier phrase has changed
roles three times, or when the most recent layer makes the first one newly
audible.

Then remove material. Do not assume the last layer added should be the first to
leave. Discover which departure changes the field most.

If you work alone, attend to the asymmetry: the archived phrases cannot hear
your present choices. If you work with an ensemble, ask the players preserving
them to remain exact until a cue releases them. Compare the two versions. A
human repetition will never be perfectly fixed, and those small adjustments
may become the most important difference.

## Chapter 108: What the Earlier Self Becomes

Shiroishi's layered work carries more than an ingenious solution to solo
instrumentation. On *Forgetting Is Violent*, he begins one suite not with
saxophone but with the recorded voice of a woman who survived the atomic
bombing of Hiroshima, layered over itself.

He has described the accumulation as anxiety-producing and placed it within a
record concerned with historical memory, racism, war, and the incarceration of
his own grandparents.[^10]

The example changes the stakes of the word *layer*. A recorded voice is not
neutral material merely because it can repeat. Its identity persists through
the texture.

Accumulation can make speech difficult to understand while making the pressure
of many simultaneous utterances more forceful. What is obscured and what is
preserved are not only acoustical questions.

At Cafe OTO, Shiroishi's own earlier saxophone phrases likewise remained
available while changing function. One became a refrain, then part of a mass,
then perhaps a trace audible through new playing.

When the field cleared, the absence did not restore the beginning. The listener
had heard what one body could become by gathering around itself.

This is the lasting principle: a layer is not what a sound was when it entered.
It is what that sound is doing now.

Once the surrounding field falls away, another question becomes unavoidable.
The exposed tone is not merely a pitch and duration. Its attack may be blunt or
porous. Its center may tremble. Its pressure can grow while its loudness falls;
its decay can carry the memory of the room. A single strand contains more
internal difference than the word *single* suggests.

Before another layer arrives, sound itself can carry the argument.
