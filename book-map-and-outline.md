# Working Map and Provisional Outline

This is a companion to `ideas.md`, not a replacement for it. It expands the menu, makes the choices easier to see, and proposes a first book-shaped sequence.

## A possible center for the book

The book could be organized around one governing question:

> How can a composer make time, attention, and feeling vivid while using limited musical means?

That question is broad enough to include ambient music, minimalism, experimental composition, tonal and modal writing, improvisation, and selected older music without having to argue that they are all the same tradition.

A useful working distinction:

- **Ambient** is treated as a relationship among music, attention, and environment.
- **Minimalism** is treated as a family of ways to work with repetition, reduction, process, and gradual change.
- **The book itself** is about compositional methods, not about policing either label.

The strongest version may be a composer's field guide rather than a history or a theory textbook. History and theory enter when they illuminate a decision a composer can make.

# I. Topic menu

The checkboxes are deliberately empty so this section can become an editorial worksheet. “Core” means the subject probably belongs in the book's main argument; “supporting” means it could live inside another chapter; “extension” means it might become an interlude, sidebar, or omission.

## 1. Time, attention, and perception

- [ ] **Musical time and clock time** — why equal durations can feel radically unequal. **Core**
- [ ] **Event listening and state listening** — listening for what happens versus inhabiting what persists. **Core**
- [ ] **Attention as a compositional material** — attracting, releasing, dividing, and gently redirecting attention. **Core**
- [ ] **Thresholds of change** — how little can change before a listener notices, and how much can change while a state still feels continuous. **Core**
- [ ] **Memory and forgetting** — the listener's imperfect memory as a source of form. **Core**
- [ ] **Anticipation without climax** — creating futurity without promising arrival. **Core**
- [ ] **Boredom, patience, and surrender** — boredom as failure, resistance, invitation, or threshold. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Multiple timescales** — gesture, phrase, cycle, section, and total duration moving at different rates. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Ritual time** — recurrence that marks presence rather than development. **Extension**

## 2. Repetition, variation, and process

- [ ] **Exact repetition and perceived difference** — why a repeated thing does not remain the same. **Core**
- [ ] **Developing variation** — transformation that preserves identity; Brahms as an unexpected resource. **Core**
- [ ] **Audible process** — music whose way of changing can itself be heard. **Core**
- [ ] **Process that exceeds perception** — underlying order that need not be consciously decoded. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Accumulation and subtraction** — form through the entrance and departure of elements. **Core**
- [ ] **Phase, drift, and near-unison** — difference generated by initially similar lines. **Core**
- [ ] **Recurrence rather than repetition** — material returning altered by context and memory. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Erosion and residue** — allowing a pattern to lose definition or leave traces. **Extension**
- [ ] **Process and dramatic pressure** — repetition that intensifies rather than pacifies; Eastman is central here. **Core**

## 3. Pitch, harmony, and tuning

- [ ] **Harmonic fields instead of progressions** — defining a region of possibility rather than a route. **Core**
- [ ] **Tonal gravity without functional cadence** — degrees of attraction, repose, and distance without conventional arrival. **Core**
- [ ] **Static harmony that is internally active** — keeping a chord while changing voicing, register, emphasis, or context. **Core**
- [ ] **Voice-leading as the real harmonic motion** — movement within apparent stasis. **Core**
- [ ] **Modes as environments** — modes as characteristic fields, not merely scales. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Pedals, drones, and reference tones** — a stable pitch against which all other pitches acquire meaning. **Core**
- [ ] **Intervals as worlds** — composing from the character and implications of one interval. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Harmonic ambiguity** — sustaining more than one plausible hearing. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Intonation, beating, and resonance** — tuning as rhythm, color, and physical sensation. **Core or extension, depending on scope**
- [ ] **Microtonal inflection** — fine pitch difference as expressive color without turning the book into a tuning manual. **Extension**

## 4. Line, counterpoint, and melodic identity

This is the clearest missing cluster in `ideas.md`.

- [ ] **Fragments instead of themes** — small melodic identities that do not demand conventional development. **Core**
- [ ] **Wandering and orbiting lines** — melodies that explore a field rather than travel toward a goal. **Core**
- [ ] **Heterophony** — several versions of one line coexisting. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Independent lines and shared climates** — counterpoint coordinated by common material rather than synchronized rhythm. **Core**
- [ ] **Register as melodic action** — relocation, spacing, and return functioning as line. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Ornament as structure** — local inflection accumulating into form. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Canons that blur rather than clarify** — imitation as texture and temporal depth. **Extension**
- [ ] **Identity without exact restatement** — how much a line may change and still be recognized. **Core**

## 5. Pulse, rhythm, and the body

- [ ] **Pulse without meter** — continuity without hierarchy. **Core**
- [ ] **Meter without an audible pulse** — proportion and recurrence beneath a floating surface. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Asynchronous layers** — parts sharing a duration without sharing a grid. **Core**
- [ ] **Breath as measure** — phrases governed by bodily capacity and release. **Core**
- [ ] **Organic tempo** — tempo as flexible behavior rather than a fixed quantity. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Polyrhythm without resolution** — rhythmic relations as stable conditions. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Attack spacing** — composing the intervals between beginnings, not only note lengths. **Core**
- [ ] **Groove, trance, and entrainment** — the bodily consequences of recurrence. **Extension**
- [ ] **Fatigue and endurance** — how duration acts on performers as well as listeners. **Extension**

## 6. Tone, dynamics, and articulation

These subjects expand “timbre as composition” into more precise compositional choices.

- [ ] **Sound as an evolving interior** — hearing activity within a single sustained tone. **Core**
- [ ] **Attack, bloom, decay, and release** — the life of a sound as a source of phrase and form. **Core**
- [ ] **Dynamics as weather** — pressure and atmosphere rather than rhetorical emphasis. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Articulation as distance** — touch, edge, weight, and connection shaping how near a sound feels. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Resonance and aftersound** — composing what continues after an action. **Core**
- [ ] **Blended and resistant timbres** — choosing whether instruments fuse or remain stubbornly distinct. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Noise, impurity, and grain** — when unstable sound makes minimal material more alive. **Extension**
- [ ] **Orchestration by function** — roles such as anchor, halo, disturbance, echo, and horizon. **Core**

## 7. Texture, density, register, and musical space

- [ ] **Texture as form** — changes of density and relationship replacing thematic development. **Core**
- [ ] **Transparency and saturation** — composing degrees of perceptual legibility. **Core**
- [ ] **Foreground, middle distance, and background** — layers distinguished by behavior, not only loudness. **Core**
- [ ] **Register as landscape** — range, spacing, and vacancy creating scale. **Core**
- [ ] **The moving frame** — an unchanged object sounding different because its surroundings change. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Masking and revelation** — material becoming audible without entering or disappearing. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Edge and center** — whether the music presents a focal object or distributes attention. **Core**
- [ ] **Silence within sound** — holes, openings, and unused registers inside an active texture. **Supporting**

## 8. Form, continuity, beginnings, and endings

- [ ] **Music without destination** — continuity not justified by arrival. **Core**
- [ ] **States, paths, and processes** — three distinct models of form. **Core**
- [ ] **Circular and spiral forms** — return at the same level versus return with accumulated consequence. **Core**
- [ ] **Open and modular forms** — order, duration, or content left partly undecided. **Core**
- [ ] **Emergent form** — large-scale shape resulting from local interactions. **Core**
- [ ] **How a world begins** — revelation, interruption, gradual gathering, and music that seems already underway. **Core**
- [ ] **How a world continues** — renewing attention without manufacturing incident. **Core**
- [ ] **How a world ends** — cadence, exhaustion, dispersal, interruption, return, and release. **Core**
- [ ] **The piece that discovers its length** — duration inferred from material rather than imposed in advance. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Miniature and expanse** — whether ambient or minimalist thinking depends on long duration. **Extension**

## 9. Constraint, chance, and compositional ecology

- [ ] **Limited materials** — depth through restriction. **Core**
- [ ] **Rules that produce behavior** — designing interactions rather than arranging every event. **Core**
- [ ] **Chance as question, not style** — deciding what the composer does not need to decide. **Core**
- [ ] **Conditional form** — “if this happens, do that” as a musical relation. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Games and feedback** — performers responding to one another through simple rules. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Self-imposed impossibilities** — productive contradictions that force invention. **Extension**
- [ ] **Redundancy and resilience** — systems that preserve identity through many possible outcomes. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Constraint and character** — how rules cease to sound procedural and begin to sound personal. **Core**

## 10. Notation, performance, and shared agency

- [ ] **What must be fixed and what may remain free** — notation as an allocation of responsibility. **Core**
- [ ] **Text scores and invitations** — describing attention or behavior instead of notes. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Proportional and spatial notation** — indicating relation without ordinary metrical precision. **Extension**
- [ ] **Improvisation inside a composed identity** — freedom that preserves a work's recognizable world. **Core**
- [ ] **Independent performers and collective emergence** — coherence without a single controlling clock. **Core**

- [ ] **Listening as an ensemble technique** — attention among performers as part of the score. **Core**

## 11. Place, environment, and the meaning of “ambient”

- [ ] **Music as object and music as condition** — something attended to versus something that alters a place. **Core**

- [ ] **Acoustic space as a participant** — resonance, distance, and decay affecting musical decisions. **Core**
- [ ] **Site, occasion, and duration** — music shaped by where and when it occurs. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Furniture, utility, and autonomy** — the productive discomfort in music made to accompany life. **Core**
- [ ] **Atmosphere versus decoration** — when unobtrusiveness becomes generic pleasantness. **Core**
- [ ] **Privacy and publicness** — intimate listening, shared listening, and music that does not demand consent. **Extension**

## 12. Expression, affect, and identity

- [ ] **Ambiguous feeling** — emotional specificity without a single nameable message. **Core**
- [ ] **Quiet intensity** — force without conventional climax or volume. **Core**
- [ ] **Beauty and its dangers** — beauty as attention, seduction, anesthesia, or avoidance. **Core**
- [ ] **Nostalgia and suspended memory** — the affect of things half-recognized. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Awe, intimacy, dread, and calm** — widening the affective range beyond serenity. **Core**
- [ ] **Neutrality as an illusion** — asking whose idea of calm or impersonality is being presented. **Core**
- [ ] **Restraint versus suppression** — reducing rhetorical gesture without erasing expressive stakes. **Supporting**
- [ ] **Identity expressed through process** — how similar procedures carry radically different urgency in Eastman, Reich, Riley, or Eno. **Core**
- [ ] **Ego, authorship, and responsibility** — Cagean non-intention examined rather than simply celebrated. **Supporting**

# II. Composer lenses to add or sharpen

These are not proposed as a canon, and not all should appear. Each earns a place only by making a musical choice clearer.

| Composer | Most useful lens | Productive complication |
|---|---|---|
| Brian Eno | Atmosphere, systems, degrees of attention | When does a system become a signature rather than a surrender of control? |
| John Cage | Silence, contingency, listening, non-intention | Can intention ever really be removed, and should it be? |
| Morton Feldman | Duration, scale, recurrence, uncertain memory | Quietness can be unstable and anxious, not merely calm. |
| Julius Eastman | Repetition, accumulation, pressure, identity | Process can be dramatic, political, bodily, and confrontational. |
| Erik Satie | Simplicity, repetition, furniture, anti-development | Functional background music can also be strange and resistant. |
| La Monte Young | Drone, tuning, duration, sustained attention | Apparent immobility can contain intense acoustic activity. |
| Pauline Oliveros | Deep listening, place, embodiment, collective attention | Listening is a social practice, not only private perception. |
| Steve Reich | Audible process, phase, pulse | Clear process does not eliminate taste, selection, or rhetoric. |
| Terry Riley | Modules, cycles, improvisation, collective form | Repetition can open freedom rather than enforce sameness. |
| Arvo Pärt | Reduction, rule, resonance, diatonic gravity | Severe limitation can yield direct affect rather than neutrality. |
| Johannes Brahms | Developing variation, motivic saturation, inner voices | Minimal material need not produce static form. |
| Claude Debussy | Harmonic color, modes, nonfunctional motion | Sensuous harmony can direct time without conventional progression. |
| Olivier Messiaen | Non-retrogradable rhythm, modes, suspended time | Symmetry may suspend direction while color intensifies it. |
| György Ligeti | Density, micropolyphony, texture as form | Innumerable moving lines may be perceived as a single object. |
| Tōru Takemitsu | Placement, timbre, silence, garden-like form | Silence is shaped by the sounds around it and by cultural context. |
| Giacinto Scelsi | The interior life of one pitch | A note can be a field of pressure, instability, and color. |
| Éliane Radigue | Near-stasis, thresholds, continuous transformation | Change can be real without becoming an event. |
| Alvin Lucier | Beating, resonance, acoustic phenomena | The sounding situation can generate form. |
| Lou Harrison | Tuning, interval character, resonance | Intonation changes harmony's physical and emotional behavior. |
| Christian Wolff | Performer interdependence and contingent form | A composition can model coordination rather than issue commands. |
| Meredith Monk | Breath, voice, gesture, ritual, embodied time | Minimal material can remain theatrical and unmistakably human. |
| Roscoe Mitchell | Silence, density, restraint, explosive contrast | Spaciousness gains force through the credible possibility of excess. |
| Gavin Bryars | Persistence, drift, found material, fragile continuity | Repetition can deepen pathos and narrative implication. |
| Harold Budd | Touch, harmonic atmosphere, restraint | A familiar tonal language can be made spacious through voicing and pacing. |

Two editorial cautions:

1. Do not make every chapter prove that an older composer was “really a minimalist.” Brahms, for example, is valuable because developing variation can challenge simplistic accounts of repetition.
2. If the book reaches into musical traditions beyond the named concert-music lineage, engage particular practices and practitioners substantially. Avoid using “gamelan,” raga, chant, or African rhythmic traditions as anonymous sources of atmosphere or technique.

# III. Possible book architectures

## A. A book of verbs

Chapters are actions: **Attend, Wait, Limit, Sustain, Repeat, Drift, Layer, Tune, Share, Place, Continue, End**.

This is the most promising alternative to composer portraits. Verbs keep the book practical and let Cage, Brahms, Eastman, Eno, and others meet inside the same chapter without pretending they share an aesthetic program.

**Strengths**

- Immediately compositional rather than taxonomic
- Gives the book momentum despite a subject centered on stasis
- Easy to combine theory, close listening, and experiments
- Memorable without leaning too heavily on landscape metaphors

**Risk**

- Some verbs overlap; each chapter needs a sharply framed question.

## B. An atlas of thresholds

Each chapter studies a boundary where one kind of experience becomes another:

- Sound / silence
- Event / state
- Repetition / difference
- Pulse / texture
- Chord / field
- Line / mass
- Intention / chance
- Composition / improvisation
- Foreground / environment
- Continuity / ending

**Strengths**

- Closely matches the perceptual subtlety of the music
- Makes ambiguity the method of the book
- Encourages nuanced comparisons rather than rules

**Risk**

- Potentially abstract; practical experiments would be essential.

## C. A spiral

The book revisits the same materials at expanding scales:

1. One sound
2. Two sounds
3. A figure
4. A cycle
5. A texture
6. A section
7. A complete duration
8. A sounding place

At each scale the same concerns return: identity, change, attention, agency, and ending.

**Strengths**

- The structure enacts recurrence with difference
- Begins concretely and grows organically
- Can make advanced ideas approachable without a textbook progression

**Risk**

- Topics such as history, performer agency, and affect do not attach neatly to only one scale.

## D. Decisions the composer gives away

The book moves from maximum determination toward increasing openness:

1. Choose every note
2. Choose a field
3. Choose a process
4. Choose a set of conditions
5. Choose how performers interact
6. Choose how the environment enters
7. Choose to listen

**Strengths**

- A strong philosophical arc about control and responsibility
- Cage, Eno, Riley, Oliveros, Feldman, and Wolff fit without becoming chapter mascots
- Makes notation and ensemble relations central rather than supplemental

**Risk**

- It privileges indeterminacy and may make fully notated music seem like an elementary starting point.

## E. Four ways music can change

Organize the book by the location of change:

1. **The material changes** — pitch, rhythm, dynamics, timbre
2. **The relationships change** — counterpoint, voicing, density, figure and ground
3. **The listener changes** — memory, attention, fatigue, acclimation
4. **The context changes** — performers, acoustics, environment, duration

**Strengths**

- A clean and original argument
- Explains how apparently static music can remain active
- Joins music theory to perception without requiring a psychology textbook

**Risk**

- Most compelling examples belong in more than one category; the prose should treat that as a feature.

## F. A braided field guide

Three kinds of short chapter alternate:

- **Ways of hearing** — attention, memory, expectation, space
- **Ways of making** — limiting, repeating, layering, tuning, ending
- **Encounters** — close readings of a work, passage, or composer's recurring practice

The braid repeats five or six times, with each pass becoming more complex.

**Strengths**

- Varied pace and approachable chapter lengths
- Historical examples can receive real attention without controlling the architecture
- The form itself alternates reflection and action

**Risk**

- Requires careful cross-references to avoid feeling like a collection of essays.

## G. Failure modes

Each chapter begins from a danger:

- When simplicity becomes thinness
- When repetition becomes inertia
- When beauty becomes decoration
- When process becomes an alibi
- When freedom becomes vagueness
- When slowness becomes solemnity
- When atmosphere erases identity
- When an ending feels arbitrary

The chapter then develops multiple musical responses rather than issuing prescriptions.

**Strengths**

- Honest, lively, and unusually useful to working composers
- Naturally invites contrasting examples
- Prevents the book from treating ambient and minimalist values reverently

**Risk**

- A whole book framed by failure may sound corrective or workshop-like. It may work better as a recurring section within each chapter.

## H. A score in prose

Instead of conventional chapters, the book is made of modules that may be read in several orders. Recurring cross-references form paths such as **Time**, **Pitch**, **Listening**, **Ensemble**, and **Form**.

**Strengths**

- The object enacts modular and open form
- Readers can make a path suited to their immediate compositional question

**Risk**

- Formal ingenuity can obstruct cumulative argument. The book still needs a recommended path.

# IV. Recommended direction

The strongest design is a hybrid of **A Book of Verbs**, **Four Ways Music Can Change**, and the problem-centered **Workshop** already in `ideas.md`.

- The **verbs** provide the visible table of contents.
- The **four locations of change** provide the hidden intellectual framework.
- Each chapter opens with a **compositional problem**, draws on contrasting composers, and closes with experiments and failure modes.

This avoids organizing the book by famous people while still giving individual works enough room to be more than name-checks. It also lets the book embody its argument: a limited set of concerns recurs in altered contexts.

## Recurring chapter anatomy

1. **The problem** — a concrete compositional question, stated in ordinary language.
2. **A listening situation** — a brief perceptual scene or thought experiment.
3. **The musical idea** — theory explained through audible consequences.
4. **Two or three encounters** — contrasting works or practices, usually not all from the same lineage or period.
5. **Choices** — a map of the decisions available to a composer.
6. **Try it** — two or three compositional experiments, from constrained to open.
7. **Failure modes** — ways the approach can flatten out, and questions that may reopen it.
8. **Afterimage** — a short observation that points toward the next chapter.

The “Try it” sections should be propositions rather than recipes. The goal is not to produce a piece “like Feldman,” but to isolate a question Feldman makes unusually audible.

# V. Provisional structured outline

## Prelude: A Music That Changes the Listener

**Question:** What do “ambient” and “minimalist” name if they do not name a single sound?

- Define the book by methods and listening relations, not genre borders.
- Introduce event, state, process, and environment as four provisional models.
- Establish the main claim: when material changes very little, changes in relationship, perception, and context become compositional events.
- Explain why Cage, Eno, Eastman, Feldman, Brahms, Oliveros, and others can converse here without being collapsed into one tradition.

## Part One: Attending

### 1. Attend — Composing the Listener's Notice

**Problem:** How can music guide attention without continually demanding it?

- Event listening and state listening
- Foreground, background, and distributed attention
- Silence as relation, not empty duration
- Existing environmental sound as context
- Music that rewards both concentration and partial attention

**Possible lenses:** Cage, Oliveros, Eno, Takemitsu

**Experiment:** Write the same brief material in three forms: one that insists on focus, one that permits attention to wander, and one that changes character depending on how it is heard.

### 2. Wait — Making Duration Perceptible

**Problem:** How can a long span feel necessary without being filled with incident?

- Clock time and experienced time
- Slowness, patience, boredom, and scale
- Attack spacing and the weight of isolated events
- Anticipation without climax
- Performer endurance and listener acclimation

**Possible lenses:** Feldman, La Monte Young, Radigue, Scelsi

**Experiment:** Compose a passage in which the rarity of events, rather than their complexity, gives them weight.

### 3. Remember — Repetition in an Imperfect Mind

**Problem:** What can recur when exact recognition is neither possible nor desirable?

- Memory and forgetting as form
- Exact repetition that is heard differently
- Recurrence, resemblance, and false return
- Identity without restatement
- Feldman's “crippled symmetry” as a possible lens, set against clearer process

**Possible lenses:** Feldman, Satie, Bryars, Brahms

**Experiment:** Return to a phrase several times while changing only one dimension; then obscure which dimension changed.

## Part Two: Limiting

### 4. Limit — Finding Abundance in Less

**Problem:** When does restriction create depth, and when does it merely reduce options?

- One note, chord, interval, gesture, or rhythm
- Constraint as identity
- Rules, games, and self-imposed impossibilities
- Selection as the composer's irreducible act
- Beauty, severity, and the danger of tasteful thinness

**Possible lenses:** Cage, Pärt, Eno, Monk

**Experiment:** Make one small collection of material support three pieces by changing its rules of behavior rather than its contents.

### 5. Sustain — Entering the Interior of a Sound

**Problem:** How can one sound become a field of activity?

- Drones, pedals, and reference tones
- Attack, bloom, resonance, decay, and aftersound
- Intonation, beating, difference tones, and acoustic life
- Microvariation within apparent identity
- Listening into rather than listening onward

**Possible lenses:** La Monte Young, Scelsi, Lucier, Radigue, Lou Harrison

**Experiment:** Sustain one interval long enough to discover several distinct kinds of motion inside it; shape a form using only those motions.

### 6. Tune — Making a Harmonic World

**Problem:** How can harmony have gravity without needing to go somewhere?

- Harmonic fields and modal environments
- Tonal attraction without functional progression
- Stable harmony with active inner voices
- Voicing, register, spacing, and resonance
- Ambiguity and the coexistence of several harmonic hearings

**Possible lenses:** Debussy, Pärt, Messiaen, Budd, Takemitsu

**Experiment:** Hold the pitch collection constant while creating departure and return entirely through voicing and register.

### 7. Connect — Letting Lines Share a Climate

**Problem:** How can several lines belong together without conventional accompaniment or lockstep rhythm?

- Fragments, contours, and orbiting melodies
- Developing variation and motivic identity
- Heterophony and blurred canon
- Independent lines joined by pitch field, register, or behavior
- Voice-leading as motion inside harmonic stasis

**Possible lenses:** Brahms, Ligeti, Riley, Feldman

**Experiment:** Derive several autonomous lines from one intervallic idea, then let them proceed at different speeds without losing family resemblance.

## Part Three: Setting in Motion

### 8. Repeat — Making Sameness Consequential

**Problem:** How can repetition accumulate meaning instead of confirming what is already known?

- Exact repetition and contextual difference
- Audible process
- Addition, subtraction, substitution, and intensification
- Groove and entrainment versus friction and insistence
- Repetition as serenity, labor, grief, joy, or confrontation

**Possible lenses:** Reich, Eastman, Riley, Satie

**Experiment:** Use the same repeating cell in one process that calms and another that increases pressure; identify which musical parameters create the difference.

### 9. Drift — Coordinating Without Locking

**Problem:** How can parts remain related while escaping a common grid?

- Phase and near-unison
- Pulse without meter
- Asynchronous layers and multiple tempos
- Organic timing, breath, and flexible cycles
- Coincidence as a source of harmony and form

**Possible lenses:** Reich, Riley, Oliveros, Wolff

**Experiment:** Give three parts the same duration and related material but different internal clocks; allow their meetings to remain unplanned.

### 10. Layer — Turning Relationship into Form

**Problem:** How can a piece develop by changing what can be perceived within it?

- Density, transparency, masking, and revelation
- Line becoming texture; texture becoming line
- Foreground, halo, disturbance, echo, and horizon
- Register as landscape
- Accumulation, saturation, clearing, and residue
- Live self-layering: a performer's captured past becoming an ensemble the
  present performer can answer but that cannot listen back

**Possible lenses:** Ligeti, Eno, Eastman, Roscoe Mitchell, Patrick Shiroishi

**Experiment:** Keep every layer unchanged but alter which one functions as foreground through spacing, articulation, and the activity around it.

### 11. Color — Letting Sound Carry the Argument

**Problem:** What if timbre, articulation, and dynamics do the work usually assigned to melody and harmony?

- Timbre as compositional syntax
- Blending and resistant instrumental identities
- Dynamics as atmospheric pressure
- Articulation as proximity, weight, and edge
- Continuous transformation without thematic development
- Families of related attacks: repeated instrumental function carrying
  nonidentical touch, decay, bleed, ambience, and performed time

**Possible lenses:** Debussy, Takemitsu, Radigue, Scelsi, DJ Shadow

**Experiment:** Compose a clear three-part form while keeping pitch and rhythm nearly constant and changing only tone, touch, and resonance.

## Part Four: Releasing Control

### 12. Share — Composing Relations Among Performers

**Problem:** What should be fixed, and what can only be discovered by people listening together?

- Composition and improvisation as a continuum of responsibility
- Performer agency and a work's identity
- Text, proportional, modular, and conventional scores
- Independent performers and collective emergence
- Hierarchy, cooperation, trust, conflict, and surprise

**Possible lenses:** Oliveros, Wolff, Cage, Riley, Monk

**Experiment:** Notate the same piece three ways, each assigning a different degree of control to the performers; compare what remains essential.

### 13. Chance — Deciding What Not to Decide

**Problem:** Which choices carry the work's identity, and which may be allowed to vary?

- Chance as a way of posing questions
- Non-intention and the persistence of taste
- Rules that generate families of outcomes
- Open and modular form
- Responsibility for consequences one does not directly select

**Possible lenses:** Cage, Eno, Feldman, Wolff

**Experiment:** Make a piece in which chance governs one parameter, performers govern another, and the composer fixes a third; then exchange those roles.

### 14. Place — Writing a Music and Its Surroundings

**Problem:** Can a piece make room for the world without disappearing into it?

- Music as object, condition, furniture, and frame
- Acoustic space as participant
- Figure and ground as changing roles
- Site, occasion, and publicness
- Atmosphere versus decoration
- The pastoral, the serene, and the supposedly neutral

**Possible lenses:** Eno, Satie, Cage, Oliveros, Lucier

**Experiment:** Compose a work with deliberate openings for unpredictable surrounding sounds, while ensuring that the work retains a recognizable identity.

## Part Five: Continuing and Ending

### 15. Continue — Renewing a World Without Leaving It

**Problem:** How can music remain itself and still earn its duration?

- States, paths, processes, circles, and spirals
- Change in material, relationship, perception, and context
- Multiple structural timescales
- Continuous renewal versus manufactured incident
- The point at which a procedure becomes character

**Possible lenses:** Feldman, Eastman, Radigue, Brahms

**Experiment:** Build a long form in which each section changes a different location: first material, then relationships, then listener expectation, then context.

### 16. End — Leaving Without Explaining

**Problem:** What makes an ending feel discovered rather than attached?

- Cadence without conventional harmonic resolution
- Exhaustion, dispersal, interruption, return, and release
- Endings implied by process and endings that resist process
- Music that seems to continue beyond its audible boundary
- Choosing a duration by listening to the material

**Possible lenses:** Feldman, Bryars, Takemitsu, Pärt

**Experiment:** Write five endings for the same passage, each based on a different premise: fulfillment, depletion, interruption, evaporation, and reframing.

## Coda: Leave Something Open

- Return to composition as listening.
- Emphasize that no method guarantees vitality.
- Offer a final set of questions rather than a doctrine.
- Invite the reader to identify which decisions their own music must retain and which it can release.

## A plausible first cut

If sixteen chapters feels too expansive, combine:

- **Wait + Remember** into **Experience Time**
- **Tune + Connect** into **Make a Harmonic World**
- **Share + Chance** into **Release Control**
- **Continue + End** into **Discover the Form**

Keep **Repeat** and **Drift** separate. Drafting the sample chapter on repetition
showed that recurrence, memory, invariants, accumulation, pressure, and
reframing already form a complete argument. Phase, asynchronous layers,
multiple tempos, and breath-governed coordination pose a different question:
how recurring parts remain related when they cease to share one clock.

That yields a twelve-chapter book:

1. Attend — Composing the Listener's Notice
2. Experience Time — Making Duration Perceptible
3. Limit
4. Sustain
5. Make a Harmonic World
6. Repeat — Making Sameness Consequential
7. Drift — Coordinating Without Locking
8. Layer — Turning Relationship into Form
9. Color — Letting Sound Carry the Argument
10. Release Control
11. Place
12. Discover the Form

This twelve-chapter version is the current working table of contents. The
sixteen-chapter version remains useful as a complete map from which to select.

Drafting Chapters 1 and 2 confirmed the `Wait + Remember` merge. `Attend` now
owns the movement among focal, peripheral, and distributed notice, while
`Experience Time` owns clock time, felt passage, remembered span, anticipation,
and endurance. The boundary keeps later chapters on Place and Sustain from
becoming second general theories of attention or duration.

Drafting `Layer` and `Color` confirmed that they require separate chapters.
`Layer` owns changing relations among concurrent strands: density, register,
masking, fusion, saturation, clearing, and residue. `Color` owns the internal
identity and transformation of an event: attack, continuation, release,
articulation, dynamics, blend, resistance, and timbral trajectory. Patrick
Shiroishi's live self-layering and DJ Shadow's construction of beats from
separately captured drum hits strengthen those distinct arguments without
requiring another structural change.

The drafts also confirm a narrow scope exception: the book may name a device or
production action when it is the condition of an audible compositional choice,
but it should move immediately to musical agency, relationship, and consequence.
`Layer` ends by exposing the internal life of one strand, leading to `Color`;
`Color` ends by asking who makes and controls that life, leading to `Release
Control`.

Drafting `Limit` and `Sustain` confirms their sequence and separation. `Limit`
owns restriction as a redistribution of consequence: inventory, behavior,
circumstance, exception, and responsibility. `Sustain` assumes that a material
has already been selected and enters its changing spectrum, tuning, renewal,
resonance, and decay. Lucier's `I Am Sitting in a Room` now belongs primarily
to `Sustain`; `Place` should make only a brief callback. Scelsi's `Quattro
pezzi su una nota sola` belongs to `Sustain` as audible evidence, while the
assistant-labor and authorship history remains reserved for `Release Control`.
The progression is now explicit: narrow the field, hear the interior of one
sound, then ask how other pitches gather around a reference to make a harmonic
world.
