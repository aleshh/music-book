# Listening paths {.unnumbered .matter}

The works below are not a canon or a required syllabus. They are a set of
doors into the book's methods. Listen first for the relation named after each
title. Then return to the relevant numbered piece if you want the longer
argument.

Recordings and performances can make substantially different decisions about
tempo, balance, duration, tuning, and space. When two versions disagree, the
disagreement is often the useful part.

**Attend**

- John Cage, *4'33"* — hear how a frame turns surroundings into events. See 4.
- Brian Eno, *Ambient 1: Music for Airports* — move between close attention and
  music that supports another activity. See 5.
- Pauline Oliveros, *Tuning Meditation* — follow the alternation between the
  whole field, a distant voice, and your own contribution. See 7.

**Experience time**

- Morton Feldman, *String Quartet II* — notice when duration ceases to feel
  like an enlarged conventional form and begins to act as scale. See 13 and 19.
- Éliane Radigue, *Trilogie de la Mort* — listen for change that remains
  undeniable even when no single crossing point can be located. See 21.
- Olivier Messiaen, “Abîme des oiseaux” from *Quartet for the End of Time* —
  compare immensities of slow time with the compressed time of birdsong. See
  22.

**Limit**

- Arvo Pärt, *Für Alina* — hear how a narrow rule gives register, spacing, and
  one exception unusual weight. See 33.
- Tom Johnson, *The Four-Note Opera* — listen for the difference between a
  small inventory and a small dramatic world. See 36.

**Sustain**

- Giacinto Scelsi, *Quattro pezzi su una nota sola* — enter the changing
  interior concealed by the phrase “one note.” See 46.
- Alvin Lucier, *I Am Sitting in a Room* — hear the room progressively select
  which parts of speech remain. See 48.

**Make a harmonic world**

- Claude Debussy, “Voiles” — listen for unequal harmonic gravity inside a
  symmetrical collection. See 58.
- Lou Harrison and William Colvig, music for Old Granddad — hear tuning as
  something built into resonating bodies rather than applied afterward. See
  63.

**Repeat**

- Terry Riley, *In C* — follow how shared material becomes form through
  performer spacing, accumulation, and restraint. See 74.
- Julius Eastman, *Gay Guerrilla* — hear repetition gather social and harmonic
  pressure rather than merely confirming sameness. See 75.
- Maurice Ravel, *Boléro* — ask how orchestration can transform a melody that
  refuses to develop conventionally. See 76.
- Gavin Bryars, *Jesus' Blood Never Failed Me Yet* — hear a repeated recording
  acquire new meanings as its harmonic surroundings accumulate. See 77.

**Drift**

- Steve Reich, *Piano Phase* — follow the resultant patterns created as one
  copy moves through successive offsets. See 87.
- Conlon Nancarrow, *Study No. 21* — listen to independent tempos cross rather
  than converge on one shared pulse. See 89.
- Steve Reich, *Music for 18 Musicians* — separate the work's audible pulse
  from the breath-length phrases laid across it. See 90.

**Layer**

- György Ligeti, *Atmosphères* — hear many lines lose individual identity
  without producing an undifferentiated block. See 102.
- Phill Niblock, *Early Winter* — listen inside a sustained mass for beating,
  register, and the motion produced among close components. See 104.
- Patrick Shiroishi, a solo saxophone performance using live self-layering —
  notice how each new layer changes the role of the player who remains onstage.
  See 97.

**Color**

- John Cage, *Sonatas and Interludes* — hear a single piano become an ensemble
  of attacks, resonances, and uneven decays. See 112.
- DJ Shadow, *Endtroducing.....* — listen for the continuity created by related
  but nonidentical sampled attacks. See 109 and 113.
- Meredith Monk, *Dolmen Music* — follow vocal color as a carrier of syntax,
  identity, and ensemble relation. See 115.

**Release control**

- John Cage, *Music of Changes* — distinguish chance used in composition from
  freedom granted in performance. See 124.
- Christian Wolff, *For 1, 2 or 3 People* — hear precision move from fixed
  pitches into cueing, response, and temporal relation. See 126.
- Julius Eastman, *Stay On It* — listen for work identity held across notation,
  rehearsal, oral transmission, and performer choice. See 120 and 123.

**Place**

- Pauline Oliveros, Stuart Dempster, and Panaiotis, *Deep Listening* — hear a
  long reverberation become an active participant rather than an effect added
  to the music. See 136.
- John Luther Adams, *Inuksuit* — move through the work and notice how location
  changes the available ensemble. See 140.
- Morton Feldman, *Rothko Chapel* — hear instrumentation and formal scale answer
  a particular room without merely depicting it. See 144.

**Discover the form**

- Gavin Bryars, *The Sinking of the Titanic* — consider a work whose identity
  permits continued revision rather than one final closed object. See 151.
- Tōru Takemitsu, *Rain Tree* — follow resonance beyond the action that produced
  it and ask where the ending resides. See 160.
- Arvo Pärt, *Tabula rasa* — listen to the written ending hand the work to
  resonance and then to the room. See 162.

Listening need not confirm the book. If a performance contradicts the account
beside it, name the audible choice that creates the disagreement. That answer
is already a compositional resource.
