# When the piece stops moving {.unnumbered .matter}

Use this as a diagnostic index or as a source of productive misfit. Find the
description nearest your difficulty and try one of the numbered interventions
beside it. If naming the difficulty has itself become a habit, choose any number
from one to thirty-six.

Test one intervention on the smallest passage that can answer it. The reference
after each group leads to the fuller experiment, where the mechanism and its
limits are explained.

**I cannot hear what matters:** 1, 2, 7, 13, 22, 25.

**There are too many materials or choices:** 7, 8, 9, 14, 28, 29.

**The repetition lies flat:** 5, 16, 17, 19, 26.

**The sound changes, but the piece does not move:** 6, 10, 12, 15, 27, 34.

**The texture is full but inert:** 2, 22, 23, 24, 27.

**The ensemble sounds polite, vague, or unrelated:** 18, 20, 21, 23, 29, 30.

**The score controls too much—or leaves necessary work unnamed:** 9, 28, 29,
30.

**The work feels too polished or generic:** 11, 13, 16, 25, 26.

**The room is fighting the piece or making no difference:** 10, 31, 32, 33.

**The ending will not arrive:** 3, 6, 15, 18, 35, 36.

**I no longer know why this must continue:** 4, 5, 12, 34, 35, 36.

**Attend**

1. Narrow attention to one sound, widen it to the whole field, then move
   deliberately between the two. Compose that change of width without naming
   it. See 11.

2. Bring one continuing layer forward without making it louder. Change a
   relation around it—register, rarity, articulation, or surrounding rests. See
   11.

3. Make a span a listener may leave and reenter. Then make one interval
   indispensable and identify exactly what creates the cost of missing it. See
   11.

**Experience time**

4. Compare two equal durations with different event densities. Record both how
   long each felt and what divisions remain in memory. See 27.

5. Give four minutes exactly nine attacks. Make one wait structurally unlike
   the others without changing the total duration. See 27.

6. Transform one clear state into another without permitting a single audible
   crossing point. Shorten the span until that uncertainty disappears. See 27.

**Limit**

7. Remove one category of possibility from an existing passage. Find the event
   that gains the most weight, then decide whether that weight earns the loss.
   See 40.

8. Keep five sound-actions and change only the law relating them. Hear when an
   inventory becomes a grammar. See 40.

9. Remove one rule from a constrained piece. Ask separately what changed for a
   listener and what changed for a performer. See 40.

**Sustain**

10. Follow one decay until you can name three internal thresholds. Place the
    same new event at each threshold and hear whether it overlaps, interrupts,
    or answers. See 53.

11. Move two sustained near-unisons through fusion, slow beating, pulse, and
    roughness. Make those rates the form. See 53.

12. Carry one sound through continuous energy, repeated renewal, and residual
    resonance. Compose the handoff among performer, action, instrument, and
    room. See 53.

**Make a harmonic world**

13. Give one pitch weight three different ways: bass and duration, recurrence
    and phrase ending, then register alone. See 67.

14. Transform a sonority without adding or removing a pitch class. Let octave
    transfer, doubling, bass, and voice-leading create departure and return.
    See 67.

15. Build a field with two plausible centers and one important common tone.
    Strengthen both homes before deciding that the ambiguity is merely
    unfinished. See 67.

**Repeat**

16. Repeat one short phrase ten times without intentional variation, then omit
    the next return while preserving its silence. Listen for the absent phrase.
    See 81.

17. Make the same cell reduce pressure and increase it without changing its
    pitches. Exchange the versions' dynamics and solve the problem again. See
    81.

18. Give an ensemble repeating modules, one shared reference, one proximity
    rule, one listening condition for advancing, and one ending. Revise the
    rules after asking which choices felt genuinely musical. See 81.

**Drift**

19. Offset two repetitions by one step and hold them there. Find the third
    rhythm generated between them and make it clearer without adding notes. See
    95.

20. Give three parts different clocks but one total span. Reserve one event for
    a heard coincidence and ask what makes the unsynchronized middle belong
    together. See 95.

21. Keep an unaccented pulse while performers phrase by comfortable breath or
    gesture. Let a gathering of releases cue the next harmonic field. See 95.

**Layer**

22. Move three distinct sustained sounds toward a composite by changing one
    relation at a time. Find where three activities become one, then recover a
    strand. See 107.

23. Keep three strands physically present while making each identity disappear
    and return through changes in the surrounding texture. See 107.

24. Preserve a short phrase and continue against it. Add no layer until you can
    name the old layer's new role; clear material when that role changes again.
    See 107.

**Color**

25. Produce one sustained pitch four ways by changing one safe physical action
    at a time. Arrange the colors until their order creates expectation. See
    118.

26. Make one rhythm first with a repeated identical attack, then with a family
    of close but nonidentical attacks. Reorder the family until its differences
    do formal work. See 118.

27. Build a three-part form from blend, separation, and a return that retains
    one edge learned during separation. Then hold dynamics fixed. See 118.

**Release control**

28. Map who decides material, onset, duration, relation, color, transition, and
    ending. Find the decisions for which no agent has both authority and
    information. See 134.

29. Notate one musical identity as fixed result, bounded choice, and relational
    behavior. Ask what survives and where performers must compose what the
    score omitted. See 134.

30. Assign one parameter to the composer, one to performer interaction, and one
    to chance. Rotate the assignments until consequence and responsibility can
    both be heard. See 134.

**Place**

31. Listen to one site for cycles, distances, masking, and activity. Make one
    sound and wait long enough to discover whether it revealed the place or
    replaced it. See 149.

32. Give one portable score an invariant and one site-determined variable.
    Perform it in three unlike places and admit if only the volume changes. See
    149.

33. Map a public work's sources, reach, quiet zones, routes, and exits. Design
    musical responses for a public louder than the work and a local sound in
    its central register. See 149.

**Discover the form**

34. Continue one state by changing material, relationships, listener
    preparation, and context in turn. Build the form from the two changes that
    matter most. See 164.

35. Give one passage five endings: fulfillment, exhaustion, dispersal,
    interruption, and handoff to the surrounding world. Revise only the final
    approach each one requires. See 164.

36. Complete the sentence “This must continue because more time allows us to
    hear…” for every section. Cut weak answers, extend the strongest one, and
    separate two kinds of ending. See 164.

The intervention has succeeded when the piece presents a more exact question,
even if the first answer is to restore what you changed.
