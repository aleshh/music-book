# Broad topics

Not all of these belong together; they're intended as a menu.

## Time

- Deep listening vs. event listening
- Musical time vs. clock time
- Repetition and perceived change
- Duration as structure
- Slowness
- Memory and forgetting
- Anticipation without climax

## Harmony

- Static harmony
- Harmonic fields instead of progressions
- Modal thinking
- Drones
- Harmonic ambiguity
- Voice-leading as the primary musical force

- Fragments instead of themes
- Melodies that wander
- Motivic evolution
- Register as melody
- Ornament as structure

## Rhythm

- Pulse without meter
- Meter without pulse
- Asynchronous layers
- Organic tempo
- Polyrhythms that never "resolve"
- Breath and human timing

## Texture

## Form

# Possible book structures

## 1. The Toolbox

Each chapter is an approach.

- Repetition
- Silence
- Duration
- Constraint
- Layering
- Harmony
- Listening

The composers appear throughout.

**Strengths:**
- Timeless
- Practical
- Easy to browse

---

## 2. The Workshop

Each chapter starts with a compositional problem.

Example:

> How do you make five minutes interesting without changing chords?

Then explores multiple solutions from different composers.

**Strengths:**
- Practical
- Problem-solving oriented
- Encourages experimentation

---

## 3. The Opposites

Each chapter explores a tension.

- Simplicity vs. Complexity
- Change vs. Stasis
- Freedom vs. Rules
- Repetition vs. Variation
- Control vs. Chance
- Sound vs. Silence
- Foreground vs. Background

**Strengths:**
- Philosophical
- Encourages nuanced thinking
- Avoids presenting "right answers"

---

## 4. The Composer's Laboratory

Each chapter is an experiment.

Examples:

- Compose with one pitch.
- Compose without rhythm.
- Compose where every note is equally important.
- Compose something that never repeats exactly.

Historical examples come afterward.

**Strengths:**
- Interactive
- Inspires actual composing
- Emphasizes discovery

---

## 5. The Landscape

Treat music as geography.

- Mountains (climax)
- Plains (stasis)
- Rivers (flow)
- Weather (texture)
- Forests (density)
- Horizons (expectation)

**Strengths:**
- Memorable metaphors
- Encourages intuitive thinking

---

## 6. The Listening Journey

Begin with perception rather than composition.

1. How we hear
2. How we notice change
3. How we lose attention
4. How music guides attention
5. How to compose for those mechanisms

**Strengths:**
- Grounded in perception
- Bridges psychology and composition

---

## 7. Conversations

Each chapter pairs two unexpected composers.

Examples:

- Cage ↔ Brahms
- Feldman ↔ Eastman
- Eno ↔ Debussy
- Reich ↔ Pärt
- Oliveros ↔ Messiaen

The ideas emerge through comparison.

**Strengths:**
- Fresh perspectives
- Avoids chronology
- Highlights shared concerns across traditions

---

## 8. A Composer's Decision Tree

Each chapter begins with a question.

Example:

> Do you want the listener to notice individual notes?

If yes...

If no...

Continue.

The entire book becomes a map of compositional choices.

**Strengths:**
- Interactive
- Practical
- Encourages intentional decision-making

---

## 9. Layers of Abstraction

Move from the microscopic to the macroscopic.

- One note
- Two notes
- A phrase
- A texture
- Time
- Form
- An entire piece

Everything builds naturally.

**Strengths:**
- Logical progression
- Suitable for beginners and experienced composers alike

---

## 10. The Verbs of Composition

Instead of organizing around nouns ("Harmony"), organize around actions.

- Repeat
- Delay
- Stretch
- Erase
- Accumulate
- Blur
- Interrupt
- Balance
- Listen
- Transform
- Suspend
- Release

This focuses on what composers *do*, regardless of style or era. Brahms, Cage, Eastman, and Eno can all appear in the chapter on **Repeat**, but for completely different reasons.

**Strengths:**
- Highly original
- Crosses stylistic boundaries naturally
- Centers compositional thinking rather than musical genres
