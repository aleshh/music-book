# How to use this book {.unnumbered .matter}

This book has two routes through it.

The first is a path. Read the Sections in order and the argument accumulates.
Attention leads to experienced time; limits give sustained sound and harmony
consequence; repetition separates into recurrence, drift, and layer; color
makes the identity of an event more exact; control and place widen the field of
agents; form gathers those changes into continuation and ending.

The second route begins with resistance. A piece is too full but still feels
empty. A repeated figure has lost its pressure. The performers have choices
but no reason to listen. An ending arrives because the allotted duration has
elapsed, not because the music has changed its terms.

When that happens, turn to **When the piece stops moving** near the back. Find
the nearest description of the difficulty and choose one of the interventions
listed beneath it. Or choose a number from one to thirty-six without looking
for the most relevant one. Try the smallest version of the action before
reading the longer experiment it references.

This use has a precedent in Brian Eno and Peter Schmidt's *Oblique Strategies*,
a deck they first published together in 1975. Eno described drawing a card when
work reached an impasse. The brief instruction did not supply the missing
music. It interrupted a narrowed way of seeing the problem and restored an
approach that pressure had made difficult to remember.[^use-os]

Piece 125, “The instruction you forgot,” considers that distinction in detail.
The interventions in this book are less cryptic than the cards, and each points
back to an explanation of the musical relation it alters. Still, they can serve
a related purpose: to place an outside question beside the work before habit
has time to answer for it.

A simple procedure is enough:

1. State what is resisting you without judging it. “The texture remains full”
   is more useful than “the piece is boring.”
2. Choose one intervention. Do not combine three remedies before hearing the
   first.
3. Change the smallest passage that can test the proposition.
4. Listen for consequence, not compliance. The prompt may reveal that the
   original version was right for a reason you had not yet named.
5. Follow the reference into the book if you need the mechanism, its limits, or
   a more complete experiment.

An apparently irrelevant intervention may be useful. If a prompt about place
meets a piece intended for headphones, it may expose the imagined space inside
the sound. If a prompt about performer responsibility meets a solo work, it may
reveal how much control has already been assigned to memory, bodily fatigue, or
an instrument's decay.

Misfit is not a command to force the analogy. Spend a moment locating the
variable the intervention would change. If none matters, discard it. An outside
voice earns its place by returning judgment to the composer, not by replacing
it.

You can also move between the two routes. Read a Section, try one experiment,
then leave the book until the work asks another question. The sequence gives
the distinctions depth; the interventions give them handles. Neither requires
you to make a piece that resembles the examples.

The aim is not to finish music by consulting a correct answer. It is to make
the next consequential choice audible.
