# Foreword {.unnumbered .matter}

In 2025 I heard Taylor Deupree's Sti.ll, and it made me rethink everything I wanted to do with my music. I'd recently purchased a cello and started learning the basics of it, the idea being that it could add a physical element to the ambient music I wanted to create, giving it a distinctive flavor. But here was something completely different. An album made with instruments including cello, vibraphone, percussion, guitar, and clarinet. All instruments I was familiar with, but combined in a way I'd never heard or imagined. Apparently it's an acoustic recreation of an electronic album, but this struck me as almost beside the point. Here was contemporary chamber music that really was something new, and I immediately imagined a a sound world and a working method. Based on improvisation and looping, but entirely acoustically originated. It seemed like the possibilities might be endless. But when I tried working with these elements, what I got ended up sounding like a cluttered mess. Working felt heavy and laborious: projects easy to start, impossible to finish. I started reading feverishly: Steve Reich _Conversations_, Morton Feldman _Give My Regards to Eighth Street_, essays by Ernst Kurth and Alex Ross. All endlessly interesting and maybe helpful, but also incredible means to procrastination. All this reading and musical experimentation happened in the age of LLMs, and my conversations with ChatGPT accompanied a lot of this, a sort of second stream of thought, a living journal.

Then I started hearing about people generating dozens, or hundreds, of AI-written books and spamming Amazon with them. How hard would it be to actually do that?

So this book happened as an experiment: what would an LLM spit out, seeded to some unknown extent by the conversations we'd had? I started with this prompt:

> I want to write a book about methodologies for creating ambient and minimalist music. No technology talk, just music theory and "approaches" defined broadly. We can draw on Brian Eno and Cage, but there are lots of composers (Julius Eastman, Feldman, Brahms, etc.) Who we could selectively incorporate as well. Can you make a list of broad topics that might or might not be included in such a book and let me pick and choose? And give me some structures that might make sense for the book (for example, each chapter focuses on a particular musician or composer as a way of discussing a particular musical idea or approach; that's kind of a cliche—are there other potentially more interesting ideas?)

Well, it took a few days, this lazy LLM wouldn't write more than two chapters at once. But I generated a PDF, pushed it to my Kindle, and was kind of shocked that this was genuinely interesting and pretty readable.

Am I just enjoying the taste of my own AI slop? Probably. One way to know for sure: put it out. My understanding is that I can put this on Amazon print-on-demand, and at the very least I'll be able to make myself a printed copy for a few bucks. Are there other people out there who would find this helpful in some way? Online promotion is a whole other thing, and I have no intention of doing much with this, so I'll probably never know.

Alesh Houdek
