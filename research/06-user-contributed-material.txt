31. PATRICK SHIROISHI MAKES A CHANGING ENSEMBLE FROM ONE SAXOPHONIST

Musical idea: Live self-layering lets a performer play with his own recent
past. Each recorded layer was once a foreground action; once it repeats, it
becomes material that the present player can support, contradict, cover, or
clear away, but that cannot listen and respond in return.

User's account: Patrick Shiroishi tours alone with a saxophone and a looper
pedal, creating unusually full performances from that small combination.

Verified account: Shiroishi's official concert archive identifies a run of
November 2024 dates, including Cafe OTO in London, as a solo European tour. He
has also described two distinct branches of his solo practice: completely
acoustic performances and performances in which he puts saxophone through
guitar pedals. In a 2025 first-person interview, he said that the electronic
branch began nearly a decade earlier with distortion and a loop pedal. He
framed the problem compositionally: when he performs alone, he has to make a
roughly thirty-minute span sustain interest while serving what he wants the
music to say. He described using extensive looping, a freeze function to make
a drone, high and low overtones around it, and distortion near the end.

Cafe OTO's description of its recording from the final night of the 2024 tour
makes the resulting form unusually concrete. Plain saxophone tones give way to
looped refrains shared between voice and instrument; new saxophone lines enter
the spaces in those refrains; a bass-register swell changes their footing;
circular breathing, melodic attacks, and fragments of recorded speech blur
together. The accumulated field eventually clears before layering resumes.
The mechanism matters because it continually changes the status of an action:
phrase becomes refrain, voice becomes texture, and a line played by one body
can acquire the density of an ensemble.

Shiroishi has used looping for quite different expressive ends. Discussing the
2020 solo recording `Descension`, which addresses his family's history and the
incarceration of Japanese Americans, he recalled that two melodies emerged
during the performance and that he looped them in a single take before moving
into a long, noisier conclusion involving his voice. This is a useful warning
against treating the loop as an aesthetic guarantee of calm or smoothness. It
can preserve a phrase long enough for the performer to oppose it, overload it,
or make it bear historical and emotional pressure.

Best book use: A principal encounter in Chapter 8, Layer. It can open the
question of how accumulation changes perceptual roles while keeping the human
source visible. The crucial asymmetry is that the present Shiroishi can hear
and answer the captured Shiroishi, while the captured layer cannot adjust to
him. A brief later callback in Release Control could use that asymmetry, but
the performance story should be told fully only once.

Verification: STRONG that Shiroishi undertook the named 2024 solo tour and has
an established solo practice using saxophone, voice, loops, freeze/drone, and
other effects. STRONG for the described Cafe OTO performance sequence, though
the wording comes from the venue's editorial description rather than a formal
score. CAUTION against implying that every Shiroishi solo set uses a loop
pedal: he explicitly distinguishes acoustic and electronic approaches. Keep
the discussion centered on the musical relationship created by live capture,
not on pedal operation. When discussing `Descension`, retain its historical
subject rather than converting it into a neutral demonstration of technique.

32. DJ SHADOW TREATS A DRUM BREAK AS A FAMILY OF NONIDENTICAL EVENTS

Musical idea: Repeated rhythmic function need not mean repeated sonic object.
Several kicks or snares drawn from one performed break carry small differences
of touch, decay, bleed, room, and neighboring sound. Alternating among them can
preserve the sense that a rhythm was made by a body in a place, even when its
new sequence is assembled one event at a time.

User's account: DJ Shadow samples roughly a dozen different but nearly
identical snare hits from a jazz recording and uses all of them in a song,
giving entirely sample-based music a rich, organic quality.

What the sources establish: In a detailed 1997 `Keyboard` interview about
`Endtroducing.....`, DJ Shadow said that he usually divided a drum break into
about nine pieces, sometimes more. Rather than repeat the entire source break,
he might isolate one bass-drum hit, the next bass-drum hit, a snare, a hi-hat,
and another snare. He could then reconstruct part of the original performance
or compose a new beat from its components. He deliberately preserved the ends
of sounds and the source recording's ambience; if necessary, he sampled the
apparently empty “air” of the record so that the joins would not become
unnaturally blank.

For “The Number Song,” he described taking every usable portion of a 45 on
which a vocalist was not speaking, producing about sixteen pieces. The singer's
reverberation still trails through some of them even though the words are
absent. That residual sound gives the reconstructed beat a particular weight
and atmosphere. Elsewhere, he described programming the beginning of “Napalm
Brain/Scatter Brain” to preserve the slight drag of the source drummer rather
than correcting it to an abstractly perfect grid.

This verified version is more musically useful than a story about collecting
nominally identical snares. The subdivisions include different members of the
drum kit and pieces of ambience, and their differences are not incidental dirt
laid over a rhythm. They are part of the rhythm's identity. A snare is not only
an onset at a point in a measure; it is an onset plus a hand's force, the drum's
decay, the room around it, and traces of what happened just before and after.
Sampling can preserve those contingencies while radically changing their
order.

Best book use: A principal or substantial supporting encounter in Chapter 9,
Color. It gives “timbre as syntax” a compact rhythmic case: events perform the
same grammatical job while remaining sonically nonidentical. Repeat may later
make a brief cross-reference to the difference between recurring function and
recurring object, but the full production account should remain in Color.

Possible exercise: Make two short versions of one rhythm. In the first, use the
same attack every time a given rhythmic role recurs. In the second, use a small
family of closely related but nonidentical attacks for that role. Keep timing
and dynamics as close as possible. Ask whether the second version sounds more
embodied, merely more random, or simply different—and identify which details
cause that judgment. The point is not to simulate a drummer but to hear how
much formal information can live inside an event usually treated as
interchangeable.

Verification: STRONG for the chopping method, the usual figure of about nine
pieces, the roughly sixteen pieces used for “The Number Song,” the preservation
of decay and record ambience, and the deliberate retention of a drummer's
drag. NOT VERIFIED as stated for “a dozen near-identical snare hits,” for a jazz
source, or for one particular song using that exact combination. These may
derive from another interview or demonstration, but the presently located
first-person sources do not substantiate them. Do not publish those details
without a new source. Also avoid claiming that sonic variation alone makes a
beat “organic”; timing, phrase shape, ambience, and the relations among sounds
all contribute.
