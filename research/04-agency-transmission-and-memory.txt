ANECDOTE BANK 04: AGENCY, TRANSMISSION, AND MEMORY
Working research notes for a book on ambient and minimalist musical practice

These stories ask who makes a work: composer, performer, audience, room,
assistant, accident, or memory. Several concern music whose identity cannot be
located in a complete conventional score.

==========================================================================
22. ROSCOE MITCHELL HOLDS A HOSTILE CROWD WITH ONE REPEATED PHRASE

Musical idea: Repetition can be confrontation, negotiation, and collective
form—not only contemplation.

At the Willisau Jazz Festival on August 28, 1976, Roscoe Mitchell substituted
for Anthony Braxton at short notice. Some members of the audience of roughly a
thousand greeted him with catcalls and demands for Braxton. Mitchell began
Nonaah with a short, angular saxophone phrase and repeated it insistently. The
crowd's noise became part of the event: each return of the phrase refused to be
dislodged, while small changes in accent, pacing, register, and extension made
the repetition an active response rather than a loop. Mitchell later explained
that the music could not move forward until the audience respected his right
to play. Over a long span, hostility gave way to concentrated listening and an
ovation. Minimal material here becomes a boundary held in public. Persistence
changes both the phrase and the people hearing it.

Verification: STRONG. The performance is recorded, and George Lewis's
recollection, Mitchell's comments, festival details, and close scholarly
analysis align. Exact estimates of elapsed time and crowd size vary, so the
account avoids stopwatch precision. The audience did not act as one body;
“some members” is more accurate than calling the entire room hostile.

23. SCELSI IMPROVISES; ASSISTANTS TURN THE SOUND INTO SCORES

Musical idea: Composition may pass through listening, performance, memory, and
collaboration before it reaches notation.

From the 1950s onward, Giacinto Scelsi often developed music by improvising,
recording the results, and having assistants transcribe and orchestrate them.
He then revised or approved the scores. This working method helps explain why
his mature pieces can feel organized around the life of a sound rather than a
sequence of symbols: pitch bends, beating, register, dynamics, and changing
overtone color are primary events. It also complicates the romantic picture of
the composer alone. Transcription was interpretive. Assistants brought ears,
instrumental knowledge, and notational choices to material that no page could
capture automatically. The book could use Scelsi to ask a generative question:
what if a score is a negotiated memory of a sounding event rather than its
origin?

Verification: STRONG for the recorded-improvisation and assistant-transcription
workflow, supported by archival and scholarly research. CAUTION on authorship.
Accounts differ about how much particular assistants contributed and whether
Scelsi's public mystique obscured them. The fair formulation neither dismisses
Scelsi's compositional judgment nor erases collaborative labor. The popular
story that a mental crisis led him simply to play one piano note for hours is
too unstable to serve as the evidence for his method.

Sources:

- Cambridge University Press, “Orality and Rhetoric in Scelsi's Music”

24. FELDMAN MEETS CAGE IN THE LOBBY AFTER WEBERN

Musical idea: A shared way of listening can found a community before it founds
an aesthetic program.

In early 1950, Morton Feldman attended a New York Philharmonic concert at
Carnegie Hall that included Webern's Symphony, Op. 21, conducted by Dimitri
Mitropoulos. At intermission he went to the lobby, where John Cage approached
and asked what he had thought of Webern. The encounter began a close friendship
and an association with the circle later called the New York School. The small
scene matters because Webern's compressed scale and exquisitely exposed sounds
acted almost like a password: before Cage and Feldman agreed on procedures,
they recognized a common intensity of listening. A creative movement can begin
not with a manifesto but with two people noticing that the same unlikely music
has altered the proportions of everything around it.

Verification: STRONG for the Carnegie Hall lobby meeting, the Webern work, and
the ensuing friendship, based on Feldman's recollection and archival scholarship.
CAUTION on a popular embellished version in which both men independently flee
the hall because nothing after Webern can be heard. Feldman's documented account
places their conversation at intermission and does not require that neat image.

Sources:
- University at Buffalo Music Library, Morton Feldman archival summary
  https://library2.buffalo.edu/music/collections/pdf/ubmu-pdf-feldman2006summary.pdf
- Modernism/modernity Print Plus, scholarly account of Cage and Feldman
  https://modernismmodernity.org/node/1398

25. STAY ON IT SURVIVES AS INSTRUCTIONS, RECORDINGS, AND PEOPLE

Musical idea: A work can retain identity through a network of partial memories
rather than a definitive page.

Julius Eastman's Stay On It, first developed in the 1970s, combines a bright
repeating figure with conducted cues, modules, improvisation, and moments of
collective release. No complete conventional score survives. Musicians who knew
the work have described sparse written materials—closer to a lead sheet or
page of cues—with crucial information communicated orally in rehearsal.
Contemporary performers reconstruct it from surviving notation, recordings,
scholarship, and the memories of people connected to Eastman's practice. This
does not make every realization arbitrary. The repeated figure, sectional
behaviors, and social energy remain powerful identifiers. Stay On It can
therefore illuminate a living model of form: exact in its commitments, flexible
in its surface, and distributed across a community of transmission.

Verification: STRONG for the incomplete surviving notation and the importance
of verbal/rehearsal transmission, supported by score archives, performer
accounts, publisher material, and scholarship. CAUTION when assigning a single
“correct” historical version. Eastman changed pieces in performance, and modern
editions necessarily make editorial decisions. Those decisions should be named
rather than disguised as recovery of a lost final text.

Sources:
- Mary Jane Leach, Julius Eastman scores and archival information
  https://www.mjleach.com/EastmanScores.htm
- WRTI/NPR, account of Wild Up reconstructing Stay On It
  https://www.wrti.org/latest-classical-from-npr-music/latest-classical-from-npr-music/2022-05-18/wild-up-stay-on-it
- Wise Music Classical, Eastman work and performance background

26. ALVIN LUCIER LETS A ROOM SMOOTH HIS SPEECH

Musical idea: A process can be simple enough to hear while producing a result
too complex to predict.

In I Am Sitting in a Room (1969), Alvin Lucier speaks a short text in a room,
then repeatedly returns the room's response to itself. With each generation,
the speech becomes less intelligible and the resonant frequencies of the space
become more prominent, until cadence and vowel traces dissolve into a shimmering
chord. Lucier's text openly announces the process and adds a personal purpose:
he wants to smooth out irregularities in his speech. He stuttered, and the work
neither hides the stutter nor turns it into confession. The room gradually
translates it into resonance. This creates an unusually transparent drama: the
listener knows what will happen in principle but cannot know the exact route or
sound, because every voice and room supplies different material.

Verification: STRONG. The 1969 work, Lucier's score-text, his first-person
accounts, and Museum of Modern Art documentation agree on the procedure and his
reference to speech irregularity. A technical account can distract from the
musical idea; for this book, one sentence about the method is enough, followed
by attention to the audible transformation, pacing, and ethics of self-exposure.

Sources:
- Museum of Modern Art, “Collecting Alvin Lucier's I Am Sitting in a Room”
  https://www.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2015/01/20/collecting-alvin-luciers-i-am-sitting-in-a-room/
- IRCAM, Alvin Lucier work chronology
  https://brahms.ircam.fr/en/composer/alvin-lucier/workcourse
- MIT Music and Theater Arts, interview with Alvin Lucier

27. GAVIN BRYARS RETURNS TO FIND AN OFFICE SILENCED BY A SINGER

Musical idea: Repetition can reveal the dignity and completeness of a fragment
without “improving” it.

In 1971, filmmaker Alan Power gave Gavin Bryars unused recordings from a film
about people living rough in London. One unnamed man sang a brief religious
song, “Jesus' Blood Never Failed Me Yet,” in tune but with an irregular pulse.
Bryars made a repeating cycle of the approximately thirteen-bar song while
working at Leicester. He has recalled leaving the adjoining art studio for
coffee and returning to find the normally lively room quiet; some people were
weeping as the unaccompanied voice repeated. Bryars then built a slowly growing
accompaniment that follows rather than corrects the singer. The fragment's
uneven timing remains sovereign. The composer changes the surrounding light,
not the person at the center.

Verification: STRONG for the recording's origin, Bryars's receipt of it, the
loop, and the gradually evolving composition. Bryars never met the singer, a
fact that corrects many retellings. CAUTION: the art-studio reaction is Bryars's
own vivid recollection and lacks a known independent eyewitness account. It is
fair to tell it explicitly as his memory, not as externally corroborated fact.

Sources:
- Gavin Bryars interview, Now Then Magazine
  https://nowthenmagazine.com/articles/gavin-bryars-never-failed-me-yet
- The Guardian, correction concerning the unidentified singer
  https://www.theguardian.com/music/2018/jun/20/homeless-mystery-jesus-blood-never-failed-me-yet-gavin-bryars
- Gavin Bryars, official note on the work's flexible orchestration

28. LOU HARRISON AND WILLIAM COLVIG BUILD “OLD GRANDDAD”

Musical idea: Tuning and timbre can grow together from the materials at hand.

In 1971, Lou Harrison and his partner William Colvig began building an ensemble
they called an American gamelan and later “Old Granddad.” Its sounding bodies
included aluminum slabs, conduit, oxygen tanks, and resonators made from cans
and other readily available material. Colvig's craft and Harrison's tuning
ideas were equally fundamental: these were not neutral found objects but parts
shaped into a coherent, justly tuned musical world. The ensemble let Harrison
compose harmony, resonance, and instrumental construction as one activity.
For a methodology book, the point is not picturesque thrift. It is that a
tuning system becomes tangible when each interval has a weight, decay, and
physical source, and that making an ensemble can itself be composition.

Verification: STRONG for the 1971 collaboration, nickname, materials, and
tuning, documented by archival programs and Harrison interviews. CULTURAL
CAUTION: Harrison deliberately called it an American gamelan, distinguishing
it from Indonesian instruments, and later studied Javanese practice more
deeply. The ensemble should be discussed as a cross-cultural invention with a
specific history, not as an inexpensive equivalent to “authentic” gamelan or
as evidence that Indonesian traditions are merely a collection of timbres.
